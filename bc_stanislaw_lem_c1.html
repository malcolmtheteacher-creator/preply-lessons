MINDS WORTH MEETING · C1 · 50 min

Stanisław Lem

The Polish writer who asked the hardest question in science fiction: what if we met a mind we could never understand?

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Look at his name and the title of this lesson: “a mind we could never understand”. What kind of writer do you imagine he was? What might that phrase mean? And have you read anything by him — what pulled you in, or kept you away? Just talk.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

Three short facts. After each one, you do the talking.

Lem was born in 1921 in Lwów (then Poland, now Lviv in Ukraine). As a young man of Jewish descent, he survived the Nazi occupation on false papers — watching a supposedly civilised society organise itself around death.

Over to you:
  • How might living through that change the kind of stories a person wants to tell? What would you stop trusting?

He trained as a doctor, then became a writer of astonishing range: bleak philosophical novels, laugh-out-loud robot fables, fake reviews of books that never existed, and serious science writing that predicted real technology. He is the most translated Polish author in history.

Over to you:
  • A scientist who writes comedy and philosophy and fake reviews — what does that range tell you about his mind? Do you trust “one-subject” people or “many-subject” people more, and why?

He thought most science fiction was shallow — “rockets and ray-guns dressed up as ideas.” He wanted the opposite: stories that take real science seriously and ask hard questions about the limits of the human mind.

Over to you:
  • Do you agree that a lot of science fiction is just “ray-guns dressed up as ideas”? Where is the line between entertainment and a genuine idea?

Solaris — and you do the thinking out loud

His most famous novel, Solaris (1961). I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

A distant planet, Solaris, is almost entirely covered by one vast ocean. After decades of study, scientists become convinced the ocean is not just alive but intelligent — perhaps a single enormous mind. Yet after generations of trying, humanity has learned essentially nothing from it. The ocean simply does not answer.

Put yourself there

You are a scientist on that station. How would you even try to talk to an ocean-sized mind? And how would it feel to give your whole career to something that never once replies?

Then the ocean acts. It reaches into the scientists' minds and, from their most painful memories, builds living “visitors”. For the psychologist Kelvin, it recreates his dead wife — perfectly, again and again. She does not know she is a copy. Every time he sends her away, she comes back.

Over to you:
  • Is the ocean being kind, cruel, curious — or is it just a reflex, like a knee twitching? How could the scientists ever tell the difference?
  • If an alien mind rebuilt someone from your memories to reach you, who might it choose — and what would that reveal about you?

Here is Lem's sharpest point. Faced with the truly alien, the humans stop studying the ocean and start drowning in themselves — their guilt, their memories, their own reflection. His verdict on our hunger for “first contact”: we do not really want to meet the Other at all. We want a mirror.

We do not want other worlds. We want mirrors — we go looking for the stars, and what we are really hunting for is ourselves.

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

“We don't want the alien; we want a mirror.” Argue for it or against it — and bring real examples: in science, in travel, in how people treat anything foreign or unfamiliar. Build your case, then I will push back and you defend it.

Vocabulary — click each card to reveal

Ten words you need to discuss Lem properly — and that work just as well for any serious conversation about ideas. Tap to reveal the meaning and an example.

sentient
adjective
Able to perceive, feel or be conscious — not just to function.
"The ocean on Solaris may be sentient, but we cannot prove it thinks the way we do."
to grapple with
phrasal verb
To struggle hard to understand or deal with something difficult.
"His novels grapple with questions most writers avoid."
futile / futility
adjective / noun
Pointless; certain to fail however hard you try.
"He suggests that contact with the truly alien may be futile — a beautiful futility."
hubris
noun
Dangerous over-confidence; pride that blinds you.
"Believing we could decode an alien mind was sheer hubris."
to project (onto)
verb / projection (n)
To attribute your own feelings or ideas to something outside you.
"We project human motives onto the universe because we cannot imagine anything else."
to anthropomorphise
verb
To give human characteristics to something that is not human.
"We anthropomorphise the ocean by asking what it 'wants'."
profound
adjective
Very deep; reaching far beyond the surface of a subject.
"There is something profound in the idea that we cannot escape ourselves."
bleak
adjective
Cold, grim, offering little comfort or hope.
"His vision is bleak, yet strangely calming."
conceivable
adjective
Possible to imagine or believe might be true.
"It is perfectly conceivable that intelligence elsewhere shares nothing with ours."
a thought experiment
noun phrase
An imagined scenario used to test an idea you cannot test in reality.
"Solaris is really one long thought experiment about the limits of knowing."

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that intelligence could exist without any sense of time.
  • It might well be that we are simply incapable of recognising it.
  • I suspect the truth is somewhere in between.
  • You could argue that the ocean is not even trying to communicate.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say contact is impossible by definition.
  • What strikes me most is how little the humans actually learn.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, the story is more relevant now than in 1961.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure I fully buy that.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that assume…?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…
Put them to work · 3 minutes

I'll make a bold claim — “Lem proves that real contact with an alien mind is impossible.” Answer me using at least one phrase from each box above: first speculate, then take a position, then complicate it. Keep going until you've used all three.

Three thought experiments — you do the talking

A · The Solaris Test (3–4 min)

Imagine first contact happens tomorrow — but the intelligence is genuinely alien: no body, no language, no concept of "you" and "me", possibly no sense of time. It does something, and we cannot tell whether it is a greeting, a question, or an accident.

Argue one side: could we ever truly communicate with it, or is Lem right that we would only ever see ourselves reflected back? Build a real case — give an example, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · Wisdom vs. Power (3–4 min)

Lem believed technology races ahead while human wisdom limps behind — and that this gap is where the danger lives. He sketched AI and virtual worlds in the 1960s and was uneasy about them.

Take today's AI, social media, or genetic engineering. Has our wisdom kept pace with our power, or has Lem's warning come true? Pick one technology and make the case with a concrete example from the real world.

C · Build Your Own (4–5 min)

The constructors Trurl and Klapaucius build machines that go gloriously wrong. Your turn: invent a Lem-style thought experiment. Describe a single machine or scenario — "a machine that…" — that looks like a solution but exposes something uncomfortable about being human.

Set it up, walk me through what goes wrong, and land the idea underneath it in one sentence. I will then poke holes in it, and you defend it.

Your take — the conversation that matters most

You introduced me to Lem, so you get the last word. Pick the questions that grab you — there are no model answers here.

Open discussion

  1. After today, has anything shifted in how you see Lem — or has it confirmed why you like him?
  2. Lem's great theme is the limit of what we can know. Is that a comforting idea to you, or a frightening one?
  3. He distrusted grand systems and easy optimism. Do you share that instinct, or do you think he was too bleak?
  4. Which feels more alien to you — an intelligent ocean, or the artificial intelligence we are actually building? Why?
  5. If you could ask Lem one question, what would it be? And what do you think he would say back?
  6. Who is the next writer or thinker you would put in front of me — and what is the one idea of theirs I need to understand?
