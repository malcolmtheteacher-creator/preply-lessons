Pompeii

The Roman city that vanished in a day — and was found again seventeen centuries later.

Today: you'll reconstruct the last day of a Roman city — putting the events in order and telling the story aloud — using narrative tenses and the language of a natural disaster.

Key words — meet them, then sequence them

Before we read, let's unlock the ten words you'll need. Read each sentence aloud, decide what the bold word means, then reveal the gloss and check yourself. Keep them in mind — in a moment you'll put them in the order events actually happened.

1. Before the disaster, Pompeii was a prosperous town — rich, busy and growing, with fine houses, shops and public baths.
prosperous (adj) — successful and wealthy; thriving. (noun: prosperity)
2. The mountain loomed over the bay — a huge, silent shape on the skyline that the townspeople saw every single day without fear.
to loom (over) (v) — to appear as a large, often threatening shape, especially close above something.
3. The eruption began with a deep crack, and then the mountain tore open and threw its contents violently into the sky.
an eruption (n) — the sudden, violent release of material from a volcano. (verb: to erupt)
4. A huge column of smoke and ash rose straight upward for kilometres, spreading at the top like the branches of a great pine tree.
a column / plume (n) — a tall, rising body of smoke, ash or gas. (Pliny compared it to a pine tree.)
5. Then it began to fall: fine grey ash and light, porous pumice stone, raining down hour after hour onto the rooftops.
ash (n) — the fine powder thrown out by a fire or volcano. pumice (n) — light, full-of-holes volcanic stone.
6. As the danger grew, thousands tried to flee — grabbing what they could carry and running for the harbour and the open road.
to flee (v, irregular: fled / fled) — to run away from danger.
7. Those caught in the gas could not breathe; the burning, poisonous air suffocated them where they stood.
to suffocate (v) — to die, or to kill, from a lack of breathable air. (noun: suffocation)
8. By the end, the falling material had completely engulfed the city, sealing it under metres of ash and stone.
to engulf / to bury (v) — to cover something completely, so that it disappears from sight.
9. Seventeen centuries later, careful excavation began — workers digging down through the ash to uncover the streets and houses below.
an excavation (n) — the careful digging up of buried remains. (verb: to excavate)
10. To recover the shapes of those who had died, archaeologists poured liquid plaster into the hollows in the ash, making a plaster cast of each empty space.
a plaster cast (n) — a solid shape made by pouring plaster into a hollow mould and letting it set.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A city in the shadow of a mountain

In the first century AD, Pompeii was a prosperous Roman town near the Bay of Naples — a place of paved streets, painted houses, busy markets and crowded baths. Together with its smaller neighbour, Herculaneum, it was home to more than twenty thousand people, going about their ordinary lives in the warm light of an Italian summer.

Above them all loomed Mount Vesuvius, a great green shape on the horizon. Its slopes were rich and fertile, planted with vines, and the townspeople had looked up at it every day of their lives. What none of them knew — what they had no way of knowing — was that the quiet mountain watching over their prosperity was a volcano, and that it had simply been sleeping.

Then, one day in the year 79 AD — traditionally remembered as the 24th of August, though some evidence now points to later in the autumn — it woke. With a force that no one in Pompeii had words for, Vesuvius erupted.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of place was Pompeii before the disaster, and how many people lived in the area?

2. What crucial fact did the townspeople not know about Vesuvius?

1. A prosperous Roman town near the Bay of Naples; together with nearby Herculaneum, the area was home to more than 20,000 people.

2. They did not know that Vesuvius — the mountain they saw every day — was a volcano; it had been dormant, or "sleeping."

Discuss:
  • The people of Pompeii lived beside a deadly danger they couldn't even recognise. Where might we be living beside risks we don't yet see for what they are?

Part 2 — Eighteen hours of falling sky

The first phase was the most spectacular and, at first, the least deadly. Vesuvius threw an enormous column of ash and pumice some fifteen to thirty kilometres straight into the sky — so high that it spread out at the top like an immense pine tree. From across the bay, a young man named Pliny the Younger watched it from the town of Misenum and would later describe exactly that shape. This phase lasted around eighteen hours, and all the while ash and small stones rained down on Pompeii, piling deeper and deeper until the city lay buried under more than three metres of debris. Roofs groaned and collapsed under the weight.

Faced with this, the townspeople made the choices people always make in disasters. Many fled at once, pouring out towards the harbour and the roads with whatever they could carry. Others sheltered indoors, covered their heads, and waited for it to pass — reasoning, perhaps, that the falling stone could not last forever. Some were simply trapped. And one famous man chose to sail towards the danger rather than away from it: Pliny the Elder, a celebrated naturalist and the commander of the Roman fleet, crossed the bay both to rescue people and to observe the eruption with his own scientist's eyes. He went ashore — and never left.

By nightfall the city had vanished beneath the ash. But for those who had stayed and survived the first day, the worst was still to come.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the first phase of the eruption look like, and roughly how long did it last?

2. What different choices did the townspeople make — and what did Pliny the Elder decide to do?

1. A vast column of ash and pumice rose 15–30 km into the sky (Pliny the Younger compared it to a pine tree); this phase lasted about 18 hours, burying Pompeii under more than 3 metres of ash and stone.

2. Many fled towards the harbour and roads; others sheltered indoors and waited. Pliny the Elder, commander of the fleet, sailed towards the eruption to help and to observe — went ashore, and died.

Discuss:
  • Some ran, some stayed and waited. With the information they had at the time, which decision would you have made — and is it easy to judge them now, knowing what we know?

Part 3 — The second day, and the long silence after

On the second day, the eruption changed its character completely. Now Vesuvius sent down pyroclastic surges — fast, ground-hugging clouds of superheated gas and ash that swept down the mountainside far quicker than anyone could run. For those who had stayed behind, sheltering and hoping, there was no escaping them. The searing, airless cloud reached the city and suffocated those still within it. Pliny the Elder, who had gone ashore the day before, was among the many overcome by the fumes.

His nephew, Pliny the Younger, safe across the bay, had watched the whole catastrophe unfold. Years afterwards he set down what he had seen in two letters to the historian Tacitus — and those letters survive as the only eyewitness account of the disaster that we possess. In all, somewhere between thirteen and sixteen thousand people are thought to have died at Pompeii; the remains of more than fifteen hundred of them have since been found. The ash that killed the city also, strangely, preserved it: sealed beneath the debris, Pompeii lay buried and largely forgotten for around seventeen hundred years.

Then, in 1748, excavations began, and a lost world slowly emerged from the ash — streets, shops, paintings, a whole Roman town caught at a single instant. In the 1860s an archaeologist named Giuseppe Fiorelli realised that the bodies had long decayed, but had left perfect hollows in the hardened ash. By gently pouring liquid plaster into these spaces, he created casts — quiet, human shapes that record the final moments of people who had died there seventeen centuries before. Today Pompeii is a UNESCO World Heritage Site: a whole city, and the memory of an ordinary day, preserved for the world to walk through.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What killed the people who had stayed in the city on the second day, and how do we have an eyewitness account at all?

2. What happened to Pompeii after the eruption, and what did Giuseppe Fiorelli do in the 1860s?

1. Pyroclastic surges — clouds of superheated gas and ash — suffocated those who had stayed. We have an account because Pliny the Younger watched from across the bay and later wrote two letters to the historian Tacitus, the only surviving eyewitness record.

2. The city lay buried and forgotten for about 1,700 years until excavations began in 1748. In the 1860s Fiorelli poured plaster into the hollows left by the bodies, creating the famous casts. Pompeii is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Discuss:
  • The very ash that destroyed Pompeii is also what preserved it for us. What does it mean that we can now walk through a city frozen at the worst moment of its life — and how should that place be treated?

The grammar this story runs on: narrative tenses

A story like this needs three past tenses working together. Get them right and your storytelling sounds effortless; get them wrong and the timeline collapses.

The three tools:
• Past simple — the main events, in sequence: "Vesuvius erupted," "many people fled."
• Past continuous — the background, the longer action in progress: "while the ash was falling…"
• Past perfect — a step further back, something that happened before another past event: "those who had stayed were killed the next day."

1. Past simple vs past continuous — event against background

Use the past continuous for the longer action already in progress, and the past simple for the shorter event that interrupts or sits inside it.

1. While the townspeople (go) about their day, the mountain suddenly (erupt).

2. Ash (still / fall) when many families (decide) to flee for the harbour.

3. As Pliny the Younger (watch) from across the bay, the column (rise) higher and higher.

1. were going / erupted (background in progress / the sudden event)

2. was still falling / decided (longer action interrupted by a decision)

3. was watching / rose (or: was rising — both fit the ongoing scene)

2. Past perfect — the step further back

When you are already telling the past, and you reach back to something earlier still, use had + past participle.

1. On the second day, those who (stay) behind were suffocated by the gas.

2. Pliny the Elder died on the shore, where he (go) the day before to help.

3. By the time excavations began, the city (lie) buried for about 1,700 years.

4. Fiorelli found hollows that the bodies (leave) in the hardened ash.

1. had stayed

2. had gone

3. had lain

4. had left

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the tense the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

The townspeople (live) peacefully beneath the mountain when, in 79 AD, Vesuvius (erupt). For eighteen hours, while ash (rain) down, the people (try) to escape. By the second day, those who (not / flee) (be) trapped, and a cloud of burning gas (engulf) the city.

were living (background) · erupted (event) · was raining (background) · tried / were trying (the events / the ongoing effort) · had not fled (earlier action) · were (event) · engulfed (the final event)

Notice how the three tenses layer the time: the long background, the sudden events, and the things that had already happened before.
