Ada Lovelace

She looked at a machine that could only do sums and saw something amazing: that one day it might make music and pictures too.

Before we read

Today we meet a woman who lived nearly 200 years ago, but who imagined the modern world of computers. Let's start with you.

Talk to me first:
  • How many machines with a computer inside do you use every day?
  • What is your favourite thing to do on a phone or computer?
  • Do you think a machine can ever be creative, like an artist?

Her story

Ada Lovelace was born in England in 1815. Her full name was Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace. Her father was the famous poet Lord Byron, but he left the family when Ada was only a baby. She never really knew him.

Her mother was worried that Ada might become a dreamer like her father. So she made sure Ada studied a lot of mathematics. This was unusual for a girl at that time. Ada loved numbers, but she also loved to imagine. She put the two things together in her own way.

When she was a young woman, Ada met a clever inventor called Charles Babbage. He had an idea for a big machine called the “Analytical Engine”. It was a kind of mechanical computer. It was never fully built, but Ada understood it deeply — perhaps even better than Babbage himself.

Sadly, Ada died young, of cancer, when she was only 36. But her ideas were far ahead of her time. She called her special way of thinking “poetical science”: she used both numbers and imagination together.

She liked to call her way of thinking “poetical science” — a mix of cold mathematics and warm imagination.

Over to you:
  • Ada was good at maths and imagination. Are you more of a “numbers” person or an “ideas” person?
  • Her mother chose what she should study. Who chose what you studied when you were young?
  • Does it surprise you that a woman did this kind of work 200 years ago? Why?

The big idea: imagination

In 1843, Ada translated an article about Babbage's machine from French into English. But she did much more than translate. She added long notes of her own — and her notes were longer than the original article!

In one note, she wrote a step-by-step method for the machine to follow. Many people call this the first computer program. Some historians are not sure, and they still discuss it today. But everyone agrees that Ada saw further than anyone else.

Here is her truly big idea. Everyone else looked at the machine and saw a fast calculator — something for numbers. Ada saw something more. She understood that the machine could work with any symbols, not only numbers.

So, she imagined, one day a machine like this might make music. It might draw pictures. It might do far more than just count. She imagined the general-purpose computer about a hundred years before it was built. That is the power of imagination.

She looked at a machine that could only count and dared to imagine that one day it might create.

Grammar focus — “used to” and “would” for the past

Ada's story is about the past: how things used to be, and what people used to think. We can use used to and would to talk about past habits and old situations that are not true now.

1. “used to” — past habits and old situations

We use used to + verb for something that was true in the past but is not true now. It works for habits and for situations (states).

Positive:   subject + used to + verb
Negative:   subject + didn't use to + verb
Question:   Did + subject + use to + verb?

People used to think a machine could only do sums. (They don't think that now.)

Ada used to study mathematics for hours. (a past habit)

Girls didn't use to learn much science. (a past situation, now changed)

2. “would” — for repeated past actions

We can also use would + verb for things that happened again and again in the past (repeated actions). But be careful: we do not use would for states (like be, have, know, like). For states, use only used to.

Use it for…Example
Repeated past actions — used to or wouldAda would write long notes about the machine. = Ada used to write…
Past states — used to only (not “would”)She used to be curious. (NOT “would be”)

Practice A — complete with “used to” (positive or negative)

1. Long ago, people ____ (think) machines could never be creative.

2. Ada ____ (study) maths for many hours when she was young.

3. Most girls ____ (not / learn) science in those days.

4. In Ada's time, women ____ (not / go) to university easily.

1. used to think

2. used to study

3. didn't use to learn

4. didn't use to go

Practice B — “used to” or “would”? Choose what is possible

1. As a child, Ada ____ (spend) hours dreaming and drawing. (action — both possible)

2. Her father ____ (be) a famous poet. (state — one answer only)

3. Babbage ____ (talk) to Ada about his machine for hours. (action — both possible)

4. She ____ (have) a strong imagination. (state — one answer only)

1. used to spend / would spend (both okay — an action)

2. used to be (state — “would be” is wrong here)

3. used to talk / would talk (both okay — an action)

4. used to have (state — “would have” is wrong here)
