Putting Events in Order

For stories and routines — what happened first, what happened next.

Spot the pattern

Three moments from someone's day:

  • "When the taxi arrived, I was still in the shower."
  • "I locked the door before I left for the airport."
  • "While I was cooking dinner, my sister called."

Before you check: which sentence has two things happening AT THE SAME TIME, not one after another?

Check the pattern CLICK
The while sentence: cooking dinner was already happening (was cooking) when the phone call interrupted it. When and before show one event, then another — while shows something already in progress.
when / before / after + subject + past simple, + subject + past simple
while + subject + was/were + verb-ing, + subject + past simple

Same two events, different order of the words

"First I finished work. Then I went for a coffee."
→
"I went for a coffee after I finished work."

You can also say it the other way round: "Before I went for a coffee, I finished work." Same order of events, different starting point.

Talking About the Future

For plans and promises — but there's a trap most learners fall into.

Spot the pattern

Three promises about later:

  • "I'll call you when I arrive."
  • "We'll leave as soon as the taxi gets here."
  • "I'll make dinner before you get back."

Look closely: after when/as soon as/before, is the verb "will arrive", "will get" or something simpler? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
arrive, gets, get — plain present simple, never "will arrive". Even though the whole sentence is about the future, after a time word like when/before/after/as soon as, English uses the present. Only the first part ("I'll call", "we'll leave") shows the future.
will + verb ... when / before / after / as soon as + present simple (NOT will)
This is one of the easiest mistakes to make — even confident learners write "when I will arrive" from habit. After a time word, the future hides inside the present.

Your turn

1. Text a friend: promise to message the moment you land.

"I'll message you as soon as I ."

2. Plan: dinner once the match is over.

"We'll have dinner after the match ."

3. Fix the mistake: "I'll phone you when I will get home."

SPEAK

Make three promises about later today or this week, starting with "I'll...". Use when, before, after or as soon as in each one — no "will" after the time word.

But Still... (Although / Though)

For surprising contrasts — things that happened anyway.

Spot the pattern

  • "Although it was raining, we walked to the market."
  • "We enjoyed the trip, though the hotel was noisy."
  • "Although I was tired, I finished the report."

None of these sentences also use the word "but". Why not, do you think?

Check the pattern CLICK
Because although and though already do the job of "but" — they join two surprising, contrasting ideas in ONE sentence. Adding "but" as well is a doubling mistake: choose one, never both.
although / though + subject + verb, + main clause
main clause + although / though + subject + verb
Common mistake: "Although it was raining, but we walked." Although already means "but" — you never need both in the same sentence.

Your turn

1. Combine using although: "The coffee was expensive. We bought two cups."

2. Fix the doubling mistake: "Although the shop was closed, but we managed to buy some bread."

3. Combine using though: "The queue was long. We waited patiently."

SPEAK

Think of a time something went wrong but you still had a good time. Say one sentence with although and one with though.
