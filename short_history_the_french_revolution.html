The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A kingdom that could not pay its bills

By the 1780s, France was the most populous and, on paper, the richest kingdom in Europe — and yet it was quietly falling apart. For centuries its kings had ruled with absolute power, answerable to no parliament and bound by no vote. Society was frozen into three estates: the clergy and the nobility, who owned much of the land and paid almost no tax, and everyone else — some twenty-seven million people, from lawyers to labourers — who carried the entire weight of taxation on their backs.

The state itself was crushed by debt. France had borrowed vast sums to fight its wars, and because the crown could not tax the rich, the interest payments alone were swallowing the budget. Then the harvests failed. A brutal winter and poor crops sent the price of bread — the very thing the poor lived on — beyond the reach of ordinary families. As a result, hunger and anger spread through the towns and the countryside at the same time.

Desperate for money, King Louis XVI did something no French king had done for 175 years: he called a great meeting of all three estates, the Estates-General, hoping they would agree to new taxes. But since the Third Estate — the common people — vastly outnumbered the other two yet could always be outvoted by them, the meeting quickly turned into a fight not about tax, but about power itself. In June 1789 the Third Estate broke away, declared itself a National Assembly, and swore not to disband until France had a written constitution.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the French state so deep in debt, and who carried the burden of taxation?

2. Why did Louis XVI call the Estates-General, and what did the Third Estate do instead?

1. The crown had borrowed heavily to fund wars and could not tax the wealthy clergy and nobility, so the poor and middle classes bore almost all the taxation.

2. He called it hoping the estates would approve new taxes; instead the Third Estate broke away, declared a National Assembly, and demanded a written constitution.

Discuss:
  • A system where the richest paid the least tax lasted for centuries before it broke. Why do you think such an obvious unfairness can survive so long — and what finally makes it collapse?

Part 2 — The storming of the Bastille

The king's response was to move troops towards Paris. To the people of the city, that could mean only one thing: the National Assembly was about to be crushed by force. Rumour and fear raced through the streets. Bread was still scarce, work was disappearing, and the crowds grew larger and more restless by the day. On 14 July 1789, thousands of Parisians went in search of weapons and gunpowder to defend themselves — and their search led them to the Bastille.

The Bastille was an old royal fortress in the east of Paris, a squat mass of stone towers that had long served as a state prison. By 1789 it held only a handful of prisoners, but that hardly mattered. In the public mind it stood for everything hated about the old order: a place where the king could lock away anyone he wished, without trial, on his word alone. Because the crowd knew that barrels of gunpowder were stored inside, they gathered before its walls and demanded that the garrison hand them over.

Negotiations dragged on through the morning and then broke down. Fighting erupted; the fortress was defended, but the attackers were many and determined, and after hours of struggle the garrison surrendered. There was bloodshed on both sides, and it was not a gentle victory. But when the crowd poured through the gates, the meaning was unmistakable. The king's great fortress had fallen to the people of Paris. As a result, the Bastille — soon torn down stone by stone — became the symbol of the whole revolution, and 14 July remains France's national day to this day.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the people of Paris go to the Bastille on 14 July 1789?

2. Why did the fall of the Bastille matter so much, given that it held only a few prisoners?

1. Fearing the king would use troops to crush the Assembly, they were searching for weapons and gunpowder, which were stored inside the Bastille.

2. It was a symbol of the king's absolute power to imprison people without trial; its fall showed that the people could defeat the old order, so it became the emblem of the revolution.

Discuss:
  • The Bastille was almost empty, yet taking it changed history. Why can a symbol sometimes matter more than the thing it stands for?

Part 3 — The end of the monarchy

Change now came at astonishing speed. Within weeks the Assembly swept away the ancient privileges of the nobles and issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man, which announced that all men were born free and equal before the law. For a time it seemed France might become a monarchy limited by a constitution, with the king reigning but no longer ruling. But trust between the king and the revolution had been badly damaged, and in 1791 it collapsed entirely: Louis XVI tried to flee the country in secret. He was recognised, stopped and brought back to Paris, and from that moment many no longer believed a king could be trusted at all.

War with France's neighbours made everything more extreme. Foreign armies threatened to restore the king's full power, and as a result suspicion, fear and radicalism deepened inside the country. In 1792 the monarchy was abolished and France was declared a republic. Louis XVI was put on trial as an ordinary citizen, found guilty of betraying the nation, and in January 1793 he was executed. A thousand years of French monarchy had ended.

What followed was darker still. Fear of enemies within and without gave rise to the Terror, a period when the new government executed thousands of people it judged to be enemies of the revolution — nobles, priests, and eventually revolutionaries themselves. The idealism of 1789 had curdled into bloodshed. Yet the ideas the revolution had set loose — that power comes from the people, that all are equal before the law, that no ruler stands above the nation — could not be buried with its victims. They spread across Europe and beyond, and they echo through our politics still.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Louis XVI do in 1791, and how did it change the way people saw him?

2. What was the Terror, and what larger ideas did the revolution leave behind?

1. He tried to flee France in secret; he was caught and brought back, and afterwards many people no longer trusted him or believed the monarchy could survive.

2. A period when the government executed thousands it judged enemies of the revolution. Despite the bloodshed, the revolution spread lasting ideas: popular power, equality before the law, and that no ruler is above the nation.

Discuss:
  • The revolution began with a declaration that all people are free and equal, and ended in the Terror. How can the same movement produce both such hope and such violence?

Grammar — cause & effect connectors

The whole story of the revolution is a chain of causes and effects: debt led to hunger, hunger to anger, anger to the fall of the Bastille. To tell it well, you need the connectors that link a reason to its result — and the trick is knowing which word attaches to which part of the sentence.

Connectors that introduce a CAUSE (a reason):
• because / since / as + clause — followed by a full clause (subject + verb): "Bread was scarce because the harvests had failed." "Since the nobles paid no tax, the burden fell on the poor." "As the crowd knew gunpowder was inside, they went to the Bastille."
• because of / due to / owing to + noun — followed by a noun, not a clause: "The famine came about because of a brutal winter." "Due to the crown's debts, taxes rose."
Connectors that introduce an EFFECT (a result):
• so + clause — the everyday link between two clauses: "The rich paid nothing, so the poor paid everything."
• therefore / as a result / consequently / thus — more formal; usually start a new sentence, followed by a comma: "The harvests failed. As a result, the price of bread soared." "The king fled and was caught; consequently, trust in the monarchy collapsed."
• which led to / which resulted in + noun — links a result onto the end of a clause: "Louis called the Estates-General, which led to the birth of the National Assembly."

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The revolution's ideals — liberty, equality, that power comes from the people — are now taken for granted across much of the world. Do we owe those ideas partly to the violence of 1789, or would they have arrived anyway?
  2. The same revolution produced both the Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Terror. Is that contradiction a betrayal of the revolution, or somehow part of its nature?
  3. France still celebrates 14 July as its national day. Should a country celebrate a day that included real bloodshed, or does the symbol outgrow what actually happened?
