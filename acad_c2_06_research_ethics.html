Why This Matters

Research misconduct—fabrication, falsification, plagiarism—erodes the foundation of science. But the language around misconduct is dense: fabrication, falsification, negligence, honest error all sit on a spectrum. Your job is to know where the line is, who decides where it is, and how to act with both integrity and self-protection.

Move 1: Defining Misconduct Precisely

1 What counts as misconduct? +
Research misconduct has a legal definition in most countries. The U.S. Office of Science and Technology defines it as fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism in proposing, conducting, or reporting research.
Fabrication

Inventing data or results that were not actually obtained. Reporting experiments that were never run.

Falsification

Manipulating, omitting, or selectively reporting data to misrepresent results. Altering outliers. Misrepresenting uncertainty.

NOT Misconduct

Honest error. Poor methodology. Mistaken analysis. Negligent record-keeping (may be subject to inquiry but not misconduct).

Move 2: Authorship and Contribution

2 Who is responsible for what? +
ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) criteria state that authors must have contributed substantially to conception/design or data acquisition/analysis; AND drafted or critically revised the intellectual content; AND approved the final version; AND agreed to accountability for all aspects of the work.
The Risk

If you co-author a paper, you share accountability for all data, even if you didn't generate it. Signing your name means you vouch for the integrity of the entire manuscript.

Move 3: Navigating IRB Language

3 What does the ethics committee actually govern? +
An Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Research Ethics Committee (REC) approves the protocol—the planned methodology, participant protections, and informed consent. They do NOT review the final data or police misconduct (that's usually a separate office).
Knowing the Difference

IRB approval protects participants. A misconduct office investigates falsified data. These are separate gatekeepers.

Move 4: Framing Informed Consent

4 Why informed consent language matters in a dispute +
Informed consent is not just a checkbox. It establishes what participants agreed to and what the researcher promised about data use. If misconduct is alleged, the consent document becomes evidence of what was promised.
Key Language

"Data will be analysed according to the pre-registered protocol" or "data will be reported accurately" are commitments that can be used to substantiate a misconduct claim.

Move 5: Whistleblowing Without Career Suicide

5 How to raise concerns safely +
Most institutions have a formal misconduct reporting process—usually an ombudsperson, integrity office, or research integrity committee. Reporting through formal channels provides legal protection. Informal complaints can isolate you.
The Safe Path

Document what you've found. Report to your institution's research integrity office, NOT directly to the media or other labs. Institutional processes are confidential (up to a point) and protect whistleblowers.

Move 6: Responding to Allegations

6 If YOU are accused +
A misconduct investigation can take months or years. You have a right to know the allegations, to respond in writing, and to see the evidence against you. Do not engage emotionally. Get legal advice.
Critical Move

Do not contact witnesses or the complainant. Do not destroy or alter records. Cooperate with the process and document your own version of events carefully.

Scaffold: Words to Use

  • "I've noticed a discrepancy between the raw data files and Table 3. Can we sit down and check this together?"
  • "I'm asking because I'm accountable for this manuscript too, and I want to make sure we're reporting accurately."
  • "This isn't an accusation—I want to understand what happened."
  • "If this is an honest error, we correct it. If there's something I'm misunderstanding, I want to hear it."
