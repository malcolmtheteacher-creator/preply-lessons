The Invention in the Drawer

Kodak built the world's first digital camera in 1975 — and the world's most profitable reason not to sell it.

Today: you'll take apart the most misquoted failure in business — and you'll master the language of causation: stem from, give rise to, set in motion, on the back of — the difference between describing what happened and explaining it.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • What is the most profitable thing your organisation does — and what would have to be true for you to volunteer to destroy it?
  • Inventing something and commercialising it are different capabilities. Which does your organisation actually have?
  • "We saw it coming" and "we acted on it" — why is the gap between those two sentences so common?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. The camera was almost given away; the money was in the film, the paper and the chemicals that every photograph consumed. The whole business ran on recurring revenue.
recurring revenue (n) — income that repeats automatically as customers keep consuming or subscribing, as opposed to one-off sales. The most defended thing in any company that has it.
2. Selling the device cheaply and profiting from what it consumes forever afterwards — printers and ink, consoles and games — is the classic razor-and-blades model.
the razor-and-blades model — from selling razors cheaply and blades dearly. Film photography was its purest form: the camera was the razor, and every roll of film was a blade.
3. Digital didn't just reduce film sales — it removed the reason film existed. For the core business, the new technology was existential.
existential (adj) — threatening the existence of the thing itself, not merely its profits. An existential threat / question / risk. Use sparingly; it loses force fast.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: margin · patent · legacy business. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the share of the selling price that is profit?
margin (n) — gross / operating / net margin; high-margin, thin-margin. Film earned extraordinarily high margins; digital hardware earned almost none — that asymmetry is the whole case.
2. Which term means the legal right to be the only one exploiting an invention for a period?
a patent (n) — to file / hold / license / infringe a patent. Kodak's digital patents later earned billions in licensing — the invention paid, even while the inventor sank.
3. Which term means the established business a company grew up on, now aging but still paying the bills?
a legacy business (n) — also legacy systems, legacy costs. Neutral-sounding, rarely neutral: calling something "legacy" in a strategy meeting is usually the first move in an argument about killing it.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The prototype was praised, patented — and quietly shelved. To shelve something is to…
  (a) put it aside indefinitely without formally killing it   (b) sell it to a competitor   (c) mass-produce it
(a) — to shelve (v) = to postpone indefinitely. Note the bureaucratic beauty of it: nothing is rejected, nothing proceeds. Near neighbour: to put something on the back burner.
2. Leadership knew the transition was coming and chose to milk the film business for as long as it lasted. To milk a business is to…
  (a) invest heavily in its growth   (b) extract maximum profit while deliberately not investing in its future   (c) sell it quickly
(b) — to milk (v) — informal but standard in strategy talk; the polite term is to harvest or to run for cash. A legitimate strategy, when it is chosen honestly and the cash goes somewhere with a future.

D · Collocation & word building

1. You know to cannibalise from the Nokia case. Kodak's version had a name inside the company: the fear of "trading ___-margin dollars for ___-margin pennies." Complete it — and say what the phrase assumes.
Trading high-margin dollars for low-margin pennies. The phrase assumes the dollars were going to keep existing — the one assumption the case destroys. The choice was never dollars vs pennies; it was pennies vs nothing.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ bankruptcy · to ___ for protection · the filing ___ a decade of decline. Which two different verbs fit, and which goes where?
To declare bankruptcy · to file for protection (US: to file for Chapter 11) · the filing capped / crowned a decade of decline. Chapter 11 = reorganisation under court protection, not necessarily death — Kodak emerged from it, much smaller.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — A toaster that took photographs

In December 1975, in a lab in Rochester, New York, a twenty-five-year-old Kodak engineer named Steven Sasson finished building a device the size of a toaster. It weighed close to four kilograms, captured a black-and-white image of one hundredth of a megapixel, and needed around twenty-three seconds to record each picture onto a cassette tape. It was, by any commercial standard, useless. It was also the first digital camera ever built — invented not by a challenger, but inside the company it would eventually help to sink.

Sasson demonstrated it to executives through 1976. He would later describe their reaction in a phrase that has entered business folklore: the response amounted to that's cute — but don't tell anyone about it. The reaction was not stupidity. Kodak at that time was one of the most valuable brands on earth, holding a commanding share of film sales in the United States, and its business model was close to perfect: cameras were cheap, film was not, and every birthday, holiday and wedding in the western world generated a stream of high-margin, recurring purchases — film, chemistry, printing paper. Sasson was showing the owners of that machine a filmless future. He was, in his own later words, presenting a product that would replace the thing the entire company existed to sell.

What Kodak did next is the detail the legend usually omits: it did not ignore digital. It patented Sasson's work and kept inventing — a megapixel sensor in 1986, pioneering work in digital imaging science, early consumer digital cameras in the 1990s, even an early photo-sharing website, acquired years before social media existed. Decade after decade, Kodak knew more about digital imaging than almost anyone alive. The invention was never the problem.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was built in 1975, and why did its inventor's employer find it unwelcome?

2. What does the legend usually get wrong about Kodak and digital?

1. The first digital camera — crude, slow, toaster-sized. It threatened a near-perfect razor-and-blades business in which the recurring, high-margin money came from film, chemicals and paper.

2. That Kodak ignored digital. In fact it patented the invention, built early sensors and cameras, and remained a leader in digital imaging research for decades. Invention was never the failure.

Discuss:
  • "That's cute — but don't tell anyone about it." As a pure business decision in 1976, was that response actually wrong? Defend it before you attack it.

Part 2 — The most rational failure in business

The trap was arithmetic, not blindness. Film earned margins that hardware could never approach — figures cited for the film-and-chemistry business run to sixty or seventy cents of gross profit on the dollar, against single digits for consumer electronics. Every dollar of digital revenue Kodak created destroyed several dollars of film profit, and the destruction fell on Kodak alone, while the digital opportunity was shared with Sony, Canon and the whole electronics industry. Executives described the fear in a phrase that circulated for years: trading high-margin dollars for low-margin pennies. On a spreadsheet, delaying the transition by one more year was always, always the rational choice.

So the company hedged, expensively, in both directions at once. It funded digital research generously while protecting film pricing; it launched digital products designed not to be too good; it bolted digital features onto film systems — a hybrid format in the 1990s, developed at great cost, satisfied neither world. Insiders and analysts described a company whose culture compounded the arithmetic: Rochester was a chemistry town, its leadership had risen through film, and the people whose skills digital would make obsolete were the same people deciding how fast digital should come.

Through the 1990s it barely mattered; film sales kept growing to a peak around the turn of the millennium, which — as at Nokia — became the argument for calm. Then the market turned, and the speed was vicious. US film sales began falling around 2000 and then roughly halved, and halved again, within a decade. Digital cameras made money for almost nobody for long, because the phone was coming for them too. Kodak, which had at one point employed well over a hundred thousand people, cut and cut, sold divisions, and burned through its reserves chasing consumer printing — one more razor-and-blades business, one war too late.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was delaying the transition always the rational choice on paper?

2. What made Kodak's hedging so expensive and so ineffective?

1. Because film margins were enormous and digital margins tiny: each digital dollar destroyed several film-profit dollars, and only Kodak bore that destruction while the digital prize was shared with the whole electronics industry. Year by year, delay maximised profit.

2. It funded both directions at once — generous digital research alongside film protection, hybrid products that satisfied neither world — while a film-raised culture in a chemistry town decided how fast its own obsolescence should arrive.

Discuss:
  • "The people whose skills the change would make obsolete were the people deciding its speed." Where does your organisation ask incumbents to schedule their own succession — and what does it get back?

Part 3 — Chapter 11, and the fork in the road

In January 2012, Eastman Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. The company that had put photography into ordinary hands — that had once sold the majority of the world's film and made Kodak moment a phrase in a dozen languages — reorganised under court protection, sold its patent portfolio for a fraction of earlier estimates, shed its consumer businesses, and emerged as a small commercial-printing and chemicals firm. Steven Sasson, by then retired, had received the National Medal of Technology from the President of the United States for inventing the thing his employer could not afford to want. The camera itself sits in a museum.

The case would be a simple tragedy were it not for the control experiment, and business history rarely supplies one this clean. Fujifilm — Kodak's lifelong rival, same industry, same product, same digital shock at the same moment — survived and thrived. Its leadership concluded early that film was doomed no matter what, harvested the declining business without sentiment, and forced the cash into anything its chemistry expertise could reach: pharmaceuticals, regenerative medicine, cosmetics built on collagen science, high-performance materials for flat screens. Today Fujifilm is a diversified healthcare and materials company worth many times its old rival. Same wave; one drowned, one surfed.

Which sharpens the question this case leaves on the table. Kodak's every annual decision was locally rational, and the sum was ruin; Fujifilm made a brutal, sentimental-sounding leap — we are not a film company, we are a chemistry company — and it was the leap that proved conservative. The live question for any business with a beautiful legacy margin is not whether it can see the future. Kodak saw it first. The question is who in the room is allowed to say out loud that the dollars are already gone, and only the pennies are real.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in January 2012, and what emerged from it?

2. Why does Fujifilm function as the control experiment — and what did it do differently?

1. Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. It sold its patents for far less than expected, shed the consumer businesses, and emerged as a small commercial-printing and chemicals firm.

2. Same industry, same shock, same moment — but Fujifilm accepted early that film was doomed, harvested it without sentiment, and redeployed the cash into pharmaceuticals, cosmetics and materials where its chemistry expertise applied. It redefined itself and thrived.

Discuss:
  • Every yearly Kodak decision was rational and the sum was ruin. What does that do to your confidence in annual planning as a decision rhythm?

Grammar — the language of cause & effect

Any analysis stands or falls on its causal verbs. "Digital killed Kodak" is a headline; a C1 speaker can say precisely how much causation they mean — direct, partial, indirect, or merely correlated.

Cause → effect (strong to weak):
• cause / lead to / result in — direct: "The collapse in film sales led to a decade of restructuring."
• trigger / spark / set in motion — starts a chain: "The 2000 downturn set in motion the final decline."
• give rise to / breed — creates conditions for: "High margins gave rise to a culture that could not price the future."
• contribute to / play a part in / feed into — partial: "Culture contributed to the delay; it did not cause it alone."
Effect → cause (arguing backwards):
• stem from / arise from / be rooted in: "The paralysis stemmed from the margin structure, not from ignorance."
• be a consequence / product / function of: "The delay was a function of who sat in the room."
• owe … to / can be traced (back) to: "Fujifilm owes its survival to an early admission of defeat."

The honesty markers: correlate with (no causation claimed) · coincide with (same time, no claim) · on the back of (riding, not causing) · and the analyst's escape hatch: "It is tempting to read X as the cause of Y — but…"

1. Choose the causal strength

Complete each sentence with the verb phrase in brackets, in the correct form.

1. The fear of cannibalisation (stem / from) the margin gap between film and hardware.

2. The 1975 prototype (set / motion) a rivalry inside the company that ran for thirty years.

3. Culture alone did not sink Kodak, but it certainly (contribute) the pace of the response.

1. stemmed from   2. set in motion   3. contributed to

2. Say exactly how much causation you mean

Rewrite each claim at the strength described. Write only the missing words.

1. TOO STRONG: "Sasson's demo caused the bankruptcy." → Weaken to a chain: The bankruptcy traced back, in part, to decisions first taken in 1976.

2. NO CLAIM YET: State only that two things moved together: The rise of camera phones the fall in standalone camera sales.

3. CONDITIONS, NOT CAUSE: Rochester's chemistry culture a leadership that priced film above the future. (gave…)

1. can be   2. coincided with / correlated with   3. gave rise to

Three different claims, three different levels of proof required. Choosing the weakest claim you can defend is what makes analysis credible.

3. The one-paragraph verdict

Complete the verdict with the causal language the meaning requires, then read it aloud.

Kodak's fall did not (result / direct) from a failure of invention — the company invented the disruption itself. It (stem) from a margin structure in which every digital dollar destroyed several film dollars, which (give / rise) a rational preference for delay, repeated annually for twenty-five years. A film-raised culture (feed) that preference, and the 2000 downturn merely (trigger) an ending that the arithmetic had already written. Fujifilm's survival (owe) less to better technology than to an earlier funeral.

result directly · stemmed · gave rise to · fed (into) · triggered · owed / owes

Notice the shape: one denied cause, one root cause, one enabling condition, one contributing factor, one trigger, one comparison. That is a complete causal argument in five sentences.

Going deeper

A note on the figures: margins, dates and market shares here are rounded and simplified; the famous executive quotes are as reported in later accounts, including Sasson's own retellings — folklore polished by repetition, which is itself part of the case.

What the case actually tests. Kodak is routinely filed under "failure to see change coming," and that filing is false — which is what makes it valuable. The company saw first, invented first, patented first. Its failure was that seeing was never the binding constraint. The binding constraint was that no executive could construct a career-safe argument for destroying the most profitable business in America one year earlier than absolutely necessary. Innovation was a technology problem; the transition was an incentive problem, and they solved only the one that could be solved in a lab.

Second: the annual trap. Every one-year delay was defensible; the sum was fatal. Systems judged in yearly increments will always prefer the locally rational path, which is why transitions of this size are governed properly only by decisions taken once, at board level, with a date attached — not re-litigated every budget round.

Third: Fujifilm's uncomfortable lesson. What saved it was not vision but disloyalty — a willingness to say that the company was not its product, then act on it against the grief of everyone whose identity said otherwise. The question a legacy-rich company must ask is not "what is our strategy for the new technology?" but "who here has permission to declare the old one already dead — and what happens to them afterwards?"

Talk it through:
  • Construct the career-safe argument the case says was impossible: you are a Kodak SVP in 1996 proposing to halve film investment. What do you say, and what happens to you?
  • "The company is not its product." Apply that sentence to your own organisation: what is it actually? What could it become that its product currently forbids?
  • Which decisions in your world are re-litigated annually that should be taken once with a date attached — and vice versa?
  • Kodak's patents out-earned its products in the end. Is inventing without commercialising a legitimate business — or a consolation prize with good PR?
