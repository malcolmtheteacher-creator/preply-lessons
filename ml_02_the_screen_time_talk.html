The Screen-Time Talk

You've been meaning to say it for about four months and you keep not saying it, because there is no version that doesn't sound like a telling-off. There is one — and it isn't about the phone at all.

The Scene 🎬

Every meal, about ten minutes in, the phone comes out face-down on the table. Then face-up.

Sam isn't doing anything wrong exactly, and that's what makes this hard. There's no incident to point at, no row to have — just a slow drift where you're increasingly in the room with someone who is somewhere else.

Two things will sink this conversation. One is making it about the phone, because then you're arguing about a device and he'll defend it, reasonably. The other is arriving as the person with the complaint, since you do exactly the same thing and he knows it.

What actually works is smaller than a complaint: name what you miss, own your half, and propose something you'd both try for a week.

  1. Is there a habit of someone close to you that you've never mentioned? What's stopped you?
  2. Where you're from, is it rude to have a phone on the table at a meal? Has that changed in your lifetime?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for raising a habit with someone you love. Each does one job, and each works on any habit — the phone, the lateness, the interrupting. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Light and unhurried, the way you'd mention something you noticed rather than something you've been building up to. Four months of rehearsal tends to come out heavy, and heavy makes a small thing sound like the start of a much bigger conversation — which will frighten them into defending rather than listening.

🚪 Opening without an accusation

Phrase 1

The job: asking permission and disarming in one breath. Which opening?

A. "We need to talk about your phone."
B. "Can I raise something — and it's honestly not a criticism?"
C. "You're always on that thing, do you realise?"
B — "Can I raise something — and it's honestly not a criticism?"
A sounds like a break-up and names the phone before you've framed anything. C is an accusation with "always" in it, and "always" is the word that starts arguments because it's never quite true. B asks permission, which gives them a second to arrive, and pre-labels the thing so they don't have to brace. "Honestly" is doing real work — without it, "not a criticism" sounds like the preface to one.

Take it anywhere: "Can I raise something — and it's honestly not a criticism?"
Phrase 2

The job: sizing it accurately — small, but not nothing. Complete it:

"It's a small thing, but it's been ___ ___ ___."

"It's a small thing, but it's been on my mind."
The two halves stop each other going wrong. "A small thing" prevents alarm; "on my mind" prevents it being dismissed as trivial, because it says the small thing has been recurring. Saying that it's been sitting with you is also more honest than pretending it just occurred to you, and honesty here buys you a hearing.

Take it anywhere: "It's a small thing, but it's been on my mind."
Phrase 3

The job: disqualifying yourself from the moral high ground before they put you there. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I'm as bad as you are, so this isn't me on a high horse."
To be on your high horse is to behave as though you're morally superior. Naming the accusation before it's made removes their best defence — "well, you do it too" — and it happens to be true, which is why it lands. Say it before the complaint, not after; afterwards it sounds like a retreat.

Take it anywhere: "I'm as bad as you are." / "This isn't me on a high horse."

🪞 Describing your side, not their fault

Phrase 4

The job: naming the feeling rather than the behaviour — which is the move that stops them arguing. Complete it:

"I end up feeling a bit ___."

"I end up feeling a bit invisible."
Nobody can dispute how you feel, so there's nothing to defend against. "End up" is quietly important — it describes a result rather than an accusation, as though neither of you chose it. And "a bit" keeps the scale honest; "I feel completely invisible" would be a much bigger claim and he'd have to answer it.

Take it anywhere: "I end up feeling a bit ___." — invisible, sidelined, like a nuisance, like the last thing on the list.
Phrase 5

The job: moving it off the object and onto the real thing. Put the pieces in order:

and more thatIt's less aboutI miss you.the phones
"It's less about the phones and more that I miss you."
The hinge of the whole conversation. As long as it's about the phone, he can defend the phone — he needs it for work, he was only checking something. "I miss you" cannot be argued with and completely changes what's being discussed. Note "less… and more…" rather than "not… but…": it concedes that the phone is part of it, which is fairer and more believable.

Take it anywhere: "It's less about ___ and more that ___." One of the most useful frames in the whole series.

🤝 Making it theirs too

Phrase 6

The job: asking for their limit instead of setting yours. Which version gets an arrangement that lasts?

A. "Can I ask what would work for you?"
B. "So can we say no phones after seven?"
C. "I think we should both make more of an effort, really."
A — "Can I ask what would work for you?"
B is a rule you've written, and rules written by one person get quietly abandoned. C is a sentiment — everyone agrees and nothing changes, because "more effort" isn't a behaviour. A gets you his limit, which here turns out to be narrower than you'd have dared ask for and wider than he'd have accepted being told. People keep the arrangements they helped design.

Take it anywhere: "Can I ask what would work for you?"
Phrase 7

The job: saying why you're asking rather than telling — the reason matters as much as the question. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "If it's just me telling you off it'll last four days."
Honest and slightly funny, and it makes you both realists rather than one of you being the enforcer. It also predicts the failure mode out loud, which makes it less likely — you've agreed in advance that a policed rule isn't the plan.

Take it anywhere: "If it's just me ___, it'll last four days."

⏳ Making it a trial

Phrase 8

The job: proposing a test rather than a change — because a week is easy to agree to. Complete it:

"Shall we ___ ___ a week and see how it feels?"

"Shall we give it a week and see how it feels?"
A week is small enough that nobody has to be sure, which is exactly why it gets agreed to — and it usually outlasts the week. "See how it feels" keeps it experimental rather than moral: you're finding out together whether you like it, not correcting a fault. And "shall we" makes it joint.

Take it anywhere: "Shall we give it a week and see how it feels?" Works for any change either of you is uncertain about.
Phrase 9

The job: agreeing that it binds you too, before they have to ask. Complete it:

"If I do it, ___ ___ — I'd rather that than you sitting there noticing."

"If I do it, say something — I'd rather that than you sitting there noticing."
Volunteering to be corrected is what converts your complaint into a shared arrangement. The second half is the persuasive part: it names the alternative — them silently resenting you — which is precisely the state you've just spent four months in, and neither of you wants a rerun of it in reverse.

Take it anywhere: "If I do it, say something — I'd rather that than you sitting there noticing."
Phrase 10

The job: when they ask how long you've been sitting on it — answering honestly rather than minimising. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "About four months. I know — I should have just said."
The temptation is "oh, only a few weeks", and it's a small lie that costs you something: it tells them the thing is smaller than it is, and it hides the fact that you found it hard to raise. Admitting the delay — and that you should have said sooner — makes it much easier for them to raise their own next time. Which is the real point of the whole conversation.

Take it anywhere: "I should have just said." Owns the silence without grovelling for it.

⚡ The twist

Sam agrees to the week, warmly. Then he says: "Can I say mine, then? Because you do the same thing when your mum rings — you go somewhere else in your head for the whole evening afterwards and you never say why."

He's right, you didn't know it showed, and it's a considerably bigger thing than a phone at dinner. What do you say?

The Echo 🕰️

On Christmas Eve 1914 the shooting stopped along parts of the Western Front. Men who had spent months trying to kill each other put down what they were holding, climbed out, and met in the middle of the mud.

They swapped cigarettes and buttons and names. Somewhere near Ypres they played football. It lasted a day or two, no general ordered it, and afterwards the high command made sure it never happened again.

What made it possible was absurdly small: both sides put down the thing in their hands at the same time. That's all a phone at dinner is. Two people agreeing to be unarmed for an hour.
