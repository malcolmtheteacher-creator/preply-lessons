What Is Critical Thinking?

Critical thinking is not criticism. It's not negative or pessimistic. Rather, it's the ability to analyze, evaluate, and synthesize information using logic and evidence—not just accepting ideas because they sound good or come from authority.

Three Core Components

1. Analysis: Breaking down complex ideas into parts; identifying assumptions; seeing the structure of an argument
2. Evaluation: Judging the quality of evidence; assessing credibility of sources; weighing competing claims
3. Synthesis: Combining information from multiple sources; developing new insights; building your own reasoned position

Why Universities Demand It

At university, you're not simply learning facts. You're learning to think like an expert in your field. Professors don't want you to memorize and repeat; they want you to engage with ideas critically. That's what distinguishes a graduate from someone who's read a textbook.

In essays, exams, and discussions, B2 students demonstrate critical thinking by:

  • Questioning claims rather than accepting them automatically
  • Supporting arguments with evidence
  • Acknowledging counterarguments
  • Identifying limitations and gaps in research
  • Making connections between ideas

Credibility: Evaluating Sources

Not all sources are equal. A claim made in a peer-reviewed journal carries different weight than a claim made on social media. Critical thinking requires assessing source credibility.

Ask These Questions

  • Who is the author? What are their qualifications and potential biases?
  • Where is it published? Peer-reviewed journals are more credible than blogs.
  • When was it published? Is it current? (Some fields value recent research highly.)
  • Why was it written? Is the author trying to inform or persuade? Sell something?
  • What evidence is provided? Are claims supported by data or anecdotes?
  • Is there acknowledgment of limitations? Does the author admit uncertainty or gaps?

Source Hierarchy (Strongest to Weakest)

  1. Peer-reviewed journal articles (articles reviewed by experts before publication)
  2. Books by academic publishers (vetted by editors)
  3. Government reports and data (authoritative but sometimes biased)
  4. Reports from reputable organizations (NGOs, think tanks)
  5. News from quality publications (journalists should verify facts)
  6. Blogs, Wikipedia, social media (minimal verification; useful for starting research, not citing)

Identifying Logical Fallacies

Logical fallacies are common errors in reasoning. Recognizing them helps you spot weak arguments—including your own.

Ad Hominem: Attacking the person instead of addressing their argument.

Example: "You can't trust her opinion on climate change because she's not a scientist." (Even if true, it doesn't address her actual arguments.)

False Cause: Assuming that because two things happen together, one caused the other.

Example: "Coffee sales increase every summer, and so do drowning deaths. Therefore, coffee causes drowning." (Both increase because it's summer—separate causes.)

Appeal to Authority: Accepting something as true just because an authority figure said it, without evaluating evidence.

Example: "A famous actor says this vitamin works, so it must be effective." (Actors aren't necessarily experts.)

Slippery Slope: Claiming that one event will lead to a series of negative consequences without evidence.

Example: "If we allow remote work, soon nobody will come to the office and companies will collapse." (Unsupported chain of causation.)

Straw Man: Misrepresenting someone's argument to make it easier to attack.

Example: Actual claim: "We should reduce plastic packaging." Straw man: "They want to ban all plastic forever!" (Not what was said.)

Begging the Question: Assuming the conclusion is true in your argument for it.

Example: "Social media is addictive because it addicts people to use it." (Uses the conclusion as proof.)

Bias in Sources

All sources have some bias—it's inevitable. What matters is recognizing bias and accounting for it.

Types of Bias

  • Confirmation bias: Tendency to seek information confirming what you already believe
  • Selection bias: Showing only data that supports your position, hiding contradictory data
  • Publication bias: Positive results published more often than negative results (affecting our view of what's true)
  • Funding bias: Research funded by interested parties (pharmaceutical companies, tobacco industry) may favor certain conclusions
  • Political bias: Sources aligned with particular political views present information selectively

Combating Your Own Bias

You can't eliminate bias, but you can manage it:

  • Actively seek sources opposing your view
  • Ask why you find certain arguments more convincing—is it evidence or comfort?
  • Read sources from different geographic regions and political perspectives
  • Look for nuance and acknowledgment of counterarguments (sign of balanced thinking)

Case Study: Analyzing a Claim Critically

Let's work through a real-world example using critical thinking tools.

Claim: "Studies show that students who use laptops in lectures perform worse academically than those who take handwritten notes."

Step 1: Break Down the Argument

  • Claim: Laptop use → worse academic performance
  • Evidence: "Studies show..."
  • Implied: This is proven by research

Step 2: Ask Critical Questions

Which studies?

The claim provides no sources. Without knowing which studies, we can't evaluate them. This is a red flag. Always ask for specifics.

How was performance measured?

Did they measure test scores? GPAs? Long-term retention? Students might perform worse on immediate tests but better long-term. The metric matters.

Could other factors explain the result?

Maybe students who take laptops are less motivated, attend less often, or study less outside class. Maybe they're distracted by the internet. The laptop itself might not be the cause.

What's the effect size?

How much worse? 1% worse? 50% worse? A tiny difference might not be practically important, even if statistically significant.

Step 3: Consider Counterarguments

  • Some students need laptops for accessibility (disabilities, processing speed)
  • For some subjects, laptops enable better organization of notes
  • Distractions exist with or without laptops—pen and paper don't guarantee focus

Step 4: Form a Reasoned Conclusion

Rather than "Laptops are bad for learning," a critical thinker might conclude: "Research suggests that some students perform worse using laptops, possibly because laptop-using students are more prone to distraction. However, this depends on individual learning styles, subject matter, and self-discipline. The medium (laptop vs. paper) matters less than active, focused engagement."

Notice the difference: specific, qualified, acknowledging complexity and limitations.

Developing Your Critical Perspective

What claim do you encounter regularly that you've accepted without questioning?

This week, try identifying three claims in news, social media, or conversations that you usually accept. Ask: What's the evidence? Who benefits from me believing this? What's the counterargument? This practice trains critical thinking.

How do you usually identify "good" information?

Are you relying on how well it's written, who said it, or whether it feels true? At university, you'll need to evaluate based on methodology and evidence instead. This takes practice.

Where is your strongest bias?

Everyone has subjects they feel strongly about. Acknowledging your bias is the first step to managing it. If you're passionate about a topic, you're more vulnerable to confirmation bias—deliberately seek opposing views.

Critical thinking skill-building: Start with small claims. As you practice evaluating arguments in everyday contexts, you'll develop the habit of asking questions. By the time you're writing university essays, critical analysis will feel natural.
