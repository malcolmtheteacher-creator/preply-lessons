Religious Attitudes to Wealth and Poverty

Christianity: Wealth as Responsibility

Key teaching: "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God" (Mark 10:25)

This doesn't mean being rich is sinful — it means wealth can be a distraction from serving God and neighbour.

Stewardship: Wealth is a gift from God to be used responsibly. Christians are "stewards" — managers, not owners.

Modern charities: Christian Aid, CAFOD work across Africa, Asia, Latin America fighting poverty, hunger, and injustice.

Islam: Wealth as a Test

Core belief: All wealth ultimately belongs to Allah. Humans are trustees.

Zakah (again): 2.5% annual duty on savings.

Riba: Prohibition of interest (usury). Borrowing money should not exploit people.

Modern movement: Islamic banking and finance follow these principles. Islamic Relief operates humanitarian aid in 80+ countries.

Sikhism: Honest Work and Sharing

Kirat Karni: Earn honestly through hard work, not through theft or fraud.

Dasvandh: Give 10% of income to the community or to help others. (This is more generous than Zakah's 2.5%.)

Langar (again): The free community kitchen funded by sewa and contributions, feeding anyone who comes.

Modern charity: Khalsa Aid provides disaster relief, food, healthcare across the world.

Humanism: Poverty as a Solvable Problem

View: Poverty is not divine punishment — it's a human problem with human solutions.

Effective altruism: Use evidence and reason to figure out which interventions reduce poverty most effectively.

Modern organisations: Oxfam campaigns for systemic change (fair trade, education, women's rights) alongside emergency aid.

Case Study Analysis

Read these real charity examples. Evaluate which approach (and why) is most effective:

Christian Aid: Building Water Systems

Instead of just handing out water bottles, Christian Aid builds wells and trains local people to maintain them. Communities gain independence, health improves, women (who often collect water) can go to school.

Islamic Relief: Education for Girls

In areas where poverty keeps girls out of school, Islamic Relief provides scholarships, books, and safe learning spaces. Education breaks the poverty cycle.

Khalsa Aid: Emergency Food

After earthquakes, floods, or famines, Khalsa Aid volunteers distribute hot meals immediately, no questions asked. Later, they help rebuild livelihoods.

Oxfam: Fair Trade Campaigns

Oxfam lobbies governments and companies to ensure farmers earn fair prices. This addresses the root of poverty: unfair trade systems, not charity, creates lasting change.

Which approach seems most sustainable? Why?

Statement to Evaluate

"Charity is not enough — we need to change the systems that cause poverty."

Do you agree or disagree? Why?

Think about:

  • What's the difference between charity (helping individuals) and justice (changing systems)?
  • Can religion support both? Which traditions emphasize which?
  • Is it possible to end poverty without changing economic systems?
  • Is charity meaningful even if it doesn't solve the root problem?

Quick-Fire Recall

1. What does "camel through the eye of a needle" mean?
It's easier for a camel to fit through a needle's eye than for a rich person to enter God's kingdom — wealth can distract from serving God
2. What is Christian stewardship?
Wealth is a gift from God; Christians are responsible managers (stewards), not owners
3. What is Riba?
Islamic prohibition of interest (usury) — lending money should not exploit the borrower
4. What is Dasvandh?
Sikh practice of giving 10% of income to help others or the community (more generous than Zakah)
5. Name one charity per tradition and what it does
Christian Aid, CAFOD, Islamic Relief, Khalsa Aid, Oxfam (answers vary depending on choice)
