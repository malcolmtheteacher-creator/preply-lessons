Listen in on this conversation

Ana: Are you coming to the barbecue on Saturday?

Leo: I hope so. If it rains, we'll move it inside — my flat's big enough.

Ana: And what about the neighbours, if it gets noisy?

Leo: They won't mind. If we finish before ten, nobody complains.

Look again at "if it rains" and "we'll move it". Both sentences are really about the future — so why isn't it "if it will rain"? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
Two clauses, two jobs. The if part uses the present simple (rains, gets, finish) even though we mean the future. The will part is kept for the result (we'll move it, they won't mind). Mix them up — "if it will rain" — and it sounds wrong to a native speaker, every time.
Today: if + present simple, will / won't + base verb — plus the word unless — so you can talk about real plans, warnings and promises for the future.

Making Plans: "If It Rains, We'll Stay In"

For real things that might happen — and what you'll actually do about it.

Spot the pattern

Three real plans:

  • "If the shop's still open, I'll grab some bread on the way home."
  • "If Tom finishes work early, he'll pick up the kids."
  • "If we leave now, we'll catch the six o'clock train."

Before you check: which part uses the present simple, and which part uses "will"?

Check the pattern CLICK
The if part: present simple ('s still open, finishes, leave). The result part: will + base verb ('ll grab, he'll pick up, we'll catch). "Will" only ever appears in the result — never after "if".
If + present simple, ... will / won't + base verb.
... will / won't + base verb if + present simple.

One comma rule

"If we leave now, we'll catch the train."
→
"We'll catch the train if we leave now."

Same meaning, no comma the second way round. The comma only appears when "if" starts the sentence.

Your turn

Say: "If Mia (get) home before six, we'll all eat together."

2. You're planning a trip depending on your savings.

Say: "If we save enough money, we (go) to the coast in August."

Warnings & Promises: "If You're Late Again, He Won't Be Happy"

For warnings, promises, and things that won't happen if you don't act.

Spot the pattern

  • "If you don't book early, the flights will be really expensive."
  • "If Jake's late again, his manager won't be happy."
  • "If we don't leave soon, we'll miss the beginning of the film."

Same question: what's true about the "if" part here, even though two of them are negative?

Check the pattern CLICK
The "if" part can be negative too (don't book, don't leave) — it's still present simple, never "will". The result still uses will or won't + base verb. Nothing about a warning changes the basic structure.
If + present simple (positive or negative), ... will / won't + base verb.

Your turn

1. A friend is still getting ready and you're both about to miss a train.

Warn them: "If you don't hurry, we (miss) the last train."

2. Sam has a report due Friday and hasn't started it.

Tell a colleague: "If Sam (not / finish) the report by Friday, his boss won't be pleased."

SPEAK

Think of a real rule at your work, school or home. Say one warning out loud: "If you ..., ... won't be happy."

Unless: If Not

One word that means "if...not" — plus offers and promises with the same pattern.

Spot the pattern

  • "Unless it rains, we'll have the barbecue outside."
  • "I'll lend you my umbrella unless you already have one."
  • "Unless you tell me the address, I won't be able to find the house."

What do you think "unless it rains" really means?

Check the pattern CLICK
Unless = if...not. "Unless it rains, we'll have the barbecue outside" = "If it doesn't rain, we'll have the barbecue outside." Same structure as "if": unless + present simple, ... will / won't + base verb.
Unless + present simple, ... will / won't + base verb.
(= If + present simple + not, ...)
Watch out: "unless" already means "if...not" — so never add "don't / doesn't" after it. "Unless it doesn't rain" is a double negative and doesn't mean what people think. Just say "unless it rains".

Same pattern, offers and promises

  • "If you need a hand, I'll help you carry those."
  • "If you're free later, I'll show you around."

It isn't only warnings — the exact same structure makes offers and promises sound natural.

Your turn

1. Friday nights at your favourite restaurant get busy fast.

Warn a friend: "Unless you (book) a table, we might not get in."

2. A new colleague looks a bit lost around the office.

Make an offer: "If you (be) free this afternoon, I'll show you around."

Make one promise to a friend using "if", and one warning using "unless". Say them both out loud.
