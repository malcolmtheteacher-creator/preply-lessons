The Numbers

Read the data. Say what it means. Defend what it doesn't prove.

Warm-up: Interpret This Data

Data: Sales rose 15% in Q1 but fell 8% in Q2. The same period last year showed 12% rise in Q1 and 3% rise in Q2. You have 60 seconds to answer: What does this tell us? What doesn't it tell us? What would you need to know before making a decision?

1:00

Your thinking process

What the data shows: Q1 performance is stronger this year (15% vs 12%). Q2 performance is weaker (fell 8% vs rose 3%). There's volatility.

What it doesn't tell you: Whether the Q2 decline is seasonal, temporary, or signals a real trend. Whether the client mix changed. Whether one big deal distorted the numbers.

What you'd need to know: Is the 8% decline distributed across clients or driven by one? Is it seasonal? What's the forecast for Q3 and Q4?

Six Moves for Data Communication

Click each move. These are the skills for speaking about data without hiding behind it or misrepresenting it.

1. Describe Before Interpret +

Why it matters: Separate what the data shows from what it means. Describe first. Then interpret.

Weak: "Our product is winning in the market."
Strong: "Usage is up 25% year-over-year. Three new enterprise clients. Churn is down 8%."

The benefit: They heard the facts first. Then you can offer your interpretation.

2. Distinguish Trend from Noise +

Why it matters: A single data point is not a trend. Three data points in the same direction might be. People often confuse the two.

Wrong: "We had a good month. We're winning."
Right: "We've had three consecutive months of growth. That's a trend. One month isn't enough to be confident."

The benefit: You're being analytically honest. You're not overstating weak signals.

3. Name the Limitations Upfront +

Why it matters: Every dataset has limitations. If you don't name them, someone will. And you'll look dishonest.

"This data comes from our top 50 clients, not our full base. So it skews toward our largest accounts. We don't have good visibility into SMB behavior from this data."

The benefit: You've shown integrity. You're not hiding the caveats.

4. Correlation vs Causation: The Critical Distinction +

Why it matters: The most common data mistake: seeing two things happen at the same time and assuming one caused the other.

Wrong: "We launched the new feature in March. Sales went up. The feature caused the sales increase."
Right: "We launched the feature in March and sales went up. But we also hired two new salespeople in March. We can't isolate which caused the increase without testing."

The benefit: You're not making causal claims you can't defend.

5. The Confident Recommendation +

Why it matters: Data supports decisions, but it doesn't make them. You still have to recommend.

"Based on the data, I'd recommend we double down on this segment. Here's why the evidence for X is stronger than for Y. And here's what we'd measure to know if I'm right."

The benefit: You've given them a clear recommendation, backed by data, but not hiding behind it.

6. The Honest Uncertainty +

Why it matters: Data can be suggestive but not conclusive. Say so.

"The data is suggestive but not conclusive. Before I'd commit major resources to this, I'd want to see [more data / a test / validation from another source]."

The benefit: You're being honest about what the data actually supports. That builds trust.

Analyse Your Data

Pick one of three datasets. Analyse it aloud and give a recommendation. You have 2 minutes to prepare. Speak for 3 minutes. Record yourself.

A. Employee Satisfaction Scores

The data: Overall satisfaction: down from 7.2 to 6.8 (out of 10). Leadership confidence: down from 7.1 to 6.4. Work-life balance: down from 5.9 to 5.2. Pay satisfaction: down from 6.0 to 5.8. Sample: 45 people this year, 38 last year.

Your challenge: What does this data actually show? What doesn't it show? What would you recommend? What's missing before you'd act?

B. Customer Churn Data

The data: Churn rate: 8% this quarter vs 5% last quarter. Customers with 5+ years tenure: 0% churn. Customers under 1 year: 22% churn. Customers citing price as reason: 60% of churners. Customers citing product fit: 25%. Support quality: 30% of churners mention it.

Your challenge: Where should you focus? What's the core issue? What would you recommend addressing first?

C. Marketing Campaign Performance

The data: Campaign A: 50k impressions, 5k clicks, 500 conversions (10% conversion rate). Campaign B: 100k impressions, 8k clicks, 400 conversions (5% conversion rate). Cost per acquisition: Campaign A $200, Campaign B $500. Both campaigns target the same audience.

Your challenge: Which campaign is better? What does the data tell you? What's missing? Should you scale one or kill one?

Reflection & Language Bank

Data Literacy for Leaders

Data doesn't make decisions. Leaders do. Data informs. Data constrains. But the final choice is yours. The best leaders know this and use data accordingly.

The core skill: Being able to say "Here's what the data shows. Here's what it doesn't show. Here's my recommendation. Here's what would change my mind."

Data Communication Language Bank

"The data shows..."
"We see [specific metric] at [specific number]."
