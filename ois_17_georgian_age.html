The Bridge

Why these lessons matter now: Elizabeth's England was strong but still anxious about Spanish Catholic power and internal division. By Lesson 17, something remarkable has happened: England and Scotland are no longer enemies pulling against each other. They have become one nation. That union is born in this lesson—and it will reshape the world.

II. The Union of Britain (1707)

Since 1603, when James VI of Scotland became James I of England, the two kingdoms had shared a king but remained separate nations with separate parliaments. Both passed their own laws, levied their own taxes, and sometimes at odds. Marshall explains what changed:

There had been only one King since 1603, but there had been two Parliaments making separate laws, and wise men saw that there could be no real union until there was only one Parliament with English and Scots discussing laws together. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Queen Anne finally achieved it. On 23 October 1707, the first British Parliament met. Marshall records her words to them:

I desire and expect from all my subjects of both nations, that from henceforth they act with all possible respect and kindness to one another, that so it may appear to all the world they have hearts disposed to become one people. This will give me great pleasure. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Queen Anne's speech on the Act of Union
The historian's note: The Act of Union of 1707 created the Kingdom of Great Britain. The Scots expected the English to dominate them economically and politically. In fact, many brilliant Scots soon rose to great heights in British life—Hume, Adam Smith, Burns, Scott, and many others. Scotland's intellectual contribution to the 18th century was remarkable. But union also meant the loss of Scottish independence, the decline of Edinburgh as a political capital, and the beginning of Highland decline. For some it was progress; for others, it was a loss.

The Hanoverian Succession & George I

Anne died in 1714 with no children. Parliament chose George, Elector of Hanover, to be king. He spoke no English. He had never even been to Britain before his coronation. Marshall captures the oddness:

George I was a German prince who could barely speak a word of English. Yet now he was King of Great Britain and Ireland. The courtiers had to speak German to him; he distrusted English politicians; he spent as much time as he could back in Hanover. A German-speaking king sat on an English throne.

The Jacobite Threat: Bonnie Prince Charlie

Many Scots and Irish believed the true king was still James II's line—the Stuarts. They were called "Jacobites" (from the Latin Jacobus for James). Two Jacobite risings had already failed (1715, 1719). In 1745, one last desperate attempt: Prince Charles Edward Stuart, "Bonnie Prince Charlie," landed in Scotland and claimed the throne.

He was young, charming, and daring. Marshall describes his arrival and his magnetism:

When Charles Stuart landed in the Highlands with only a handful of followers, the Highlanders flocked to his banner. Lovely ladies and brave men crowded to see and do honour to their Bonnie Prince Charlie, and they went away happy if they had touched his hand or heard his voice. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Before the Battle of Prestonpans, where he won a shocking victory, Marshall records his words to the chiefs:

Charles Stuart has come to claim his own and win the crown of his ancestors, or die in the attempt. Lochiel, if he chooses, may stay at home and learn the fate of his Prince from the newspapers. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Prince Charles Edward to the chief Cameron of Lochiel

And in a moment of commitment, he drew his sword:

Gentlemen, I have thrown away the scabbard. There is no turning back. My sword shall never be sheathed until I have conquered or died in the attempt. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Prince Charles Edward before Prestonpans

But the dream died at Culloden Moor in April 1746. A Highland army met the Duke of Cumberland's professional soldiers. The Highlanders charged, the redcoats fired in disciplined volleys, and the cause was lost. Hundreds died. It was the end of the old Highland Scotland—the clan system, the Gaelic tongue, the independent warrior culture. Bonnie Prince Charlie fled to France.

The historian's note: Culloden was not just a military defeat. It was the moment when the old Britain—the one that existed in separate tribes, kingdoms, and regions—finally gave way to a unified nation-state. The British army defeated the Highlanders not just militarily but culturally. Tartan and Gaelic became relics of the past, preserved in museums and ballads, not lived as daily reality. For Scots, Culloden is a moment of profound loss. Yet paradoxically, within a generation, the genius of Scotland was helping to build the British Empire.

III. Empire & the Loss of America

With Scotland subdued and united, Britain turned outward. The Georgian age was the age of empire. British merchants and soldiers reached across the world—to India, to Canada, to America. But empire brought conflict, and one colonial war would cost Britain everything.

Clive in India: The Battle of Plassey (1757)

In India, the East India Company was not just trading; it was fighting. Robert Clive, a young clerk turned soldier, won a victory that changed everything. At Plassey in Bengal in 1757, he defeated a vast Indian army with a much smaller force. Marshall tells of Clive rising to power:

Young Robert Clive, who had been a mere clerk in the East India Company, became a soldier. He fought the French for control of India. At the Battle of Plassey in 1757, Clive defeated a much larger Indian army. From that moment, the power of Britain in India grew steadily. Within a hundred years, the whole of the vast subcontinent was under British rule.

The historian's note: Marshall writes from an imperial viewpoint—Plassey is told as a triumph of British courage and cunning. But the battle was won partly through betrayal and partly through luck. The Nawab's general, Mir Jafar, switched sides in the midst of the battle. Clive's victory opened India to British conquest and exploitation. By the end of the 19th century, India was Britain's greatest prize and wealth-maker—but the cost to Indians was immense: famine, deindustrialization, and the loss of political autonomy. Marshall's 1905 view of empire did not include these costs.

Wolfe in Canada: The Plains of Abraham (1759)

In North America, the French and British fought for control of Canada. The key city was Quebec, held by the French general the Marquis de Montcalm. A young British general, James Wolfe, led the assault. The battle that decided it was fought on the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec's walls.

Marshall records Montcalm's words when he saw the British soldiers appearing on the heights:

I see them where they ought not to be. We must fight them, and I am going to crush them. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Marquis de Montcalm at Quebec

But Montcalm was wrong. In the battle that followed, Wolfe was wounded twice, then again fatally. Yet as he lay dying, one of his officers cried out:

See, they run, they run! — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, an officer to the dying General Wolfe

The French were retreating. Montcalm was also mortally wounded. With his last breath, Wolfe had conquered Canada for Britain. Marshall records his last words:

Now, God be praised, I will die in peace. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, General James Wolfe, dying on the Plains of Abraham

Before the battle, legend says Wolfe had recited a line from Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard":

Now I would rather be the author of that poem than take Quebec. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, General Wolfe before the assault

The American Revolution (1776)

But now the empire began to fracture. The thirteen American colonies had grown wealthy and confident. They resented paying taxes to a distant British Parliament where they had no representatives. When Britain tried to tighten its grip after 1763, the Americans resisted. In 1776, they declared independence.

The Loss of America

Marshall wrote her book in 1905, long after America had become independent and powerful. She could see that losing the colonies had been a catastrophe—but also perhaps inevitable. The Americans had been born in freedom. They would not accept being ruled. British attempts to tax them, to station soldiers in their towns, to close their ports—all this had driven them to revolution. And once they declared independence, Britain could not win the war. France and Spain sided with America against Britain. By 1783, it was over. America was lost.

The historian's note: Marshall's perspective on the American Revolution is the view of a 1905 British writer looking back. She can see now that the empire had grown too large, too fast, and that the colonists were not willing subjects but people with their own vision of what freedom meant. The Revolution represented two competing ideas of liberty: Britain's view of liberty as rights granted by a sovereign Parliament, and America's view of liberty as a natural right preceding government. The conflict was philosophical as much as political. And when it ended, a new nation—destined to become more powerful than Britain itself—was born.

VI. Your Turn: Reflection & Discussion

Choose 2–3 of these questions. Think deeply. Write honestly.

What makes a real union?

Scotland and England had the same king for over 100 years before they united their Parliaments. What makes two nations "one"? Is it the flag? The parliament? Something deeper?

Why was Bonnie Prince Charlie so loved?

He lost his battle and his cause, yet became a legend. Why do lost causes and tragic heroes capture our hearts more than successful ones?

Was Culloden a tragedy or progress?

The Battle of Culloden destroyed the Highland way of life. But it also united Britain and allowed Scotland's genius to flourish in the modern world. Was this a loss or a transformation?

Empire from whose perspective?

Marshall tells the story of Clive and Wolfe as triumphs. But what if you were an Indian or a French Canadian? How would these same victories feel?

Why did Britain lose America?

The Americans had been British once. What changed? Was it the taxes? The distance? Or something about how freedom is understood differently in different places?

What is an "Island Story" really for?

Marshall wrote in 1905, when Britain still had a vast empire. If she were writing today, how would she tell the story of the Georgian age differently?
