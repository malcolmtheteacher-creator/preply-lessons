C1 Level • Better Presentations

What Makes a Presentation Easy to Follow?

Think about a presentation you have watched recently. Was it easy to understand? Did you know where the speaker was going and what to expect? Or were you confused, wondering what the point was?

The structure of a presentation is one of the most important factors in whether your audience understands your message. A well-structured presentation guides your listeners through your ideas logically and persuasively. It keeps them engaged and helps them remember what you have said.

In this lesson, you will discover how professional presenters organise their ideas, learn powerful structures you can use, and practise identifying transition signals that help audiences follow along.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify and name different presentation structures
  • Use the "Power of Three" principle to organise your ideas
  • Recognise verbal, vocal, and visual transition signals
  • Plan and deliver a structured presentation on a topic of your choice

Identifying Presentation Structures

Professional presenters use structures to organise their ideas. Let"s look at an example from a business presentation:

To start with, I"ll provide a brief outline of how Aargson Drinks started. Then I"ll run through our current product range and why we believe they will appeal to your customers. I"ll finish by sharing some of the exciting new products we are launching next month. There"ll be an opportunity for you to ask questions at the end.

What structure is this speaker using? Look at the three options below:

A. Problem → Solution → Outcome
B. Past → Present → Future
C. Rationale → Change → Benefits
Correct answer: B (Past → Present → Future)

The speaker mentions how Aargson Drinks started (Past), their current product range (Present), and the exciting new products we are launching next month (Future).

Common Presentation Structures

Here are three popular structures used by professional presenters:

  • Problem → Solution → Outcome: Start with a problem, propose a solution, and explain the benefits of solving it.
  • Past → Present → Future: Begin with background information, discuss the current situation, and introduce what"s coming next.
  • Rationale → Change → Benefits: Explain the reasons for a change, describe what will change, and outline the advantages.

    • Each of these structures helps your audience understand your message because it follows a logical path.

    The Power of Three

    Why do all these structures have three parts? Because groups of three are memorable, balanced, and persuasive. Look at these four different three-part structures:

    Structure 1

    Problem / Solution / Outcome

    Structure 2

    Rationale / Change / Benefits

    Structure 3

    Past / Present / Future

    Structure 4

    Pros / Cons / Best Choice

    Question: What do these structures have in common?
    Answer: All of these structures use groups of three. Three is a magic number in presentations — it"s easy to remember, it feels complete, and it"s persuasive. Whether you"re discussing problems and solutions, changes and benefits, or different options, dividing your content into three main parts makes it easier for your audience to follow and remember your ideas.

    Why Three Works

    • Memorable: People remember information in groups of three more easily than other numbers.
    • Balanced: Three parts feel complete — not too few, not too many.
    • Persuasive: Three-part structures are used in famous speeches and marketing campaigns worldwide.
    • Flexible: You can apply the "Power of Three" to almost any topic.

    The 3 Vs of Transition Signals

    Transition signals help your audience understand when you"re moving from one idea to the next. There are three types of transition signals:

    Verbal

    What the audience understands — words and phrases

    Vocal

    What the audience hears — tone, pace, pause

    Visual

    What the audience sees — slides, gestures, movement

    Explore Each Type

    Verbal Transition Signals

    Words and phrases that show the connection between ideas:

    • "First, let me..." / "Moving on to..." / "Now, let"s look at..."
    • "As a result..." / "This leads to..." / "For this reason..."
    • "In summary..." / "To conclude..." / "Finally..."
    Vocal Transition Signals

    How you deliver helps guide your audience:

    • Pause for a moment before introducing a new point
    • Lower your pitch slightly, then raise it for emphasis
    • Slow down when making an important point, speed up when moving forward
    • Change your energy level to signal a shift in topic
    Visual Transition Signals

    What your audience sees on screen and in your body language:

    • Move to a different position on stage
    • Point to a new slide or visual element
    • Use hand gestures to emphasise the transition
    • Change the slide design or colour to signal a new section

    Think About It

    Can you think of more examples of transition signals?

    Other examples include:

    • Verbal: "In contrast..." / "Similarly..." / "Here"s the evidence..." / "Let me explain..."
    • Vocal: Becoming more quiet or louder to emphasise; using pauses for effect
    • Visual: Making eye contact with different parts of the audience; using props; revealing information on the slide step-by-step

    Which might be more useful when delivering online?

    When delivering online, verbal and vocal transition signals become even more important because your audience cannot see your full body or movement across a stage. However, visual signals through slides, animations, and your facial expressions and gestures on camera remain powerful tools. You might rely slightly more on words and tone, but combining all three is still the key to engaging your online audience.

    Planning Your Presentation

    Now you will plan and deliver a presentation on "A Person That You Admire."

    Your Task

    1. Organise your ideas into key content areas.

      Think about what you want to share. Who is this person? Why do you admire them? What have they done that inspires you? What can others learn from them?

    2. Decide on a structure.

      Choose one of the structures you have learned: Problem → Solution → Outcome, Rationale → Change → Benefits, or Past → Present → Future. Which structure works best for your topic?

    3. Select verbal, visual, or vocal signals.

      Write down the transition phrases you will use, the vocal techniques you will apply, and the visual elements (slides, gestures) you will incorporate.

    4. Present your ideas.

      Deliver your presentation, using your plan to guide you.

    Planning Framework

    Step 1: Identify your key content areas

    Who is this person? What makes them remarkable? Why do you admire them? What impact have they had?

    Step 2: Choose your structure

    Click to see structure options
    Past → Present → Future: Their background, their achievements now, their vision

    Problem → Solution → Outcome: A challenge they faced, how they overcame it, the results

    Rationale → Change → Benefits: Why change was needed, what they changed, what improved

    Step 3: Plan your transition signals

    Verbal: "When they first started..." / "Now, in recent years..." / "In the future, I believe..."

    Vocal: Pause at the end of each section; vary your pace

    Visual: New slide for each section; show a photo or timeline
