The Defence and The Prosecution

The consulting industry has passionate defenders and fierce critics. Both have evidence. Your job is to understand both sides deeply before forming your own position.

Chunk 1: What Consulting Firms Say They Do

The Defence

"We solve problems companies can't solve alone."

Consulting firms argue they bring cross-industry expertise, global benchmarking data, and specialised talent that no single company could maintain in-house. McKinsey, for example, works with over 70 of the world's 100 largest companies annually.

They point to digital transformation, post-merger integration, and crisis management as areas where outside expertise genuinely creates value.

The Prosecution

"They repackage obvious advice at astronomical prices."

In 2024, New York City hired McKinsey to solve its rubbish collection problem. The result: an 80-page report recommending "containerisation" -- essentially, putting rubbish in bins. Cost to taxpayers: $4 million. That's roughly $42,000 per slide.

Critics argue consultants often tell companies what they already know, but executives use the consultant's brand name to justify decisions to boards.

Quick check: Before we go further -- which argument do you find more convincing so far, and why?

Chunk 2: The Ethics Question

The Defence

"Bad actors exist in every industry. The system self-corrects."

McKinsey paid $650 million for its opioid work and $1.1 billion in South Africa. KPMG fired its entire South African leadership. Bain apologised publicly. Defenders say these fines and reputational consequences show the system works -- firms get caught, they pay, they reform.

McKinsey has since banned all work involving controlled substances and hired a chief ethics officer.

The Prosecution

"The fines are pocket change. Nobody goes to prison."

McKinsey's $650 million fine came from a firm estimated to earn over $10 billion annually. The US Justice Department called it the first time a consulting firm was held criminally responsible for client advice -- but the charge was a misdemeanour, not a felony. Meanwhile, street-level drug dealers receive years in prison for far smaller roles in the same crisis.

In South Africa, McKinsey's former partner Vikas Sagar allegedly laundered millions through shell companies linked to a corrupt political family.

Quick check: Is a financial penalty enough to change corporate behaviour? Or does it just become "a cost of doing business"?

Chunk 3: The AI Disruption

The Defence

"AI makes us better, not obsolete."

Accenture booked $3 billion in generative AI contracts in 2024. Deloitte invested $1.4 billion in AI capabilities. McKinsey's AI platform "Lilli" now supports 70% of its proposal and presentation work. These firms aren't being disrupted -- they're selling the disruption.

The Prosecution

"AI exposes what consultants actually do."

In October 2025, Deloitte Australia was exposed for charging the federal government AUD $440,000 for a report largely written by AI tools. Both McKinsey and Deloitte cut more than 10% of their consulting workforce between 2024 and 2025. If AI can draft strategy decks and analyse data in minutes, what exactly are clients paying for?

Building a Complex Argument

Simple opinions are easy. Nuanced arguments are powerful. Let's work on building the kind of argument that acknowledges complexity.

I Do: Watch How It Works

Here are two ways someone might respond to the question: "Are consulting firms good for the world?" One is weak. One is strong. Read both, then we'll discuss why.

Response A

"I think consulting firms are bad because they charge too much money and they caused the opioid crisis. Companies should solve their own problems instead of paying millions to consultants."

Response B

"There's a reasonable case that consulting firms provide genuine value -- particularly in areas like digital transformation where companies may genuinely lack internal expertise. That said, the industry's track record raises serious questions about accountability. When McKinsey can advise Purdue Pharma on strategies that contributed to hundreds of thousands of deaths and face only a misdemeanour charge, it suggests the regulatory framework hasn't kept pace with the influence these firms wield. What's more troubling is that this isn't an isolated incident -- the South African state capture scandal and Deloitte's AI billing controversy point to systemic issues rather than individual failures."

Click to reveal

Response B does four things Response A doesn't:

1. Concedes the opposing view first ("There's a reasonable case that...") -- this shows intellectual honesty.

2. Uses specific evidence (McKinsey/Purdue, misdemeanour charge, South Africa, Deloitte) rather than vague claims.

3. Escalates from specific to systemic ("this isn't isolated... systemic issues rather than individual failures").

4. Hedges appropriately ("suggests," "raises questions") rather than making absolute claims.

Language for Complex Arguments

Notice how Response B uses specific structures to build sophistication. These aren't fixed phrases to memorise -- they're strategic moves:

Conceding before countering: "There's a reasonable case that... That said, ..." / "While it's true that... what's more troubling is ..."
Hedging certainty: "This suggests..." / "It raises questions about..." / "The evidence points to..."
Moving from specific to systemic: "This isn't an isolated case..." / "What we're seeing is a pattern of..." / "The common thread here is..."
Acknowledging nuance: "The picture is more complicated than..." / "It depends on whether you believe..." / "There's a tension between..."

We Do: Hypothesis Testing

Let's test some common claims about consulting. For each one, decide: is this a strong argument, a weak argument, or does it depend on context?

"Consulting firms are just paid to tell executives what they already know."
Discuss first, then click

It depends. Sometimes this is literally true -- a CEO uses McKinsey's name to justify a decision to the board. But in highly technical areas (AI implementation, post-merger integration, regulatory compliance across 50 countries), outside expertise often fills a genuine gap. The question is: how often is each scenario?

"If consulting firms were truly useful, companies wouldn't need to keep hiring them year after year."
Discuss first, then click

Interesting but flawed. You could make the same argument about doctors or lawyers. Complex organisations face changing problems. However, there IS a valid concern about "dependency" -- consulting firms have a financial incentive to create repeat business rather than genuinely solving problems. The South African case showed consultants "hollowing out" internal expertise, making the client MORE dependent.

"The $650 million McKinsey fine proves the system works."
Discuss first, then click

Weak, unless you define "works" very loosely. $650M from a firm earning $10B+ annually is 6-7%. The charge was a misdemeanour. No executive served prison time. Compare this to a street-level drug dealer facing years in prison for a far smaller role in the same crisis. The question isn't whether there was punishment -- it's whether the punishment matches the harm.
