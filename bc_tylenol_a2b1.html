Seven Deaths and a Hundred Million Dollars

In 1982 someone put poison in Tylenol medicine on shop shelves. It was not the company's fault. The company took back every bottle in America anyway.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then answer the questions before you read the next part.

Part 1 — Three terrible days

In 1982, Tylenol was the most popular painkiller in America.

More than a third of all painkillers sold in the country were Tylenol. The company that made it was Johnson & Johnson.

At the end of September 1982, seven people near Chicago died suddenly. It happened in about three days.

The police soon found the link. All seven people took Tylenol capsules just before they died. Somebody had put poison inside the capsules.

The poison did not come from the factory. Somebody took bottles from shop shelves. They opened the capsules, added poison, and put the bottles back.

In 1982, bottles had no seals. You could not see if somebody had opened one.

So it was not the company's fault. But the company's name was on every bottle. And nobody knew how many poisoned bottles there were.

True or false? Click your answer.

1. Tylenol was a small brand that few people used.

2. The poison was put in the capsules after they left the factory.

3. In 1982, you could easily see if someone had opened a bottle.

Talk about it
  • It was not the company's fault. Was it still the company's problem? Why?
  • Imagine you are the boss. It is day two. What do you do first?

Part 2 — Take everything back

As soon as the company understood the danger, it stopped all Tylenol advertising.

It opened phone lines for worried customers and doctors. It talked to journalists openly, every day.

Then it did something very big. It took Tylenol capsules back from every shop in America. This recall was about thirty-one million bottles.

The recall cost about one hundred million dollars.

Not everyone agreed with this decision. The deaths were only in one city. Some officials worried that other criminals would copy the crime.

Customers stopped buying Tylenol. Before the deaths, more than a third of painkillers were Tylenol. A few weeks later, it was less than one in ten.

Many experts said the brand was finished. They said nobody would ever trust the name again.

True or false? Click your answer.

1. The company stopped advertising Tylenol.

2. The company removed bottles only from shops in Chicago.

3. Everybody agreed that the national recall was a good idea.

Talk about it
  • The recall cost a hundred million dollars. Was it the right decision, or too much?
  • Why do you think some officials were worried about copy crimes?

Part 3 — A famous comeback, and hard questions

About six weeks after the deaths, Tylenol came back to the shops.

The new bottles had three seals: a glued box, plastic around the top, and foil under the cap. If someone opened a bottle, you could see it.

The company also gave people money-off coupons. It sent many workers to talk to doctors.

It worked. In about a year, Tylenol won back most of its customers. The brand recovered.

The police never found the killer. After 1982, most medicine bottles in the world started to use seals.

Business schools still teach this story as a perfect example. But the later story is not so simple.

Years later, around 2010, the company had problems in its own factories. It had to take back children's medicines. People said it forgot its own lesson.

In 2025 the US government said the medicine may be linked to autism if women take it in pregnancy. The company and many doctors said the science does not show this.

So here is the question. In 1982, was the company brave? Or was it easy, because the company was clearly the victim?

Time words — when, as soon as, before, after, until

This story happens very fast, so we need words that show when things happen.

when — at that time: I was at school when the phone rang.

as soon as — immediately when: Call me as soon as the train arrives.

before / after — earlier / later: Wash your hands before you eat.

until — up to that time, then it stops: We stayed at the party until midnight.

Careful: when we talk about the future, we do not use will after these words. I'll text you when I get home. (NOT when I will get)

WordMeaningExample
whenat that timeIt was raining when we left.
as soon asimmediately whenI'll start as soon as you are ready.
beforeearlier thanTurn off the lights before you go.
afterlater thanWe had coffee after the meeting.
untilup to that timeThe shop is open until nine.

Find the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Say the correct sentence out loud, then check.

1. I'll call you as soon as I will get home.

3. We waited outside until the shop open.

4. When I will see her tomorrow, I'll give her the book.

1. I'll call you as soon as I get home. (no will after time words)

2. Before you take (or taking) the medicine, read the label. (not before to)

3. We waited outside until the shop opened. (past story = past simple)

4. When I see her tomorrow, I'll give her the book. (no will after when)

5. After I finish (or finishing) work, I usually go to the gym. (add a subject, or use -ing)
