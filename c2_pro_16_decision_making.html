Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

C2 Level · Strategic Leadership

Two Systems of Thinking

Daniel Kahneman's research distinguishes between System 1 and System 2 thinking. Most decisions use System 1. But good decisions often require System 2.

System 1: Fast, Intuitive, Biased

System 1 thinking is automatic. It's pattern recognition. You see a situation and instantly categorise it based on past experience. You make gut decisions. This is useful for speed, but it's prone to bias.

Your gut tells you to hire the candidate who reminds you of you. Your gut says "expand into Asia" because a competitor did and it worked for them. Your gut says "cut costs" when revenue drops. System 1 is efficient, but it's often wrong.

System 2: Slow, Deliberate, Accurate

System 2 is effortful. It requires you to slow down, gather data, consider alternatives, and think critically. It's slower, but it's more accurate. Good decisions for high-stakes situations use System 2.

A good decision-making process forces System 2. It asks: "What are we assuming? What could prove us wrong? What are we not seeing?"

Cognitive Biases That Trap Leaders

  • Confirmation bias: You seek information that confirms what you already believe. You avoid information that challenges you.
  • Sunk cost fallacy: You keep investing in a failing project because you've already spent so much on it. The money is gone; it shouldn't influence the decision.
  • Availability heuristic: You assume the most memorable examples are the most common. One bad hire feels typical. One good decision feels like luck.
  • Anchoring: The first number you hear becomes your reference point. If someone says "we need $10 million," you negotiate down from there—even if the true budget should be $5 million.
  • Groupthink: A team converges on one view and stops questioning it. Dissent feels disloyal.

Pre-Mortem Analysis

A pre-mortem is a technique where you imagine it's 18 months from now and your decision failed catastrophically. Then you work backward. "Why did this fail?" The answers reveal risks you weren't seeing.

Why is this powerful? It bypasses optimism bias. In a normal meeting, everyone focuses on why something will work. A pre-mortem gives permission to focus on why it might not.

Reflection: Think of a decision you made recently that you're now questioning. What information were you not seeing? What bias might have influenced you?

Second-Order Thinking in Practice

Most people think one level deep. "If we cut costs, we'll improve profitability." True. But what's the second-order effect? Cut costs in customer service, and response times slow. Response times slow, and customer retention drops. Customer retention drops, and long-term revenue falls more than you saved. So the first-order effect (cost reduction) is negative when you think about second-order effects.

Second-order thinking asks: "Then what?" "If we open a factory in this country, that's cheaper. Then what? Currency risk. Then what? Geopolitical risk. Then what? Supply chain vulnerability." Good decision-makers follow the chain further than others.

The Case Study: Strategic Crossroads

Your company is a profitable, growing software company with £100M revenue. The market is cooling. Your Board is debating three strategies for the next 3 years. You have 10 minutes to present your analysis and recommendation.

10:00

10:00

The Three Options

Option A (Conservative): Maintain current business. Invest in product stability and customer retention. Expect 5-8% annual growth.

Option B (Aggressive): Acquire a competitor in a new vertical. Spend £50M. Potential for 20%+ growth if integration works. Risk: integration failure, debt burden.

Option C (Pivot): Shift from product sales to services. Lower margin but more stable revenue. Better for recession. Takes 2 years to shift. Risk: losing existing product revenue during transition.

Your Analysis: Option A

Start with the conservative option. What are its strengths? What are its risks? What assumptions are you making?

Framework to consider:
Strengths: Low risk, predictable, maintains team stability, sustainable.
Risks: Competitors grow faster. Market opportunity passes. Shareholders expect higher growth. We atrophy.
Assumptions: That 5-8% growth satisfies our investors. That we can defend our position without growth. That the market will remain stable enough for this strategy.
Second-order effects: Slow growth means losing talent to faster-growing competitors. Losing talent reduces innovation. Eventually we're disrupted by someone younger and faster.

Your Analysis: Option B

Now the aggressive option. What's attractive? What could go wrong? Use pre-mortem thinking.

Framework to consider:
Attractive: Growth, scale, market position, synergies.
Risks (pre-mortem): "It's 2027 and the acquisition failed. Why?" Integration was harder than expected. Cultural clash. Customers of the acquired company left. We overpaid. Management distraction. Market downturn hit before we could realise synergies. Key talent left.
Assumptions: That integration is manageable. That synergies will materialise. That markets stay favourable for 2 years.
Second-order effects: Taking on £50M debt increases financial risk. If revenues drop, we're in trouble. Debt also limits future flexibility—we can't acquire again if a better opportunity appears.

Your Recommendation

You've analysed all three. Now recommend one. But don't just say what's best—explain your reasoning, acknowledge the risks, and address the assumptions you're making.

Example recommendation: "I recommend Option C with a caveat. Here's my thinking: Option A is safe, but in a cooling market, 'steady' becomes 'slow.' We'll lose momentum and talent. Option B is attractive for growth, but we're vulnerable to a market downturn, and integration risk is real. Option C is harder but more strategic. Services revenue is more resilient. The 2-year transition is expensive, but if we're going to do this, the next two years are the time—we have cushion in our current profits to invest.

Here's what could go wrong: We lose product customers during transition faster than we gain services revenue. We become stuck in the middle—not enough product strength, not enough services scale. The market keeps growing and we miss it because we're in transition.

So my actual recommendation is a hybrid: Invest 60% in services transition, 40% in maintaining and incrementally growing the product. This keeps optionality. If the services shift works, we have a more stable company. If it doesn't, we still have a growing core business. We're not all-in on any bet."

Why this works: It's not a simple answer. It acknowledges risks. It shows second-order thinking. It doesn't pretend certainty. It keeps the company flexible.

Defend Against Pushback

A board member challenges you: "This hybrid approach is wishy-washy. We need to commit." How do you respond?

Example response: "I understand the appeal of commitment. But optionality is not weakness—it's prudence in uncertainty. We don't know which option will work best. A hybrid approach lets us learn. In year one, we'll see whether services customers are converting. Whether product customers are staying. Whether our teams can execute both. By year two, we'll have real data. Then we can commit fully to what's working. That's not wishy-washy. That's disciplined decision-making under uncertainty."

Reversible vs. Irreversible Decisions

Jeff Bezos distinguishes between Type 1 decisions (irreversible) and Type 2 decisions (reversible). Most decisions are Type 2. You can undo them. You should make Type 2 decisions fast, with incomplete information.

Type 1 decisions are rare. They require more deliberation, more data, more questioning. The mistake is treating Type 2 decisions as Type 1. You delay, you over-analyse, you miss the opportunity.

When to Trust Your Gut

Trust your gut when: You have deep expertise. You're facing a decision similar to ones you've made before. The stakes are low or medium. You need speed.

Don't trust your gut when: It's a domain you're new to. The stakes are high. You're under stress (stress increases bias). You're emotionally invested in one outcome.

Decision Fatigue

The research is clear: the more decisions you make, the worse they get. By afternoon, you're depleted. You make sloppy choices. You're more likely to stick with the default. Leaders who make big decisions early in the day do better.

This is why systems matter. Don't make big decisions ad hoc. Have a process. Have decision criteria. Have someone who plays devil's advocate. Have a pre-mortem before finalising. These systems reduce the cognitive load and improve the decision.

Reflection: Think of your best decision in the last year. Did you use System 2 thinking? Did you challenge your own assumptions? What would have happened if you'd trusted your gut instead?
