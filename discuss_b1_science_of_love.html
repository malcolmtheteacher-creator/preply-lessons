II. Your Brain on Love

Anthropologist Helen Fisher spent 30 years studying love using brain scans. She discovered that romantic love isn't an emotion — it's a drive, as fundamental as hunger or thirst. And it follows a predictable chemical sequence.

The Three Stages

🔥
Stage 1: LUST
Driven by testosterone & oestrogen. Pure physical attraction. Evolved to motivate us to seek partners.
🥰
Stage 2: ATTRACTION
Driven by dopamine, norepinephrine & serotonin. Obsession, euphoria, can't stop thinking about them. Lasts 1–3 years.
🤝
Stage 3: ATTACHMENT
Driven by oxytocin & vasopressin. Deep bonding, comfort, security. Can last a lifetime.

Why does this matter?

Many relationships fail at the transition between Stage 2 and Stage 3. The dopamine rush of early romance fades — and people think they've "fallen out of love." But Fisher's research suggests they've simply moved from attraction to attachment — a deeper, quieter, but equally powerful form of love.

III. What the Research Says

Scientists have spent decades trying to understand love. Here are three of the most fascinating findings.

🧠 Arthur Aron's 36 Questions (1997)

Psychologist Arthur Aron created a list of 36 increasingly personal questions. Two strangers ask each other these questions, then stare into each other's eyes for 4 minutes. In Aron's study, several pairs reported feeling deeply connected — and one pair married six months later.

The principle: vulnerability + reciprocal self-disclosure = intimacy. Love isn't just chemistry — it can be built through deliberate emotional openness.

🧠 John Gottman's "Magic Ratio" (1994)

Psychologist John Gottman filmed thousands of couples and predicted with 94% accuracy which would divorce. His secret? The ratio of positive to negative interactions. Couples who lasted had a ratio of at least 5:1 — five positive moments for every negative one. Couples below that ratio almost always failed.

The principle: Love is maintained by small, daily acts of kindness — not grand gestures.

🧠 Helen Fisher's Brain Scans (2005)

Fisher put people who said they were "madly in love" into MRI scanners. The same brain region that activates for cocaine addiction lit up when they saw photos of their beloved. Love literally uses the same neural circuits as addiction. This is why heartbreak feels like withdrawal — because neurologically, it is.

IV. The Big Questions

DISCUSSION TIME

Now that we know the science, let's wrestle with the questions it raises. Pick 2–3 cards and discuss them with your teacher.

🤔

Is love just chemistry?

If we can explain love through dopamine and oxytocin, does that make it less meaningful? Or does understanding the science make it more amazing?

💔

Can you choose who you love?

Arranged marriages have higher satisfaction rates than love marriages in some studies. Does this mean love is a decision, not just a feeling?

🌎

Is romantic love universal?

86% of cultures have romantic love — but 14% don't. Some cultures see passionate love as a kind of illness. Is our idea of romance just a Western invention?

💊

Would you take a love pill?

Scientists are exploring whether oxytocin sprays could strengthen relationships. If a pill could make you fall in love — or fall out of love — would you take it? Should it exist?

📱

Are dating apps killing love?

Unlimited choice. Swipe culture. The "paradox of choice." Is technology helping us find love or making it harder to commit?

⏰

Does love have an expiry date?

If the chemical "in love" phase only lasts 1–3 years, what keeps couples together for 50? Is long-term love the same thing as early love — or something completely different?
