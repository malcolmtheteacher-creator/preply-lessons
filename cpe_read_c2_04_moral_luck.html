The Problem of Moral Luck

Thomas Nagel's seminal essay on moral luck exposes a fundamental tension within consequentialist ethical frameworks: we are inclined to hold people morally responsible for outcomes that lie partially or wholly beyond their volitional control. Consider the paradigmatic case: two drivers, identically reckless and inattentive, navigate residential neighborhoods at speeds that contravene traffic ordinances. One driver's child darts into the street; swerving desperately, the driver narrowly averts catastrophe. The second driver encounters no obstacle, proceeds unimpeded, arrives home safely. Both drivers have acted identically, evincing identical moral turpitude—yet our intuitive judgment assigns drastically different moral culpability. The first driver, rescued by contingency, faces potential accusations of negligence and recklessness; the second driver receives no opprobrium. Only fortune distinguishes them.

This phenomenon constitutes what Nagel calls "resultant luck"—the degree to which outcomes depend upon factors beyond the agent's control. Yet resultant luck is merely one permutation of a broader category. Circumstantial luck determines what situations individuals encounter; a person born into abject poverty faces moral temptations vastly distinct from those confronting the privileged. Causal luck governs the origins of one's dispositions: was the prospective criminal constituted with an inclination toward violence through genetic inheritance, neurochemical abnormality, or childhood trauma? Constitutive luck underlies the fundamental question: what accounts for the type of person one is? These four categories of moral luck collectively undermine the assumption that moral responsibility correlates exclusively with conscious intention and volitional capacity.

The implications become vertiginous when confronting criminal justice systems predicated upon retributive principles. If a person commits an identical act but circumstantial luck determines whether that act causes grievous harm, should sentencing disparities reflect the luck of outcome? The retributivist position, which grounds punishment in the wrongfulness of the act itself rather than its consequences, seems morally defensible—yet it conflicts with the intuitive perception that outcomes matter. Simultaneously, consequentialist frameworks that appraise moral culpability by actual consequences become ethically troubling: they allocate blame for factors beyond volitional control, violating the foundational principle that "ought implies can."

Bernard Williams and Nagel propose that moral luck may be an ineliminable feature of the human condition rather than an anomaly to be resolved. If we acknowledge that our moral appraisals systematically incorporate luck—that we cannot fully excise contingency from ethical judgment—we must confront the humbling recognition that morality itself is neither purely rational nor fully objective. Our condemnation of the person whose recklessness produced a fatality carries more force than condemnation of one whose recklessness produced nothing, not because the latent wrongness differs, but because luck has transformed our emotional and rational responses. To deny this phenomenon is to deny the texture of moral reality itself.

Multiple Choice Questions

Question 1

The hypothetical case of two reckless drivers is presented to illustrate that:

A) bad drivers should always be held equally responsible

B) moral judgment often diverges from intention when outcomes differ despite identical actions

C) traffic laws are ineffective at preventing accidents

D) children should not play in residential neighborhoods

Correct Answer: D — The passage explains that two drivers act identically but face different moral appraisals because one causes harm by accident while the other causes none. This illustrates resultant luck.

Question 2

The distinction between resultant luck and circumstantial luck is that:

A) resultant luck affects outcomes while circumstantial luck affects the situations encountered

B) they are essentially identical concepts with different names

C) circumstantial luck is impossible to verify scientifically

D) resultant luck applies only to crimes

Correct Answer: B — The passage defines resultant luck as affecting outcomes and circumstantial luck as determining what situations are encountered. These are distinct categories within the framework.

Question 3

The phrase "ought implies can" suggests that:

A) people can achieve anything they set their minds to

B) moral responsibility requires causal control; we can only be blamed for what we could control

C) consequences determine moral worth

D) intention alone suffices for moral judgment

Correct Answer: C — The passage uses this principle to critique consequentialist frameworks that allocate blame for uncontrollable factors. It means we can only be obligated to do what we are capable of doing.

Question 4

According to the passage, retributive justice frameworks are morally defensible because they:

A) focus on outcomes rather than intentions

B) ground punishment in the wrongfulness of the act itself, not lucky consequences

C) eliminate all forms of moral luck

D) prioritize rehabilitation over punishment

Correct Answer: A — The passage states that retributivism grounds punishment in the act itself rather than consequences, which seems defensible. However, the passage suggests it conflicts with intuitions about outcomes mattering.

Question 5

The passage implies that Williams and Nagel believe moral luck is:

A) a problem that can be solved through better ethical theories

B) an intrinsic feature of human moral experience that cannot be eliminated

C) relevant only to criminal justice, not personal morality

D) less significant than philosophical theory suggests

Correct Answer: B — The passage states: "moral luck may be an ineliminable feature of the human condition rather than an anomaly to be resolved." This suggests it cannot be eliminated but must be acknowledged.

Question 6

The passage concludes that acknowledging moral luck requires recognizing that:

A) morality is purely objective and rational

B) emotions have no role in moral judgment

C) morality incorporates contingency and is not purely rational or objective

D) criminal justice systems are fundamentally flawed

Correct Answer: B — The final paragraph concludes: "we must confront the humbling recognition that morality itself is neither purely rational nor fully objective...luck has transformed our emotional and rational responses."

Discussion & Extension

Question 1: Sentencing and Fairness

Should courts sentence two identical crimes differently if one caused a fatality and the other caused no harm? How does acknowledging moral luck change your approach to sentencing policy?

Question 2: Personal Responsibility

To what extent do you feel responsible for your accomplishments versus your luck (family wealth, education, talent, geographic location)? How does this reframe your sense of achievement or failure?

Question 3: Empathy and Judgment

If we acknowledge that luck heavily influences outcomes, should this make us more forgiving of others' failures and less prideful about our successes?

Question 4: Systemic Justice

How should awareness of moral luck inform policy on wealth redistribution, reparations, or criminal justice reform?

Speaking/Writing Task

Option A (Speaking): Argue for or against the proposition: "Criminal sentencing should be identical regardless of actual harm caused, focusing only on the defendant's intention and action, not luck-dependent outcomes."

Option B (Writing): Write a 400-word essay examining a real or hypothetical case where moral luck creates a tension between intuitive fairness and rational principle. Propose how the justice system should handle it.
