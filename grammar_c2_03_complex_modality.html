Turning It Up or Down: "Might Well" / "Could Hardly" / "Can't Possibly"

A small adverb next to a modal verb turns the certainty up or right down.

Spot the pattern

Three reactions you might hear in conversation:

  • "She might well take the job — it's exactly what she's been looking for."
  • "It could hardly be an accident that he turned up right when the topic came up."
  • "I can't possibly finish all of this before Friday."

Before you check: where does the extra word sit, and what job is it doing in each one?

Check the pattern CLICK
The adverb sits right after the modal, before the verb. "Well" turns the likelihood up — more confident than plain "might". "Hardly" and "possibly" (with a negative modal) turn it right down — close to disbelief or flat impossibility.

There's a fourth member of this family worth knowing: "just", which adds a note of hope to a slim chance — "It might just work if we're lucky" sounds very different from the confident "might well".
might / may / could + well / just + verb → sharper likelihood
could + hardly + verb → strong doubt
can't / couldn't + possibly + verb → rules it out completely

From flat to precise

"I'm sure that's not really a coincidence."
→
"That could hardly be a coincidence."

One tight little phrase does the work of the whole first sentence — and it's exactly how people actually talk.

How Sure, Exactly? Bound to, Sure to, Likely to, Liable to

Four ways to place something on a scale from "certain" to "just a tendency".

Spot the pattern

Three predictions:

  • "The trains are always delayed on a Friday — it's bound to happen again this week."
  • "Prices are likely to go up again before the year's out."
  • "He's liable to lose his temper if the football's on and his team's losing."

What comes after each of these words — a verb straight away, or something else first?

Check the pattern CLICK
be + bound to / likely to / liable to + verb — these aren't true modal verbs, they're adjectives followed by "to" and the bare verb. "Bound to" (and its close cousin "sure to") mean near-certain. "Likely to" is the neutral middle — probable, not guaranteed. "Liable to" describes a tendency, often something a bit unwelcome, based on character or habit.
be + bound to / sure to + verb → near-certain
be + likely to + verb → probable
be + liable to + verb → a tendency (often unwanted)

From flat to precise

"It will definitely rain later — look at those clouds."
→
"It's bound to rain later."

Softening it in conversation

Sometimes you want to sound less definite than a flat "I think". Conversation has softer ways of doing it too:

A: Do you think the flight will be delayed?

B: I'd have thought so, with this fog — but you never know.

A: The new manager seems really strict.

B: You'd imagine she'd ease off once she's settled in.

A: Was it wrong of me to leave the meeting early?

B: It could be argued you were right to — it had basically finished anyway.

What's happening here? CLICK
"I'd have thought", "you'd imagine" and "it could be argued" all soften a claim without sounding evasive — they sound like someone genuinely weighing it up, not dodging the question. Notice "it could be argued" sounds completely natural said out loud here — very different from starting a written essay with it.

Getting the Past Right: Needn't Have, Didn't Need to, Was to Have

The past deserves the same precision — wasted effort, plain necessity, and plans that fell through.

Spot the pattern

  • "I needn't have brought an umbrella — it didn't rain at all."
  • "In the end I didn't need to bring my laptop, so I left it at home."
  • "The plumber was to have come at nine, but he never turned up."

In the first sentence, did the umbrella get brought or not? What about the laptop, in the second? Think it through before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
needn't have + past participle: you DID it, and only afterwards did it turn out to be unnecessary — a bit of wasted effort.
didn't need to + verb: simply states something wasn't necessary — here, paired with "so I left it at home", the laptop stayed put.
was / were to have + past participle: something was arranged or expected to happen, but it didn't.
needn't have + past participle → done, but unnecessary (wasted effort)
didn't need to + verb → wasn't necessary
was / were to have + past participle → arranged, but never happened

Two more to keep straight

"He must have missed the bus." — a confident guess, working out why he's late from the evidence.

"He had to walk because he missed the bus." — a plain fact about the past, not a guess.
