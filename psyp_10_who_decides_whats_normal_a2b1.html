The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — Eight people go to hospital

In 1973, a psychologist called David Rosenhan published a study with the title 'On being sane in insane places'. Eight healthy people, including Rosenhan, tried to enter psychiatric hospitals in the USA. He called them 'pseudopatients'.

Each person said the same thing at the door: 'I hear a voice. It says empty, hollow, thud.' Everything else they said was true. After they were admitted, they said, 'I feel fine now. I don't hear the voice.' and acted normally.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many people went to hospitals?

2. What did they say at the door?

1. Eight.

2. 'I hear a voice. It says empty, hollow, thud.'

Discuss:
  • What do you think happened next?

Part 2 — What happened

All eight people were admitted to hospital. Seven were told they had schizophrenia. They stayed in hospital for between 7 and 52 days, on average 19 days. When they left, the doctors wrote 'schizophrenia in remission', not 'healthy'.

The staff did not say the people were healthy. But many of the real patients said, 'You're not crazy. You're checking up on the hospital.' Rosenhan wrote that the staff noticed ordinary things in a different way. For example, one nurse wrote that a pseudopatient who took notes was 'engaging in writing behaviour'.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many were told they had schizophrenia?

2. How long did they stay?

1. Seven.

2. Between 7 and 52 days; on average 19 days.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think the real patients noticed, but the staff did not?

Part 3 — A second look

In a second part of the study, a hospital heard about the first study. The hospital said, 'This cannot happen here.' Rosenhan told them that one or more pseudopatients would try to enter in the next three months. The staff looked at 193 new patients. They said 41 were probably pretending. In fact, Rosenhan did not send anyone.

Years later, a journalist called Susannah Cahalan looked at Rosenhan's papers. She said that some of his data did not match his story, and that he may have changed some details. So today many people say: the main idea is important, but the study is not as strong as it seemed. The study also helped to change how psychiatry made diagnoses, and the next big manual of diagnosis, in 1980, used clearer rules.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in the second study?

2. What did Cahalan say?

1. The staff said 41 of 193 new patients were probably pretending, but Rosenhan sent nobody.

2. That some of the data did not match his story.

Discuss:
  • Does it matter if a famous study is not perfect? What do we keep?

Grammar — reported speech

Use reported speech to say what someone said. The verb usually goes one step back in time.

Changes:
• present simple → past simple: 'I hear a voice.' → He said (that) he heard a voice.
• present continuous → past continuous: 'I'm feeling fine.' → She said she was feeling fine.
• will → would, can → could: 'I can leave.' → He said he could leave.
• Past simple → past perfect: 'I saw the doctor.' → She said she had seen the doctor.
Also change: I → he/she, my → his/her, here → there, today → that day.
For questions: 'Where are you from?' → He asked where I was from. (no question word order!)

Fact or label?

Five situations. Say whether it is a fact or an interpretation, and why. Then reveal an idea.

1. A nurse writes: 'Patient is engaging in writing behaviour.' What is this?
Idea. It is a fact (he writes) with a label (behaviour) that makes it sound like a symptom.
2. A doctor reads in the notes that a person once had depression. How can this change the next meeting?
Idea. The doctor may see everything through the old label.
3. A person says she hears a voice, but is not worried. Is it always a problem?
Idea. Not always. Some people hear voices and live well. Distress and daily life are important.
4. A client asks: 'Do I have a disorder?' What do you say?
Idea. Explain what the label means and does not mean, and what it is useful for.
5. In 1973, an association removed homosexuality from its list of mental illnesses. What does this tell us?
Idea. That diagnoses change with time, evidence and society. Ask: who decides?
