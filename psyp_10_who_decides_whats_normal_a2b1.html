Healthy people in hospital: real or made up?

In 1973, a psychologist called David Rosenhan did a very surprising study. Here are six things about it. Some are real and some are made up for this game. Which are real?

1. The doctors always noticed that the eight people were healthy.
Made up. The doctors did not notice.
2. After they were admitted, they said they felt fine and behaved normally.
Real. They acted like ordinary people.
3. The hospitals said "You are healthy" and sent them home.
Made up. All eight were admitted to hospital.
4. They stayed in hospital for only one day.
Made up. They stayed between 7 and 52 days.
5. Some real patients said, "You are not ill."
Real. The patients noticed more than the doctors did.
6. Eight healthy people went to hospital and said they heard a voice.
Real. They said the voice said one word, for example 'empty'.
Talk about it:
  • Why did the doctors not notice?
  • How can a label, like a diagnosis, change how people see someone?

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — Eight people go to hospital

Before you read: Healthy people say they hear a voice. They are taken into hospital. After that they act normally. What will the doctors think?

In 1973, a psychologist called David Rosenhan published a study with the title 'On being sane in insane places'. Eight healthy people, including Rosenhan, tried to enter psychiatric hospitals in the USA. He called them 'pseudopatients'.

Each person said the same thing at the door: 'I hear a voice. It says empty, hollow, thud.' Everything else they said was true. After they were admitted, they said, 'I feel fine now. I don't hear the voice.' and acted normally.

Which word means 'a person who pretends to be ill'? (p_______)
pretend (v) — to act as if something is true when it is not. A pseudopatient pretends to be a patient.
The staff did not notice that the people were ______ . (h______)
healthy (adj) — the opposite is ill or unwell. Example: "They were healthy people."

Part 2 — What happened

Before you read: One nurse wrote: "Patient is engaging in writing behaviour." What was the person doing, and what did the note make it sound like?

All eight people were admitted to hospital. Seven were told they had schizophrenia. They stayed in hospital for between 7 and 52 days, on average 19 days. When they left, the doctors wrote 'schizophrenia in remission', not 'healthy'.

The staff did not say the people were healthy. But many of the real patients said, 'You're not crazy. You're checking up on the hospital.' Rosenhan wrote that the staff noticed ordinary things in a different way. For example, one nurse wrote that a pseudopatient who took notes was 'engaging in writing behaviour'.

The doctor gave her a diagnosis: depression.

He was discharged from the hospital after three weeks.
What does discharged mean? (a) allowed to leave   (b) moved to another room   (c) given medicine
discharge (v) — answer (a). To let a patient leave the hospital.

Part 3 — A second look

Before you read: A hospital hears about the study and says, "This cannot happen here." What could the researcher do to test it?

In a second part of the study, a hospital heard about the first study. The hospital said, 'This cannot happen here.' Rosenhan told them that one or more pseudopatients would try to enter in the next three months. The staff looked at 193 new patients. They said 41 were probably pretending. In fact, Rosenhan did not send anyone.

Years later, a journalist called Susannah Cahalan looked at Rosenhan's papers. She said that some of his data did not match his story, and that he may have changed some details. So today many people say: the main idea is important, but the study is not as strong as it seemed. The study also helped to change how psychiatry made diagnoses, and the next big manual of diagnosis, in 1980, used clearer rules.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

Which word means 'a name or description that you give to a person'? (l____)
label (n) — Example: "A label can stay with a person for years."
Which word means 'normal'? (o_______)
ordinary (adj) — Example: "She did ordinary things: she wrote notes and ate lunch."
Your call:
  • What should a doctor ask before giving a label?

Reported speech

Use reported speech to say what someone said. The tense usually goes one step back.

Changes:
• present simple → past simple: 'I hear a voice.' → He said (that) he heard a voice.
• present continuous → past continuous: 'I'm feeling fine.' → She said she was feeling fine.
• will → would, can → could: 'I can leave.' → He said he could leave.
• Past simple → past perfect: 'I saw the doctor.' → She said she had seen the doctor.
Also change: I → he/she, my → his/her, here → there, today → that day.
For questions: 'Where are you from?' → He asked where I was from. (no question word order!)

Join the two facts so they mean the same as the model will show. Type your sentence, say it, then reveal.

3. "Where do you live?" the doctor asked. Report it. The doctor asked…

Fact or label?

Five situations. Say whether each sentence is a fact or a label, then report what the person said using reported speech. Then check an idea.

1. A nurse writes: 'Patient is engaging in writing behaviour.' What is this?said that
Idea. It is a fact (he writes) with a label (behaviour) that makes it sound like a symptom.
2. A doctor reads in the notes that a person once had depression. How can this change the next meeting?asked if
Idea. The doctor may see everything through the old label.
3. A person says she hears a voice, but is not worried. Is it always a problem?told me that
Idea. Not always. Some people hear voices and live well. Distress and daily life are important.
4. A client asks: 'Do I have a disorder?' What do you say?said that
Idea. Explain what the label means and does not mean, and what it is useful for.
5. In 1973, an association removed homosexuality from its list of mental illnesses. What does this tell us?asked if
Idea. That diagnoses change with time, evidence and society. Ask: who decides?
