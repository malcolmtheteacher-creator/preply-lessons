The Signal-Man

Charles Dickens (1866) — a lonely man beside a dark tunnel is haunted by a warning he cannot understand

Easier version available: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in three parts

Dickens tells this ghost story in the half-light of a railway cutting. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The ending arrives quietly, in a handful of words — do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The cutting and the man below

"Halloa! Below there!" When the man on the line below heard the voice calling down to him, he was standing at the door of his box, with a flag in his hand. One would have thought, considering how steep and narrow the path was, that he could not have mistaken where the voice came from; but instead of looking up to where I stood on the top of the deep cutting, he turned himself about and looked down the line. There was something remarkable in his manner of doing so, though I could not have said for my life what it was.

The cutting was extremely deep, and unusually precipitate. It was made through a clammy stone that became oozier and wetter as I went down. For these reasons I found the way long enough to give me time to recall the singular air with which he had looked down the line.

His post was as solitary and dismal a place as ever I saw. On either side, a dripping-wet wall of jagged stone, shutting out all view but a strip of sky; the perspective one way only a crooked prolongation of this great dungeon; the shorter perspective in the other direction terminating in a gloomy red light, and the gloomier entrance to a black tunnel, in whose massive architecture there was a barbarous, depressing, and forbidding air. So little sunlight ever found its way to this spot that it had an earthy, deadly smell; and so much cold wind rushed through it that it struck chill to me, as if I had left the natural world.

He was a dark, sallow man, with a dark beard and rather heavy eyebrows. He directed a most curious look towards the red light near the tunnel's mouth, and looked all about it, as if something were missing from it, and then looked at me.

"That light was part of his charge? Was it not?"

He answered in a low voice that it was. He had been there a long time, he said, and the place had grown upon him. He had learned the work without much help; he had taught himself a language, if only to know it by sight, and to have a crude idea of its pronunciation. He had been, when young, a student of natural philosophy — but he had run wild, misused his opportunities, gone down, and never risen again. He had no complaint to make of that. He had made his bed, and he lay upon it.

Pause and discuss:
  • Dickens spends a whole paragraph on the cutting before we meet the man properly. What is that doing to you as a reader?
  • The signalman looks down the line, not up at the voice. Why might that small detail matter later?
  • "He had made his bed, and he lay upon it." What kind of man does this make him sound like?

Part 2 — The figure by the red light

When I came down again the following evening, he was waiting for me at the bottom, with his lighted lamp in his hand. He was troubled, and he told me at last what weighed upon him. He had been visited, he said, by something he could not name.

"One moonlight night, I was sitting here, when I heard a voice cry, 'Halloa! Below there!' I started up, looked from that door, and saw some one standing by the red light near the tunnel, waving. The left arm was across the face, and the right arm was waved — violently waved. 'Clear the way!' it seemed to say."

He had run towards the figure, he told me, but it was gone. He had run on into the tunnel, five hundred yards, calling out, with his own lamp lifted high; but there was nothing — no one. He had telegraphed both ways: An alarm has been given. Is anything wrong? The answer came back, both ways: All well.

Within six hours of the appearance, he said, the memorable accident on that line had happened, and within ten hours the dead and wounded had been brought along through the tunnel, over the spot where the figure had stood. He shuddered as he told me this, and I felt the cold of the place settle on me.

"That was a year ago. Six or seven months passed, and I had recovered from the surprise, when one morning, as the day was breaking, I, standing at the door, looked towards the red light, and saw the spectre again."

This time, he said, the figure had not cried out. It had stood by the light, both hands before the face, in an attitude of mourning. And that very day, as a train came out of the tunnel, he had seen at one of the carriage windows what looked like a confusion of hands and heads, and something waved. He had shown the danger-light; the train had been stopped; and a beautiful young lady had died inside, instantly, and had been brought into his box, and laid down on the floor between us where we were standing.

Now, he told me, the spectre had come back, a week ago. It was there, at intervals, by the red light. He had seen it again and again, the left arm across the face, the right arm waving, and the cry — though no sound reached him — "For God's sake, clear the way!" It gave him no peace. It rang his little bell in a way that no living hand could ring it. And it would not tell him what was wrong, nor where, nor when.

"What is the danger? Where is the danger? There is danger overhanging somewhere on the Line. Some dreadful calamity will happen. After what I have seen, I cannot doubt it. But surely it is a cruel haunting of me. What can I do? I cannot send warning up or down the line that there is danger, for I should be discharged. They would say I was mad."

Pause and discuss:
  • Twice now the figure has appeared, and twice a disaster has followed. Is the signalman being warned, or simply being tormented?
  • What is so unbearable about a warning that won't say what, where, or when?
  • He fears that if he reports the danger, he will be called mad and dismissed. How does that trap make his situation worse?

Part 3 — The next evening

I left him that night uneasy. I did not like the way the place had worked upon him, nor the way it now worked upon me. I resolved to return the next evening, and to find some means of helping a man so troubled, so faithful to his lonely post, so plainly sane in every matter but this one.

When I came to the top of the cutting the following evening, the sun had not yet quite gone down. I saw, at the bottom, near the mouth of the tunnel, the appearance of a man, with his left sleeve across his eyes, passionately waving his right arm. For an instant my blood ran cold; then I saw that this was no apparition, but a small group of men, and that the figure of warning was one of them.

Something had happened. There had been an accident on the line. The signalman was dead.

He had been cut down by an engine that morning, as he stood with his back to it in the mouth of the tunnel. He had been looking at the red light, they said. The day was bright, the line was clear, and yet he had not moved.

I asked the engine-driver to tell me how it had happened. He was much shaken, and he told it in broken words.

"I saw him at the end of the tunnel, as if through a glass. There was no time to check speed, and I knew him to be very careful. As he did not seem to take heed of the whistle, I shut it off when we were running down upon him, and called to him as loud as I could call."

"What did you say?"

"I said, 'Below there! Look out! Look out! For God's sake, clear the way!'"

I started.

"Ah! it was a dreadful time, sir. I never left off calling to him. I put this arm before my eyes not to see, and I waved this arm to the last; but it was no use."

And as the driver said the words, and laid his left sleeve across his eyes, and waved his right arm, I understood. The gesture was the gesture of the spectre. The words were the words the signalman had heard, with no living mouth to speak them: "For God's sake, clear the way!"

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Go back over the story. The warning was real all along — but who was it for? Could the signalman ever have used it?
  • The driver's words and gesture were exactly the spectre's. What does Dickens leave us to feel, rather than explaining?
  • Dickens never tells us what the figure was. Why is the story stronger for that silence?
  • Your turn: Tell me about a time you sensed something was wrong but could not say what — and what happened next.

How Dickens builds the fear — three machines under the surface

The horror of this story does not come from blood. It comes from atmosphere, from what we know that the characters do not, and from a meaning held back until the very end.

The grammar this story teaches itself

A ghost story about earlier events warning of later ones is the perfect place to practise the past perfect — the tense English uses to put one past event clearly before another.

The rule in one line

When you tell a story in the past simple and need to step back to something that happened earlier still, use the past perfect: had + past participle. It says "this came first."

Signposting words make the order clear: before, after, by the time, already, when. Compare: "When the train arrived, the figure disappeared" (one after the other) with "When the train arrived, the figure had already disappeared" (it was gone first).

1. Past perfect vs past simple — choose the form

Put each verb in the past simple or the past perfect, depending on which event came first.

1. The figure (appear) before the accident (happen).

2. By the time the narrator returned, the signalman (lose) his life.

3. The signalman ran into the tunnel, but the spectre (vanish) already.

4. After the young lady (die), the train (continue) on its way.

1. had appeared / happened (the appearance came first; the accident followed)

2. had lost ("by the time" + the earlier completed event)

3. had vanished ("already" + the earlier event)

4. had died / continued (the death first; the train moved on after)

2. Reporting what the signalman had seen

When we report what someone said about their own past, the past simple shifts back to the past perfect.

1. He told the narrator that he (see) the figure twice.

2. The signalman explained that he (run) into the tunnel but (find) no one.

3. The driver said that he (call) out as loudly as he could.

1. had seen

2. had run … had found

3. had called

3. Sequencing with before, after, by the time

Join the two events into one sentence, keeping the order clear with the past perfect.

1. Each time the spectre (come), a misfortune followed soon after.

2. By the time the narrator (reach) the cutting, a small group (gather) there.

3. The narrator only understood the warning after the driver (repeat) the very words and the gesture.

1. came (or "had come"; both read naturally with the repeated pattern, but the present focus is the past simple of habit)

2. reached / had gathered (the gathering was complete before he reached the top)

3. had repeated (the repetition came first; understanding followed)

Note: Use the past perfect for the earlier of two past events. The later event stays in the past simple. The clue words before / after / by the time / already tell you which event came first.

What Dickens does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are the reason this story still raises the hairs on your neck in 2026.

1. Gothic atmosphere and setting

Before anything supernatural happens, Dickens makes the place do the frightening. The deep cutting, the dripping stone, the black tunnel, the earthy and deadly smell — all of it establishes dread and primes us to expect horror. The setting is almost a character: a dungeon shut off from "the natural world."

2. Dramatic irony

By the final scene we, the readers, sense the truth gathering before the narrator names it. When the driver lifts his sleeve and waves his arm, we recognise the spectre's gesture a beat before the narrator does. Dickens withholds from the characters what he has already let us assemble, and the small gap between our knowing and theirs is where the chill lives.

3. The ambiguity of the warning / the supernatural

Is the figure a real ghost, a trick of an overworked, isolated mind, or something the story refuses to decide? Dickens sustains the ambiguity and never resolves it. The warning is real — disasters do follow — yet it is useless, because it will not say what, where, or when. That uncertainty destabilises any comfortable reading.

4. The withheld meaning, resolved in the final lines

The whole story has been pointing at a meaning it would not give. In the last lines the driver's own words and gesture turn out to be the spectre's, and the warning recontextualises everything that came before. Dickens reframes the haunting in a single recognition — and then stops, leaving us to feel it rather than explaining it.

Task A · A warning you ignored, or wish you had heeded (4 minutes)

Think of a time you were warned about something — by a person, by a feeling, or by the signs around you — and you ignored it, or wish now that you had listened. Describe three things, in order:

  1. what the warning was, and who or what gave it;
  2. what you had already decided before the warning came, and why you carried on;
  3. what happened in the end, and what you understood only afterwards.

Use at least three past perfect verbs ("I had already decided…", "by the time I realised…") and at least two effect verbs from the Technique tab (reframes, unsettles, recontextualises…).

Task B · Tell the chain of events in order (3 minutes)

Retell the story of the signalman — or any true sequence of events from your own life — making the order completely clear. Each step must connect to the one before it.

Build at least four sentences that use before, after, and by the time, with the earlier event in the past perfect. For example: "By the time the narrator returned, the signalman had already been killed."

Task C · Do you believe in premonitions? (4 minutes)

The signalman believed his visions were warnings; the narrator was not so sure. Where do you stand? Do you believe in premonitions, intuition, or a "gut feeling" that something is wrong — or do you think we only see the pattern afterwards, once we already know how things turned out?

Argue for two minutes for one side, then for two minutes for the other. Use the past perfect to describe what people felt or knew before events happened ("she had sensed it for weeks before…"), and at least one effect verb to describe how hindsight reframes a memory.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The warning was real, yet useless, because it never said what, where, or when. Have you ever been given information that was true but impossible to act on?
  2. The signalman was alone with his fear and could tell no one for fear of being thought mad. What does isolation do to a frightened mind?
  3. Dickens leaves the ending unexplained — no answer about what the figure was. Would the story be better or worse if he told us? Why?
  4. If you could go down into the cutting and speak to the signalman the night before he died, what would you say to him?
