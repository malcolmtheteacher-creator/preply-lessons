Priced Like the Next Tesla

In November 2021 a young electric-vehicle maker with almost no revenue went public and, within two days, was worth more than Ford or General Motors. Five years, tens of billions of dollars of losses and one paused factory later, it has finally launched the affordable car it was always going to need. Looking back, what should it have done differently — if anything?

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you’ll follow Rivian’s bet on becoming the next Tesla — from a spectacular stock-market debut to the long, expensive climb towards a mass-market car — and practise the language of hindsight: should have, could have, would have, needn’t have, might not have…
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • Have you ever bought something — a share, a house, a gadget — at the top of the hype? What happened next?
  • Is it fair to judge a decision by its outcome? What else should we judge it by?
  • When a company says it wants to be “the next” something, what is it really asking investors to believe?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Rivian sold shares to the public for the first time in November 2021. The IPO raised about $12 billion.
an IPO (initial public offering) — a company’s first sale of shares on a stock exchange. Collocations: price / launch / postpone an IPO; go public; a stock-market debut.
2. Before the IPO, Rivian said it expected revenue of at most $1 million for the quarter. It was still effectively pre-revenue.
pre-revenue (adj) — not yet earning meaningful money from sales. Common in start-ups, biotech and deep tech. Related: pre-profit, early-stage.
3. At the IPO Rivian had a backlog of 55,400 pre-orders for its pickup truck and SUV, which it expected to fill by the end of 2023.
a backlog (n) — orders received but not yet delivered. Build up / work through / clear a backlog. Also used for unfinished work in general.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms from the case: market capitalisation · a joint venture · a production ramp. Say the right term, then reveal.

1. Which term means the total value of a listed company’s shares at today’s price?
market capitalisation (market cap) — share price × number of shares. A market cap of $100bn; overtake X by market cap.
2. Which term means a new business owned and run together by two companies?
a joint venture (JV) — set up / launch / exit a joint venture; a 50/50 joint venture.
3. Which term means the period in which a factory moves from first units to full-volume output?
a production ramp — ramp up production; ramp costs; a slow / steep ramp. Ramps are where new car makers usually lose the most money.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. In 2024 Rivian decided to pause its Georgia factory and build its new model in Illinois instead. Pause here means…
  (a) cancel permanently   (b) stop for now, intending to restart later   (c) sell to another company
(b) — pause / suspend / mothball (mothball = close but keep in condition to reopen). Resume is the opposite.
2. By the end of 2025 Rivian’s balance sheet showed an accumulated deficit of about $27 billion. This means…
  (a) its total losses since it was founded   (b) money it owes to banks   (c) the value of unsold cars
(a) — all past losses added together. The opposite is retained earnings.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Investors paid for Rivian’s future, not its present. The word for the excitement that pushes prices beyond the facts is ___. (four letters)
hype — the hype around…; live up to the hype; overhyped. More formal: exuberance, froth, a bubble.
2. Make the noun from see + hind (behind) that means “understanding a situation only after it has happened”.
hindsight — with (the benefit of) hindsight; in hindsight. Opposite: foresight. Hindsight bias = believing afterwards that the outcome was obvious.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The debut (2021)

Rivian had spent years developing an electric pickup truck, the R1T, and an SUV, the R1S, at its factory in Normal, Illinois. It had powerful backers. Amazon had invested more than $1.3 billion and ordered 100,000 electric delivery vans, to be delivered by 2030. Ford was also a shareholder. The first production R1T left the Illinois line in September 2021.

On 9 November 2021 Rivian priced its IPO at $78 a share, valuing it at $66.5 billion and raising about $12 billion. Its own prospectus was frank: it expected to lose up to $1.28 billion in the third quarter, on revenue of between zero and $1 million. By 31 October it had built 180 R1Ts and delivered 156 — nearly all of them to its own employees. It had a backlog of 55,400 pre-orders and a factory that could, it said, make up to 150,000 vehicles a year.

The market did not hesitate. The shares jumped on their first day of trading, and by the end of the second day Rivian was worth $104.9 billion — more than General Motors or Ford, companies that sold millions of vehicles a year. CNBC summed up the mood: investors were “continuously trying to hop on the next Tesla”.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How many vehicles had Rivian delivered before its IPO, and to whom?

2. What was Rivian worth at the IPO price, and two days later?

1. 156 R1Ts by 31 October 2021, nearly all to its own employees.

2. $66.5 billion at $78 a share; $104.9 billion after the second day — more than GM or Ford.

Discuss:
  • Rivian raised about $12 billion at a very high price. With hindsight, was that the company’s best decision — or its most dangerous one?

Part 2 — The climb (2022–2024)

Building cars at scale proved far harder than selling shares. Rivian lost $6.75 billion in 2022 and $5.43 billion in 2023. Demand for electric vehicles grew more slowly than many had expected, competition increased, and the gap between Rivian’s market value and its sales became the story instead of its trucks.

In 2021 Georgia’s governor had announced a $5 billion second Rivian factory near Atlanta, with two phases of 200,000 vehicles each. In March 2024, as it revealed a smaller, cheaper SUV called the R2, Rivian paused Georgia and said it would build the R2 in Illinois instead, saving more than $2.25 billion. It later announced a $1.5 billion expansion of the Illinois plant.

Then came help from an established car maker. In June 2024 Volkswagen Group agreed to invest up to $5 billion; in November the two launched a 50/50 joint venture to develop the electrical architecture and software for both companies’ future vehicles, and VW raised the total to up to $5.8 billion. In January 2025 Rivian finalised a US Department of Energy loan of up to $6.6 billion for the Georgia plant.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Rivian decide about Georgia in March 2024, and why did it help?

2. What did Volkswagen get, and what did Rivian get, from the 2024 deal?

1. It paused the Georgia factory and moved R2 production to Illinois, saving more than $2.25 billion.

2. VW got access to Rivian’s electrical architecture and software through a 50/50 joint venture; Rivian got up to $5.8 billion of investment.

Discuss:
  • Pausing Georgia saved money — but it also delayed the capacity Rivian would need to grow. Should it have paused earlier, later, or not at all?

Part 3 — The car it needed (2025–today)

By 2025 Rivian was smaller in losses but not yet in profit. It delivered 42,247 vehicles that year and lost $3.63 billion; its accumulated deficit — all losses since it was founded — reached about $27 billion. For the first time it made a gross profit over a full year, $144 million, but almost entirely from software and services, much of it from the Volkswagen joint venture. The car business on its own still made a gross loss of $432 million.

The Georgia plan changed again. Rivian held a ceremonial groundbreaking in September 2025, and in April 2026 it renegotiated the government loan down to $4.5 billion for a single phase of 300,000 vehicles a year, with production planned for late 2028. Its chief executive said any further expansion would be paid for by the company.

On 9 June 2026 the first R2s — Rivian’s first vehicle priced under $50,000 — reached customers. In the second quarter of 2026 revenue rose 27% and the company recorded its best gross margin so far, but still a net loss of $837 million; it sold another $1.3 billion of shares in July. Rivian expects to deliver 65,000 to 70,000 vehicles in 2026. The next Tesla? Not yet. A survivor? So far, yes.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did Rivian’s first full-year gross profit come from?

2. How did the Georgia loan change in April 2026?

1. Mainly from software and services, especially work for the Volkswagen joint venture — the car business still made a gross loss.

2. From up to $6.57 billion for two phases (400,000 vehicles) to $4.5 billion for one phase of 300,000 vehicles, with production in late 2028.

Discuss:
  • Rivian’s software is currently paying for its cars. Is that a sign of a smart new business model, or of a car company that has not yet worked?

Grammar — past modals & hindsight

Rivian’s story is a perfect test of hindsight: everyone now has an opinion on what it should have done. At C1 the skill is to choose the modal that says exactly what you mean — criticism, possibility, an imagined alternative, or wasted effort.

1 · Criticism and regret — should have / shouldn’t have / ought to have
• “Rivian should have launched a cheaper model sooner.” (= it was the right thing to do, but it didn’t)
• “Investors shouldn’t have valued it above Ford before it had sold more than a few trucks to the public.”
• Softer: “With hindsight, it might have been wiser to…” · “It would have been better to…”
2 · Unrealised possibility — could have / might have
• “It could have raised less money at the IPO.” (= it was possible; it chose not to)
• “Without the IPO cash, it might not have survived 2023.” (= speculation about a past that didn’t happen)
• Careful: could have is also used for past probability: “The Georgia pause could have been a relief to investors.” (= perhaps it was)
3 · Imagined alternatives — would have / wouldn’t have (often with if)
• “If Rivian had waited a year, it would have gone public into a much weaker market.”
• “Without Volkswagen, the gross profit wouldn’t have happened in 2025.”
4 · Wasted effort — needn’t have vs didn’t need to
• “They needn’t have built the Georgia site so early.” (= they did it, but it was unnecessary)
• “They didn’t need to build R2 in Georgia, so they didn’t.” (= it wasn’t necessary — usually not done)
Form watch: always modal + have + past participle: should have gone, not should have went; in speech, should’ve, never should of.

1. Which modal?

Complete each sentence with should have · could have · would have · needn’t have.

1. The company raised less money — nobody forced it to sell so many shares.

2. The team worked all weekend — the launch was postponed by a month anyway.

3. If R2 had arrived in 2023, Rivian sold far more vehicles.

4. With the evidence they had, investors asked harder questions in 2021.

1. could have   2. needn’t have   3. would have   4. should have

2. Say it with hindsight

Rewrite each sentence using the word in brackets.

1. It was a mistake to delay the cheaper model. (should) → Rivian the cheaper model sooner.

2. It’s possible that the company survived only because of the IPO cash. (might) → Without the IPO cash, the company survived.

3. They spent money on Georgia early, but it wasn’t necessary. (needn’t) → They so much on Georgia so early.

4. Volkswagen invested, so Rivian had enough money. (wouldn’t) → If Volkswagen hadn’t invested, Rivian enough money.

1. should have launched / built   2. might not have   3. needn’t have spent   4. wouldn’t have had

3. The post-mortem — complete it, then deliver it

A business journalist asks a former Rivian investor: “Looking back, what went wrong?” Complete the answer, then deliver it aloud in under a minute.

“I’m not sure anything went ‘wrong’ exactly. We paid so much in 2021 — that’s on us, not the company. They moved faster on a cheaper car; that was always where the volume was. But if they hadn’t raised all that cash, they survived the last four years. And honestly, they started Georgia so early — pausing it saved them billions.”

shouldn’t have · could have / should have · might not have / wouldn’t have · needn’t have

Notice the balance: self-criticism, a fair criticism of others, an imagined alternative, and wasted effort. Good hindsight is specific — and admits what nobody could have known.

Take these into the Speak tab: a post-mortem that only uses should have sounds like blame; mix in could have, would have and couldn’t have known.

Going deeper

A note on the figures: amounts are rounded and come from the sources listed at the end of this tab. Market values change daily; the 2021 figures are those reported at the time. Rivian is still loss-making and still ramping up R2, so the case is still moving.

What the case actually tests. The first reading sees genius — or at least good timing. Rivian went public at the moment investors would pay the most, and raised about $12 billion when it could. That cash, plus Amazon, Volkswagen and a government loan, is the reason it is still here, still building, five years later. On this reading the high valuation was the market’s problem, not Rivian’s; the company used it to buy the years it needed to reach R2.

The second reading sees madness — or at least a strategy built for a world that did not arrive. Rivian started with expensive vehicles, planned a second giant factory before the first was profitable, and has lost about $27 billion. It paused Georgia, cut the plan from 400,000 to 300,000 vehicles, and in 2025 its car business still lost money on its own; the profit came from selling software to a rival. On this reading the “next Tesla” label set expectations no company could meet, and the cost of chasing it is still being paid.

The third point complicates both: hindsight itself. Many of the things Rivian “should have” done — a cheaper car first, a slower build-out — look obvious now, after demand for electric vehicles slowed. Whether they were obvious in 2021, when investors were rewarding speed and paying for the next Tesla, is a different question. The case asks you to separate a bad decision from a bad outcome — and to admit when you cannot tell them apart yet.
Talk it through:
  • Genius or madness? Argue one side with at least two facts from the case — then name the fact that most weakens your side.
  • Rivian’s first full-year gross profit came mostly from software sold to Volkswagen. Is that a weakness to fix, or the real business?
  • Amazon is both Rivian’s biggest shareholder and a major customer. Is that a strength or a risk for the company?
  • What would Rivian need to show in 2027 for you to call the 2021 bet a success?

Speak

All of it out loud, against the clock. Looking back is easy; looking back fairly — in front of people who lost money — is the skill.

Useful language: with hindsight, we should have…, we could have…, it would have been better to…, we needn’t have… · nobody could have known…, at the time it seemed… · a bad outcome is not always a bad decision · and to close: the question is not what we should have done in 2021, but what we do now.

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

You are Rivian’s chief executive at the annual shareholder meeting. A shareholder who bought in November 2021 stands up: “You were worth more than Ford when you’d delivered a handful of trucks — mostly to your own staff. You’ve lost twenty-seven billion dollars since. Admit it — you should never have gone public at that price.”

Answer properly: accept what you would do differently, defend what you would not, use at least one could have and one would have, and finish by moving to the future. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one pause (4 minutes)

Your company has just paused a major new site to save money and focus on one product. Explain it four ways, one minute each — with hindsight where it helps, and without where it would sound like blame:

  • To the board: what you should have seen earlier, and what you are doing now.
  • To the market: why pausing is discipline, not retreat.
  • To the workforce: what changes for them — and what you needn’t have asked of them.
  • To the press: one sentence about the decision that you will still stand behind in two years.

To finish — the harder questions

  1. Should companies raise as much money as they can when the market is generous — even if the valuation will be hard to justify later?
  2. Who should carry the blame for a bubble: the company that sold shares, the banks that priced them, or the investors who bought?
  3. Is “being the next Tesla” ever a useful goal — or does the comparison do more harm than good?
