Giving Your Opinion Away: Stance Adverbs

Small words that sit outside a sentence and label your whole judgement of it — not how something was done, but what you think about it.

Spot the pattern

Three honest verdicts:

  • "Frankly, the presentation could have been half as long."
  • "Predictably, the printer jammed the moment we needed it most."
  • "To my surprise, the new intern handled the client call better than anyone expected."

Before you check: where do these words sit, and what's their job — describing an action, or judging the whole sentence?

Check the pattern CLICK
They sit at the front, followed by a comma, and they judge the whole sentence, not a verb. Compare: "She spoke frankly" (frankly describes how she spoke) with "Frankly, she's wrong" (frankly signals your stance on the whole statement). They can also sit mid-sentence, set off by commas: "The presentation, frankly, could have been half as long."
Stance adverb, + rest of sentence.
Subject, stance adverb, + rest of sentence.
The same family covers a range of stances: frankly (a blunt personal opinion), predictably (exactly what you expected), to my surprise (the opposite of what you expected), worryingly (something concerning) — "Worryingly, three buses were cancelled on the same morning."

From a plain observation to a stance

"It's no surprise that the meeting overran again."
→
"Predictably, the meeting overran again."

The stance adverb does the work of a whole clause ("it's no surprise that...") in one word, right at the front.

How Strong Is 'Very'? Graded vs Extreme Adjectives

Some adjectives sit on a scale. Others already sit at the top of it — and that changes which intensifier fits.

Spot the pattern

Three verdicts:

  • "The lecture wasn't just good, it was absolutely brilliant."
  • "After the flight, I wasn't just tired, I was utterly exhausted."
  • "The reviews weren't just mixed, they were completely contradictory."

Before you check: try saying "very brilliant" or "very exhausted" out loud. Does it sound right? What's different about these adjectives compared with "good" or "tired"?

Check the pattern CLICK
Brilliant, exhausted and contradictory are already "extreme" — they already mean "very good," "very tired," "very mixed." So they take a different family of intensifiers: absolutely, utterly, completely, totally — never "very." Ordinary graded adjectives (good, tired, mixed, cold, big) take very, really, quite, fairly instead, because there really are degrees of them.
graded adjective (good, tired, cold, big) ← very / really / quite / fairly
extreme adjective (brilliant, exhausted, freezing, huge) ← absolutely / utterly / completely / totally

Choosing Your Words Carefully

The same fact can carry two different judgements — and sometimes the most diplomatic move is not to state the criticism directly at all.

Spot the pattern

  • "She's careful with money — some people would call that stingy, but I'd call it sensible."
  • "He's confident in meetings, though a couple of colleagues think it tips into arrogant."
  • "The feedback wasn't glowing exactly — let's say it was somewhat mixed."

Before you check: "careful with money" and "stingy" can describe the exact same behaviour. What changes between them? And what is "somewhat" doing in the third sentence — is it hiding the truth, or softening it?

Check the pattern CLICK
Word pairs like thrifty/stingy, confident/arrogant or slim/skinny describe the same facts but carry opposite judgements — the word you pick reveals your attitude, even when you didn't mean it to. "Somewhat," "rather," "less than" and "not exactly" don't hide the truth — they distance you from a blunt verdict, which is exactly what diplomatic feedback needs.
same behaviour, different judgement: thrifty / stingy · confident / arrogant · slim / skinny
somewhat / rather + negative adjective (somewhat disappointing)
less than / not exactly + positive adjective (less than ideal, not exactly cheap)
