Christmas at Ours

Twenty-six years she has done Christmas. The crackers are already in the cupboard. And this year, for reasons that are perfectly good and will sound like nothing at all down a phone line, you're not coming.

The Scene 🎬

It is the third week of October, which in Barbara's house is more or less the start of Christmas. She has bought the crackers. She has probably bought them in July. Every year since before you met Ian, the whole family has driven to Barbara's on Christmas Eve, eaten at hers on the day, and driven home on the 27th with the boot full of leftovers she wouldn't take no for an answer about.

This year you're not going. Last December the kids were strapped in the back of the car for five hours on a motorway that wasn't moving, and one of them was sick somewhere outside Stoke, and you and Ian sat in that traffic and said, quietly, never again. You've got the room now. You want your own kitchen, your own morning, your own mess. It is not a complaint about Barbara. It will, however, sound exactly like one.

So you're ringing her. Not Ian — you. Which is its own small problem, and one she will notice. And you're ringing now, in October, while there's still time for her to rearrange things and be cross about it in private, rather than in December when it's too late to be anything but hurt.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of one tradition in your family that nobody chose and nobody can seem to stop. Who would have to be the one to end it — and what would it cost them?
  2. When you've had to disappoint someone older than you, did you tell them straight or let it leak out slowly? Which do you think they'd have preferred, honestly?

Who's who

You
The One Who Rings 📞

Married to Ian, Barbara's son. Fond of her, genuinely. Absolutely not doing that motorway again. You've decided — you're not asking permission — but you'd rather she heard it kindly, in October, from a voice than from a text.

The other voice
Barbara 🎄

Twenty-six Christmases, all of them hers. She's not a monster and she won't shout. She'll go quiet, she'll ask a small question that isn't small, and she'll want to know whether she's lost this for one year or for good.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's a phone call, so there's nowhere to look but at the floor.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The caller never once says "it's just easier for us this year." What sort of reason do they give instead — and why is that harder for Barbara to push back on?

2. When Barbara asks "is it yours one year and mine the next, forever?", she isn't really asking about next December. What is she actually afraid of, and how does the caller settle it?

3. Barbara ends by saying she's disappointed, and the caller doesn't try to talk her out of it — no "oh don't be", no apology. Why is that the right move?

1. They give a fact that Barbara already knows is true — five hours in the car, a child sick outside Stoke — rather than an opinion about what suits them. An opinion invites another opinion back, so "it's easier for us" would simply meet "well, it's easier for me too". A fact she was there for leaves her nothing to argue with, because arguing would mean saying the five hours didn't happen.
2. She's afraid this isn't one year but the end of the whole thing — that Christmas has quietly been taken off her for good and nobody's going to say so. The caller answers that fear directly instead of the question, promising there's no turn-taking arrangement coming and that this call is about this December only, which is much smaller than what she was bracing for.
3. Because she's telling the truth, and the caller asked her to. Trying to argue her out of being disappointed would mean the invitation to say what she thought wasn't genuine, and she'd simply take the feeling away and keep it. Letting her be disappointed out loud, without panicking, is what lets her put it down.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for ending something that was never officially agreed in the first place. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Everything here depends on you sounding settled rather than sorry. If your voice goes up at the end of the sentence, you've turned a decision into a request, and she will quite reasonably answer it. Keep it low, keep it slow, and leave the silences alone — the pause after "at ours" is hers to fill, and filling it for her with nervous explaining is how people end up apologising for something they haven't done wrong.

📅 Getting in early

Phrase 1

Her December fills up early and you'd rather she heard this while there's still room to move things about. Which opener does that job without sounding like you've done something wrong?

A. "I know it's late notice, but—"
B. "I wanted to ring you before it all gets booked and settled."
C. "Have you started thinking about Christmas yet?"
B — "I wanted to ring you before it all gets booked and settled."
A apologises for a fault you haven't committed, and once you've handed someone an apology they will generally accept it and then hold onto it. C pretends to ask a question you already know the answer to, which she'll see through in about a second. B does something better: it explains the timing of the call before you say anything difficult, so that your ringing in October reads as consideration rather than as an ambush.
Phrase 2

Now the thing itself. You and Ian have decided — this isn't up for a vote, and pretending it is would be worse. Which version tells her, kindly, rather than asking her something you don't actually intend to let her answer?

A. "We were sort of thinking maybe we might have Christmas at ours?"
B. "Would it be alright if we did Christmas at our house this year?"
C. "We're going to do Christmas at ours this year."
C — "We're going to do Christmas at ours this year."
A and B both hand her a decision that isn't hers, which sounds gentler but is actually unkinder, because when she says no you'll have to overrule her and then she's been refused as well as disappointed. C is a plain statement of what is happening, and "at ours" is how families say it — warmer and less official than "at our house". Telling someone a decision straight is a form of respect: it treats them as an adult who can take it.

🕯️ Naming the size of it

Phrase 3

Don't skate past what you've just done. You say "I know it's a change. It's the first time, ___ ___" — two little words that hand her the floor. What are they?

"I know it's a change. It's the first time, isn't it."
That tag on the end isn't really a question, which is why it's written with a full stop rather than a question mark — you know perfectly well it's the first time. What it does is invite her to agree with you about how big this is, so that the two of you are looking at it together instead of from opposite sides. Saying "I know it's a change" before she has to tell you it's a change also takes the accusation off the table, because it's very hard to accuse someone of not understanding something they've just said out loud.
Phrase 9

She's noticed it's your voice and not her son's, and she hasn't quite said why that bothers her. Put the pieces in order to get ahead of it:

and not Ian.I knowme ringinghow it'll look,
"I know how it'll look, me ringing and not Ian."
"How it'll look" means the story she could tell herself about this — that his wife is behind it and he's been talked into it. Saying it out loud yourself is what stops it working, because a suspicion only has power while nobody names it, and the moment you name it she has to either agree with you or drop it. Notice you don't deny it or defend yourself; you just put it on the table.

🚗 The reason, and the thing it isn't

Phrase 6

She's already halfway to deciding this is punishment for something. Shut that door before she gets through it: "It's ___ anybody's done. ___ at all." Same word twice.

"It's nothing anybody's done. Nothing at all."
The second sentence is the whole point, and it's why you say it twice. One denial sounds like the first move in a negotiation; the repetition, with no explanation hung on the end of it, sounds like a door closing. "Anybody" matters too, because saying "it's nothing you've done" plants the idea that somebody did something — she'll simply go looking for who.
Phrase 5

Now the reason. She was there last Christmas; she saw the state of everyone when you arrived. Which of these can she least easily argue with?

A. "It's just easier for us this year."
B. "We're a bit fed up of all that driving."
C. "The kids were in the car for five hours last year."
C — "The kids were in the car for five hours last year."
A and B are opinions about what suits you, and an opinion can always be met with another opinion — she's tired too, she's been cooking since six, and now you're both comparing hardships. C is a fact she watched happen, so the only way to argue with it is to say it didn't, which she can't. Give people something true and specific to hold rather than a feeling to weigh against their own.

🤝 The invitation and the smaller yes

Phrase 4

Half the people who say "you're welcome to come to us" don't mean it, and she knows that, so the invitation has to arrive with proof attached. Put the pieces in order:

not just saying it.to come to us —We'd love yougenuinely,
"We'd love you to come to us — genuinely, not just saying it."
The interesting half is after the dash. Admitting out loud that this is exactly the sort of thing people say without meaning shows you know the difference, and knowing the difference is the only evidence available to you over the phone. It's a small trick worth stealing: when a phrase has been worn out by insincere use, the fastest way to rescue it is to acknowledge that it has been.
Phrase 7

She's not going to come to yours, and you can hear it. So don't leave her with nothing — offer her a different day that's properly hers, with all of you there and no rushing off. Say it out loud in your own words first. What do you offer?

"Would Boxing Day work instead? We'd come to you for the whole day."
Two things are doing the work. It's a real date rather than "we'll see you at some point over Christmas", which is what people say when they mean nothing. And "for the whole day" answers the fear underneath, because what she's picturing is the fly-in visit — two hours, coats on, everyone looking at the time. Take a thing away from someone and put a specific, generous thing back in their hands.

🔒 The question she's really asking

Phrase 8

"So is it yours one year and mine the next, forever?" She isn't asking about next December. She's asking whether she's lost Christmas for good. Which reply answers the real question?

A. "Let's just get through this one first, eh?"
B. "We're not starting a rota, I promise you that."
C. "Well, we'll see how it goes, won't we."
B — "We're not starting a rota, I promise you that."
A rota is a list of people taking it in turns — the sort of thing pinned up in a workplace so everyone knows whose week it is. Christmas becoming a rota is precisely the future she's dreading, so naming it and refusing it is the reassurance. A and C are both polite ways of not answering, and she will hear that, because when someone asks whether they've lost something forever and you say "we'll see", you've told them yes.
Phrase 10

She's gone quiet and polite, which is worse than cross, and you'd rather have this out now than hear it second-hand from Ian in eight weeks. Put the pieces in order:

hear it nowSay what you actually think —than in January.I'd far rather
"Say what you actually think — I'd far rather hear it now than in January."
"Actually" is carrying the sentence, because it quietly acknowledges that what she's said so far isn't quite what she means, without accusing her of anything. And naming January is what makes it a real invitation rather than a polite one: it says you know the alternative is her being fine about it for two months and then not fine about it at a birthday, and you'd rather take it on the chin today. Only say this if you can hear the answer without flinching.

🔑 Make them yours

These phrases aren't really about Christmas. They're for any moment when you're changing something that someone else built their year around. Pin three of them to your own life so you'll reach for them when it happens. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"We're going to do Christmas at ours this year." — something you've already decided but have been floating as a question. What is it, and how would you say it as a decision?

"It's nothing anybody's done. Nothing at all." — who might take one of your decisions personally, and what would you need to shut down before they got there?

"Would Boxing Day work instead? We'd come to you for the whole day." — a "no" of yours that could come with a specific, generous alternative attached. What would you put back in their hands?

The Twist

🦃 She says yes

She takes it beautifully. "Of course, love, you should have your own Christmas." You're so relieved you nearly cry. And then she carries on: she'll come on the 23rd, she'll bring her turkey because yours won't be big enough, she's already spoken to the butcher; she'll do the stuffing the night before, she's bringing her tablecloth, and what time were you thinking of putting it in, only twelve is too late, it wants to be in by half nine. She is not being difficult. She is being lovely. She is also, gently and completely, hosting Christmas in your kitchen — which is the one thing this whole call was about. Start the timer. You have to say no a second time, in the same conversation, to a woman who has just said yes. Where exactly is your line — the turkey? the tablecloth? the 23rd? — and can you hold it without taking back the invitation you fought to make her believe?

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Nobody ever sat down and agreed that Christmas would be at Barbara's. It just happened one year, and then happened again, and somewhere around the fourth or fifth time it stopped being a plan and became a fact — the kind of fact that buys crackers in July.

Which is why ending it feels so much heavier than it ought to. There's no agreement to renegotiate, no meeting to call. There's only a phone call, and someone has to make it, and whoever makes it will spend a while afterwards wondering whether they were the villain of it.

The traditions we mind about most are almost always the ones nobody chose. They got their weight from repetition, not from anyone deciding they mattered — and then one October somebody has to say out loud that the repetition is stopping, which is a strange sort of confession to have to make about something you never signed up for.

Two bigger questions

  1. Barbara has hosted twenty-six Christmases. Is that a gift she's been giving, or a job she's been stuck with — and would she know the difference by now? Would anyone ever have asked her?
  2. Think about who in your family does the hosting, the ringing, the remembering of birthdays. How did they get that job, and what would actually happen if they stopped?
