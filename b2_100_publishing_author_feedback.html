Giving Feedback to an Author

B2 Level · Publishing · Editorial Conversations

Publishing · Dewey 070.5

First Thoughts

An author you commissioned has delivered the first draft of their novel. Some of it is wonderful. Large parts of the middle — about a hundred pages — are not working: the pacing sags, a major character’s motivation is unclear, and the ending does not earn its climax.

You have to tell them this, in a way that makes them want to fix it. An author who feels attacked will either fight you or freeze. An author who feels guided will dig back in and write you a better book.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. What is the difference between feedback that helps a writer improve and feedback that makes them defensive?

2. When you’ve received creative feedback in your own life, what kind of phrases made it easier to hear? Which made it harder?

3. Is it kinder to be very direct or to soften feedback carefully? Does it depend on the person?

4. How honest should an editor be on a first-draft call? Everything at once, or just the biggest issues?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • Imagine the author has just picked up the phone. What is your very first sentence — before any feedback at all?
  • A senior editor once said: “Always name what is working first — not as a trick, but because it tells you what you are trying to save.” What do you think of that?

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Opening warmly and honestly

  • “Thank you for sending this over — I know how much of you is in it, and I want to do it justice in this call.”
  • “I want to start with what I think is really working, because that matters for how we approach the rest.”
  • “Before I share my notes, I’d love to hear how you feel about the draft — what you’re happy with, what you’re less sure about.”

Naming what’s working — specifically

  • “The opening chapter is remarkable — the voice is exactly the voice we commissioned you for.”
  • “Elena is a wonderful character. I believed her completely for the first two acts.”
  • “There are three scenes in here that I read twice because they are just beautiful.”

Framing the bigger concerns

  • “I’d like to walk you through two developmental concerns — both are fixable, both are worth talking through in detail.”
  • “The biggest question I have for you is about the middle section — I think we’re losing the reader somewhere around chapter fourteen.”
  • “None of this is about the quality of the writing — line by line it’s strong. This is about structure.”

Closing the loop so they go home motivated

  • “What I’m going to send you is a written version of these notes — take a few days before you respond.”
  • “I don’t want you to race back to the desk tomorrow — let this settle first.”
  • “I still believe in this book completely. I want you to know that before you hang up.”

The Scenario

The situation: You are an editor at a mid-sized publisher. The author, a debut novelist you championed through acquisition, has sent in their first full draft. The opening and ending are strong; the middle hundred pages lose pacing and a key character’s motivation becomes muddled. A call has been scheduled for this afternoon. The author is nervous — this is their first real editorial conversation.

Your task: Run the call. By the end of it, the author should know what is working, what the two biggest concerns are, and what happens next. They should hang up feeling challenged but motivated, not crushed.

Step 1 · Plan your response

Step 2 · Deliver

When you are ready, role-play the conversation out loud. Be ready to handle the follow-up questions below.

Follow-up questions to handle:

  • Do you still think this is publishable?
  • How much do I need to rewrite? Am I starting again?
  • Is this going to delay the publication date?
  • Could you just tell me exactly what to change?

Reflection

The most important part. What did you notice, what surprised you, what will you try differently next week?

1. Which follow-up was hardest to answer honestly without either over-reassuring or scaring them?

2. Where in the conversation did you want to soften a note more than it deserved to be softened? What did you do?

3. What is the difference between kind feedback and useful feedback? Can they be the same thing?

4. If you had to teach a new editor one rule about author calls, what would it be?
