THE COLUMBO CASEBOOK · B2/C1 · 50 min

Murder by the Book

A crime writer plots the perfect murder — and a rumpled lieutenant reads his books with great interest.

Today: you'll tell the story of a crime writer who plans a murder on paper and then for real, and you'll master must have, can't have and might have — the language of reasoning from clues.
Warm up — have a go, there are no wrong answers.
Talk about:
  • Do you enjoy detective stories? What makes a good one?
  • You walk into an empty office and find a cup of tea, still warm, and a lit desk lamp. What can you work out?
  • What does it mean to “take the credit” for someone else's work? Why does it matter?

The words for today's story

Work it out from the situation

Read each sentence aloud and say what you think the bold word means — then reveal.

Each time a copy of her novel sells, a small payment goes into her bank account, and the cheques have kept arriving for twenty years. These are her royalties.
royalties (n, plural) — payments made to an author, musician or inventor for each copy or use of their work.
When the police asked where he had been at nine o'clock, he named a restaurant, and three waiters and a bill stamped 8.52 backed up his story. It was a perfect alibi.
an alibi (n) — proof that you were somewhere else when a crime happened.
He labelled every file, checked each figure three times and never left a pen out of place. His planning was meticulous.
meticulous (adj) — extremely careful and precise about every small detail.

Which word?

Read the meaning and try to find the word before you reveal. The first letter is given.

Which word means making someone look guilty, or seeming to prove that they are?  i__________ (adj)
incriminating (adj) — suggesting that someone is guilty. An incriminating letter / photograph / fingerprint.
Which word means the reason why someone does something, especially a crime?  m_____ (n)
a motive (n) — the reason behind a person's actions. Money is the oldest motive of all.
Which word means to pretend to have a feeling or quality you do not really have?  f____ (v)
to feign (v) — to pretend. He feigned surprise / illness / ignorance.

Choose the meaning

Pick a, b or c, then reveal.

plausible — “Being stuck in traffic is a plausible excuse for being late.”  plausible means:
a) shameful   b) rather unusual   c) easy to believe
(c) — plausible (adj) means seeming reasonable and likely to be true. It does not mean the thing is true.
rumpled — “He arrived in a rumpled raincoat and a crumpled hat.”  rumpled means:
a) very expensive   b) creased and untidy   c) brand new
(b) — rumpled (adj) means creased and untidy, as if slept in.
unravel — “It took the team months to unravel the mystery.”  unravel is closest to:
a) untangle and understand   b) hide from view   c) repeat again and again
(a) — to unravel (v) literally means to undo knitted thread or rope; here, to work out something complicated.

Odd one out

Three of these words mean “to admit what you did”. One does not. Which is the odd one out?  
confess  ·  own up  ·  deny  ·  come clean
deny is the odd one out — it means to say something is not true. to confess (v) — to admit that you have done something wrong or illegal. He confessed to the crime.
Use them out loud:
  • Describe a situation in which someone had a perfect alibi but you still doubted it.
  • Name a motive for a crime in a film you know.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, try the quick check from memory, then talk about the question.

Part 1 — A partnership with a secret

Ken Franklin and Jim Ferris are the famous partnership behind a string of bestselling detective novels. Their names appear together on every cover, and the public assumes they share the work equally.

They do not. Jim writes the books, every word of them, while Ken enjoys the interviews, the fame and the royalties. It is a comfortable arrangement for Ken, until the day Jim announces that he intends to carry on alone. Ken realises that his whole easy life is about to disappear.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who really writes the novels?

2. Why does Ken have a problem when Jim makes his announcement?

1. Jim writes them. Ken only takes the credit and the money.

2. Jim plans to work without him, so Ken would lose his fame and his income.

Discuss:
  • Is it fair when one person takes the credit for another's work? Where have you seen it happen?

Part 2 — A plot with a telephone call

Ken is a man who has spent his career inventing perfect crimes on paper, so he decides to commit one for real. He plans it carefully and builds his alibi around a telephone call. When Jim is found dead, Ken is shocked, helpful and polite.

Then a rumpled lieutenant arrives. He is full of admiration for the novels and seems more interested in how the plots are put together than in the crime itself. Ken, flattered, enjoys talking about his craft.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Ken use as the centre of his alibi?

2. How does the lieutenant behave towards Ken at first?

1. A telephone call.

2. He admires the novels and asks about how the plots are constructed, as if he were a fan.

Discuss:
  • Why might a suspect relax more with a polite, untidy detective than with an imposing one?

Part 3 — A note on a scrap of paper

Columbo learns something small about the dead man: Jim was in the habit of jotting down story ideas on any scrap of paper that happened to be nearby.

Searching through them, Columbo finds a note that describes the very telephone-call alibi Ken has just used. The man who sells himself as the creator of the plots has no answer. He is forced to confess.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What habit of Jim's does Columbo discover?

2. What does he find, and what is the result?

1. He wrote story ideas on whatever scrap of paper was nearby.

2. A note describing the same telephone-call alibi; Ken has to confess.

Discuss:
  • There is a neat irony here: the partner whose work was stolen helps to catch his killer. Is the ending satisfying? Why, or why not?

Grammar — must have / can't have / might have

Detectives never see the crime — they see what is left behind. English has three modal structures for reasoning about the past from the evidence. Each one tells your listener how sure you are.

The three structures — modal + have + past participle:
• must have + pp — almost certain it happened: “The tea is still warm, so someone must have been here a minute ago.”
• can't / couldn't have + pp — almost certain it did not happen: “The door was locked, so he can't have walked in.”
• might / may / could have + pp — it is possible, but you cannot be sure: “She might have left by the back stairs.”
Watch out: for the opposite of must we say can't, not mustn't. And might have is for a guess; if you have proof, use must have.

1. Which one? Choose must have, can't have or might have

Use the cue in brackets to decide how sure the speaker is.

1. The restaurant's records show that he paid and left at 8.40, so he been there at nine. (almost certainly not true)

2. The note is in Jim's handwriting, so Jim written it. (almost certainly true)

3. Nobody saw how the killer left; he escaped through the garden, but there are other possibilities. (just a possibility)

1. can't have (or couldn't have — certain that it did not happen)

2. must have (strong evidence)

3. might have (or may / could have — only one possibility)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence has the right modal but the verb is missing. Write the verb in the correct form (past participle).

1. The lieutenant must have (notice) the scrap of paper as soon as he walked in.

2. She can't have (know) about the plan; she was abroad all week.

3. They might have (argue) before the accident, but nobody heard it.

1. noticed (must have + past participle)

2. known (irregular: know – knew – known)

3. argued (might have + past participle)

Detective drill

Three clues. For each one, say what must have, can't have and might have happened.

Clue 1. A cup of coffee on a desk is still hot, and the desk lamp is still on, but the office is empty.
Possible answers: Someone must have been here only minutes ago. They can't have gone home for the night. They might have stepped out to the corridor, or been called away suddenly.
Clue 2. A window is wide open in the middle of winter, and a curtain is lying on the floor.
Possible answers: Someone must have opened it deliberately. The wind can't have pulled the curtain down alone. Somebody might have climbed in or out — or been in a hurry.
Clue 3. A single muddy footprint is just inside the front door, but the hall carpet is clean.
Possible answers: The person must have stopped at the door. They can't have walked further in. They might have taken their shoes off, or been interrupted.

Speak

Do all of this out loud. The timer keeps you going: fill the time with detail.

Useful language: for certainty — must have, can't have; for guesses — might / could have. To tell the story: “It turns out that…”, “What gives him away is…”, “In the end he has no choice but to…”. For opinions: “You could argue that…”, “What strikes me is…”.

Task A · Tell the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell the story of the writer, his partner and the lieutenant as if to a friend who has never seen it: the secret partnership → the plan and the telephone call → the polite, rumpled visitor → the scrap of paper and the confession.

Use at least three of today's modal structures (must have, can't have, might have) and five key words (royalties, alibi, meticulous, incriminating, motive, feign, rumpled, plausible, unravel, confess).

Task B · Credit where it is due (4 minutes)

Ghostwriters write books for famous people; songwriters write hits for singers; assistants prepare work that a boss presents as their own. When is this acceptable, and when does it become unfair? Build an argument with examples, and decide where you would draw the line.

Task C · A mystery of your own (4 minutes)

Describe a puzzling situation — something you found, a noise, an object out of place — and reason aloud about what must have, can't have and might have happened.

Stuck for an idea? Choose one:

  • A famous unsolved mystery or disappearance you have heard about.
  • An imaginary house where you find a half-finished meal and a ringing telephone.
  • A sudden power cut in a big building at night.
  • A museum painting that has gone missing from its frame.
  • A friend who unexpectedly left a party early.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The killer in this story is caught by a note he never imagined existed. Why are small, ordinary pieces of evidence so powerful in stories like this?
  2. The lieutenant is polite, untidy and seems slightly lost. Is it better for a detective to look threatening or harmless? Why?
  3. Writers often say “write what you know”. Is it possible to plan a perfect crime on paper and still make a mistake in real life? Why?
