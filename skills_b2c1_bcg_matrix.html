A famous picture

In 1963 Bruce Henderson founded a small consulting firm in Boston. Around 1970 the Boston Consulting Group published a simple two-by-two chart for companies with many products. It asked just two questions about each one: Is its market growing fast? and Is it a leader in that market?

The four boxes got memorable names — and those names are still used in boardrooms today, often by people who have never seen the original chart.

Guess before you learn. Here are the four names:

⭐ 🐄 ❓ 🐕

  • Star · Cash Cow · Question Mark · Dog
  • Which one do you think a company is happiest to have? Which is in trouble?
  • Why might a cow be good news for a business?
  • What do you think a “question mark” product is uncertain about?

Build the matrix

The chart has two measurements:

  • Market growth rate (up the side) — how fast the whole market is growing each year.
  • Relative market share (along the bottom) — your share compared with your biggest competitor. Above 1.0 means you are the leader.

Follow the money

The matrix is really about cash moving around a company. In the ideal story, a product travels like this:

❓ Question Mark→ ⭐ Star→ 🐄 Cash Cow→ 🐕 Dog

Cash from the Cows pays for the Question Marks, hoping some of them grow into Stars. When a Star’s market slows down, it becomes the next Cow.

Work it out together

1. A Star is a market leader. So why does it often produce very little spare cash?

Its market is growing fast, so it must keep spending — on factories, marketing and staff — just to hold its share. The money comes in and goes straight back out.

2. Why does a Star not stay a Star for ever, even if the company does nothing wrong?

Markets mature. When growth slows, the product moves down the chart — if it keeps its share, it becomes a Cash Cow.

3. What happens to a company that has plenty of Cows but no Question Marks or Stars?

It is rich today but has nothing to replace its earners when their markets decline. Its future is at risk.

Say it three ways

Each idea below is said plainly. Say it again in “boardroom” style, using the words above. Then say it a third way — softer, as if the product’s manager is in the room.

1. “The old laptop range makes money and we barely spend on it. Let’s keep it that way.”

2. “Our garden furniture business is losing money. Let’s sell it.”

3. “Nobody is buying the paper version any more, so let’s stop making it slowly.”

1. Boardroom: “The laptop range is a classic cash cow — we should keep milking it.” Softer: “The laptop range is doing a great job funding the rest of us, so it probably doesn’t need extra investment.”

2. Boardroom: “Garden furniture is a drain on resources. I’d divest.” Softer: “It might be time to ask whether garden furniture still belongs in our portfolio.”

3. Boardroom: “Let’s phase out print.” Softer: “We could look at a gradual move away from print over the next year or two.”

Where the matrix breaks

The matrix is famous, but it has critics. For each point, first say whether you agree and why. Then open it to see the argument.

1. “Two numbers can’t describe a business.”

The chart ignores profit margins, brand strength, competition, regulation and the skills of the team. A small share of a very profitable niche can be worth more than leadership of a cheap mass market.

2. “A Dog can be worth keeping.”

Some products matter because of what they support: they bring customers into the shop, complete a range that big retailers expect, or share costs with a stronger product — exactly the problem in Question 5.

3. “It all depends on how you define the market.”

Is iced coffee in the “canned coffee” market, the “cold drinks” market or the “caffeine” market? Change the definition and the same product can jump from Dog to Star.

4. “The labels can come true.”

Call something a Dog, take away its budget and its best people, and it will certainly decline — which then seems to prove the label was right.

Looking back further

Some business English you may have met before. Answer from memory — have a go, even if you are not sure.

1. What is the difference between revenue and profit? Give an example with numbers.

2. Give two polite ways to disagree with someone senior in a meeting.

3. How would you interrupt a speaker politely to ask a question?

4. What is the difference between “The sales rose by 5%” and “The sales rose to 5 million”?

1. Revenue is all the money coming in from sales; profit is what is left after costs. Sales of €10m with costs of €8m = €2m profit.

2. “I see it slightly differently…” / “I’m not sure I’d go that far…”

3. “Sorry to interrupt, but could I just ask…?”

4. By gives the size of the change; to gives the final figure.
