The House Viewing

Twenty minutes inside the biggest purchase of your life, guided by a man whose job is to make you hurry. The photos were wide-angled, the description was poetry, and the word 'cosy' is doing a lot of work. You have a list of questions. Use all of them.

The Scene 🎬

Nobody buys a jacket without trying it on twice. Houses, people buy in twenty rushed minutes — because someone in a nice suit kept the tour moving.

You and your partner are viewing a house you might genuinely buy: three bedrooms, garden, close to the school. The listing called it "a charming period property with huge potential, in a sought-after pocket" — which, translated, could mean anything from a dream to a money pit with good light.

Showing you round is Julian, the estate agent. Julian is not a villain; he's a professional whose income depends on the sale, whose language has evolved over a century to make small sound snug and problems sound like character. Every question you ask, he's answered a thousand times — which is fine, because the questions still work. Asking them is the whole game.

The skills of this twenty minutes: translating the polish back into facts, asking the questions that reveal why this house is really for sale, escaping the guided tour long enough to actually look — and leaving with your decision still your own, whatever the "two other interested couples" are supposedly doing tomorrow.

  1. What's the biggest thing you've ever bought too quickly? What question would have saved you?
  2. "Cosy", "characterful", "up-and-coming" — does your language have estate-agent words too? What do they really mean?
YouSerious buyer, done the homework, knows the street prices. Has one rule: no decisions inside the house.
JulianThe agent. Charming, quick, professionally optimistic. Every flaw has a euphemism and every silence has a filler. Respects buyers who ask real questions — they're the ones who actually buy.

The Conversation 💬

Saturday, 11am. Julian is already at the door, keys jangling, smile on.

JulianCome in, come in! Isn't it charming? You can really feel the period character. This is the reception room — cosy, as you can see, wonderful light in the mornings.
YouIt's lovely. And "cosy" meaning small?
JulianHa! Compact, I'd say. Intimate. People pay a premium for intimate these days. Now, through here—
YouBefore we tour — how long has it been on the market?
JulianOh — a little while. It's a discerning market at this price point.
YouHow long is a little while?
Julian…Eleven weeks. Which is nothing for a property of this character, honestly.
YouEleven weeks. And why are they selling?
JulianThe owners are… relocating. Family reasons. All very amicable.
YouAmicable as in divorce?
Julian*a pause* …You didn't hear it from me. But yes — which means, between us, they'd both like it done quickly.
YouThat's useful, thank you. So — what's it actually going for, realistically? Not the asking price. The street says number 14 went for thirty under asking in March.
JulianYou HAVE done your homework. Look — the asking is ambitious, I'll grant you. Realistically? Somewhere nearer number 14's money. But I didn't say that either.
YouNoted and forgotten. The listing says "scope for updating". What does "scope for updating" mean when I'm standing in it?
JulianThe boiler's original. The wiring… has history. And you'll want to look at the bathroom with generous eyes.
YouHonest answer — appreciated. Would you mind if we had a proper look round on our own? Ten minutes. You've seen it; we haven't.
Julian…Most people don't ask that. Go on then — I'll be in the garden pretending to check my emails. Cupboards open, taps run, that's what they're there for.
You*ten minutes later, returning* The bedroom cupboard has damp in the corner, and the garden wall's leaning. Also — what are the neighbours like? That trampoline next door has seen action.
JulianYoung family that side, retired couple the other. The trampoline's louder than the retired couple, I'll say that. Look — I can feel you're serious. Full disclosure: there are two other couples viewing this weekend, so if you're interested—
YouThere are always two other couples, Julian. If we go anywhere near an offer, I'd want a survey before anything — damp corner included. That's not negotiable.
JulianCompletely standard. So… shall we talk numbers? The vendors are motivated, as I accidentally told you.
YouNot today. We'll sleep on it and call you tomorrow either way — you won't have to chase us. And Julian — if the price moved towards number 14's, we might talk.
Julian*writing his number down* You know, most viewings, I do all the talking. This was refreshing. Tomorrow, then — either way.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for viewing anything big — houses, flats, cars, businesses — where someone charming is paid to keep you moving. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The agent's tools are pace and polish; yours are questions and time. Every phrase here either slows the tour down or trades a euphemism for a fact. Ask cheerfully — the tone is friendly throughout — but ask everything, and decide nothing indoors.

🔎 The questions that reveal the real story

Phrase 1

The job: the first fact every buyer needs. Complete it:

"How long has it been on the ___?"

"How long has it been on the market?"
Time on the market is the one number that summarises everything: eleven weeks means other buyers have looked and walked, and it means the seller is getting nervous — both of which are your negotiating position. Note the follow-up when the answer was fog: "How long is a little while?" Repeat the question calmly until it gets a number. Vague answers to this one are always hiding something.

Take it anywhere: "How long has it been on the market?" Houses, cars, anything for sale — and always chase the fog.
Phrase 2

The job: the question agents dread most. Complete it:

"And ___ are they selling?"

"And why are they selling?"
Four words that tell you how to negotiate. "Relocating for work" means no hurry; a divorce, a probate, a chain about to collapse means the seller values speed over price — which is worth thousands to you. You won't always get the truth, but watch what happened here: a gentle follow-up ("amicable as in divorce?") and suddenly you know they want it done quickly. Ask it lightly; listen hard.

Take it anywhere: "Why are they selling?" Houses, used cars, second-hand anything. The reason for the sale is the shape of the deal.
Phrase 3

The job: getting past the asking price to the real one. Which version?

A. "Would they take less, do you think?"
B. "What's it actually going for, realistically?"
C. "The price is ridiculous, obviously."
B — "What's it actually going for, realistically?"
A begs, and begging gets "make an offer and we'll see". C insults the thing you might buy — never do that until you own it. B treats the asking price as what it is — an opening position — and asks the professional for his professional read. It works best with homework attached: "number 14 went for thirty under in March" told Julian you can't be quoted poetry. Evidence plus a calm question got the real number in one move.

Take it anywhere: "What's it actually going for, realistically?" — with one researched comparison in your pocket.
Phrase 4

The job: translating the euphemism, with a smile. Complete it:

"And 'cosy' means ___?"

"And 'cosy' means small?"
The gentle translation game — said with a smile, never a sneer. Each time you cash a euphemism into plain English, two things happen: you get the fact, and the agent learns that polish is wasted on you, so the rest of the tour gets more honest. The variant for listings: "What does 'scope for updating' mean when I'm standing in it?" — which bought you the boiler, the wiring and the bathroom in one answer.

Take it anywhere: "And '___' means ___?" Job adverts, menus, dating profiles, sales decks — cash every pretty word into a plain one.

👀 Taking control of the viewing

Phrase 5

The job: escaping the guided tour. Put the pieces in order:

a proper look roundWould you mind if we hadon our own?
"Would you mind if we had a proper look round on our own?"
The tour is choreography — it faces you towards the light and walks you past the damp. Ten unsupervised minutes is where you meet the actual house: open the cupboards, run the taps, stand in the quiet. "Most people don't ask that," said Julian — exactly. Most people also miss the corner of the bedroom cupboard. You didn't.

Take it anywhere: "Would you mind if I had a proper look on my own?" Houses, cars, offices you might work in — never inspect anything only at escort pace.
Phrase 6

The job: the question about what you can't see on a Saturday morning. Complete it:

"What are the ___ like?"

"What are the neighbours like?"
You're not buying a building; you're buying a spot between other people's lives — and a viewing shows you none of that. Ask the agent, then verify: walk the street on a weekday evening, look at the state of the gardens either side, knock on a door if you're brave (people tell the truth about their street). The trampoline observation did half the work here — evidence first, question second.

Take it anywhere: "What are the neighbours like?" — and its cousins: what's it like at night, in the rain, in term time?

🚪 Leaving with the power

Phrase 7

The job: the condition that protects you from everything you can't see. Complete it:

"I'd want a ___ before any offer."

"I'd want a survey before any offer."
A survey is a professional inspection of the building's bones — and this sentence, said early, does two jobs. It tells the agent you're a serious, process-following buyer (they prefer those). And it pre-loads your negotiating position: when the survey finds what the damp corner suggests, the price conversation reopens with evidence. "That's not negotiable" costs nothing to say now and everything to skip.

Take it anywhere: "I'd want ___ checked before any offer." Surveys, test drives, trial periods, references — the look-before-money rule, stated up front.
Phrase 8

The job: answering "two other couples are viewing this weekend". Complete it:

"There are ___ two other couples, Julian."

"There are always two other couples, Julian."
The oldest pressure line in the trade, met with a smile and total immunity. You're not calling him a liar — sometimes the couples are even real — you're declining to let invented urgency make a six-figure decision. Said warmly, with his name on the end, it actually earns respect: he knows you know the game, and the game stops. If the house sells to the phantom couples, it was never at your price.

Take it anywhere: "There's always another buyer / another candidate / an offer ending Friday." Urgency you didn't verify is urgency you should ignore.
Phrase 9

The job: the exit — decision protected, courtesy intact. Complete it:

"We'll ___ on it and call you tomorrow ___ way."

"We'll sleep on it and call you tomorrow either way."
The whole strategy in one sentence: no decisions inside the house. "Sleep on it" moves the choice out of the charm field and into your own kitchen; "either way" is the professional courtesy that keeps the agent warmly on your side — nobody wastes his time, so he'll answer your calls quickly for the next house too. And then actually call, either way. That reputation follows you round a small market.

Take it anywhere: "We'll sleep on it and call you tomorrow either way." Cars, contracts, job offers — big decisions travel badly under pressure; take them home.
Phrase 10

The job: leaving one door open — on your terms. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "If the price moved towards number 14's, we might talk."
Not an offer — an orientation. It tells the agent exactly which direction unlocks you, anchored to evidence he's already conceded, and it costs you nothing: "might talk" commits you to precisely nothing. Now the pressure flows the other way — the motivated sellers hear tonight that the serious couple would move at a realistic number. You negotiated from your own hallway.

Take it anywhere: "If ___ moved, we might talk." Salaries, rents, deals of every size — name the direction, promise nothing.

The Echo 🕰️

The most beautiful house ever built was never lived in. The Taj Mahal — white marble, twenty-two years, twenty thousand workers — was raised by the emperor Shah Jahan as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz, who died giving birth to their fourteenth child. It is the greatest property listing in history: perfect symmetry, river views, gardens of paradise. And every inch of it is grief, dressed in marble.

Which is the thing about beautiful properties: the polish always has a story underneath, and the story is where the truth lives. Shah Jahan spent so ruinously on perfection that his own son eventually deposed him — and the emperor ended his days locked in a fort across the river, able to see the beautiful thing he'd built only from a window, at a distance, like a buyer who never got the survey done.

By all means fall in love at the viewing — everyone does, that's what viewings are for. Just remember that "charming period property" is marble-language, and ask what it cost, why it's really there, and what's in the corner of the cupboard.
