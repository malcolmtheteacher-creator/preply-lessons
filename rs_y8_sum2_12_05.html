Three Faiths, One City

Think: The City of Peace?

Jerusalem is one of the world's most sacred cities. It is holy to three religions: Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Yet it is also one of the world's most contested cities, with centuries of conflict over sacred spaces and religious claims.

Jerusalem
An ancient city in the Middle East, capital of Israel, holy to three faiths

Jerusalem (called Yerushalayim in Hebrew, Al-Quds in Arabic) is built on hills overlooking a desert valley. Archaeological evidence shows continuous inhabitation for 5,000+ years. Today, roughly 1 million people live in Jerusalem; the Old City is a UNESCO World Heritage site.

  • How can different faiths share the same sacred spaces?

  • Is Jerusalem a place of peace or a source of conflict?

Encounter: Three Sacred Sites in One City

In Jerusalem's Old City, three major pilgrimage sites stand as symbols of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. They are geographically close—sometimes separated by only a few hundred meters—yet represent distinct religious traditions and claims.

The Western Wall (Judaism)

Location: The western edge of the Temple Mount, in the Old City of Jerusalem

Historical Significance: The Western Wall is the only remaining wall of the Second Jewish Temple, which was destroyed by Romans in 70 CE. The Temple was the centre of Jewish religious life and the holiest site in Judaism.

Religious Significance: Jews believe God's presence (the Shekinah) dwelt in the Temple. Even though the building is destroyed, the wall remains sacred. Pilgrims believe prayers offered at the wall are especially powerful, as they are closest to where the Temple stood.

Pilgrimage Practices: Pilgrims approach the wall, place their hands against the ancient stones, and pray silently or aloud. Many insert written prayers into cracks in the wall. Men and women pray in separate sections, following Jewish law. Pilgrims often wear prayer shawls and phylacteries (leather boxes containing Torah passages).

Annual Visitors: Approximately 3 million pilgrims visit the Western Wall each year. The wall is a living place of prayer, active 24/7.

Church of the Holy Sepulchre (Christianity)

Location: In the Christian Quarter of the Old City, a few hundred meters from the Western Wall

Historical Significance: The church marks the site where Christian tradition holds that Jesus was crucified and rose from the dead. Built in the 4th century by the Roman Emperor Constantine, it is one of Christianity's holiest sites.

Religious Significance: For Christians, Jesus's crucifixion and resurrection are the foundation of faith. Standing in the place where these events occurred deepens spiritual connection. The church houses the Holy Sepulchre (Jesus's tomb) and Golgotha (the crucifixion site).

Pilgrimage Practices: Pilgrims enter the church, pray before the tomb, touch the stone where Jesus's body lay, and often experience profound emotional moments. Many walk the Stations of the Cross (retracing Jesus's final journey). Some pilgrims cry, prostrate themselves, or spend hours in prayer.

Annual Visitors: Approximately 3-4 million Christian pilgrims visit annually. During Easter, the church is packed to capacity.

Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa Mosque (Islam)

Location: The Temple Mount (called Haram al-Sharif in Arabic), which overlooks the Old City

Historical Significance: The Dome of the Rock is an Islamic shrine (not a mosque) built in 691 CE to commemorate the spot where Muslim tradition holds Prophet Muhammad ascended to heaven. The Al-Aqsa Mosque, adjacent to it, is one of Islam's three holiest sites.

Religious Significance: The rock beneath the Dome is believed to be where Muhammad stood when the Archangel Gabriel took him on the Night Journey (Isra and Mi'raj). At the rock, Muhammad allegedly met with previous prophets (Abraham, Moses, Jesus) in heaven. For Muslims, this is a sign that Islam connects to and fulfils previous faiths.

Pilgrimage Practices: Muslim pilgrims pray in the mosque, walk around the rock, and experience the site's spiritual atmosphere. The site is decorated with intricate Islamic calligraphy and geometric patterns. Pilgrims maintain ritual purity and remove shoes.

Annual Visitors: Millions of Muslim pilgrims visit, especially during Islamic holidays.

Explore: Shared Sacred Space

Jerusalem is unique because three major world religions claim the same city as sacred. This creates both profound opportunities for dialogue and significant tensions.

How Faiths Overlap in Jerusalem

Judaism

Key Events: Abraham brought Isaac; King David made Jerusalem capital; Temple built.

Sacred Location: The Temple Mount (though Jews currently cannot pray there).

Pilgrimage Destination: Western Wall, tomb sites of biblical figures.

Christianity

Key Events: Jesus's birth (Bethlehem, near Jerusalem), crucifixion, resurrection.

Sacred Location: Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Mount of Olives.

Pilgrimage Destination: Sites where Jesus taught and died.

Islam

Key Events: Muhammad's Night Journey; connection to Abraham and Moses.

Sacred Location: The Temple Mount (Haram al-Sharif), Dome of the Rock.

Pilgrimage Destination: Third holiest site in Islam (after Mecca and Medina).

Common Ground and Conflict

Common Beliefs: All three faiths believe Abraham is a father figure. All recognize Jerusalem as central to God's covenant with humanity. All see the city as a place where the divine has intervened in history. All believe God's presence is especially accessible in Jerusalem.
Overlapping Claims: The Temple Mount is revered by all three faiths but is controlled by Muslims. Jews cannot pray there (Islamic law). Christians have limited access. Disputes over who has authority over the site have sparked conflicts throughout history and today. The Western Wall, Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and Dome of the Rock are metres apart, yet represent separate religious territories.

Historical Timeline of Conflict and Coexistence

586 BCE: Babylonian Exile

Jews exiled from Jerusalem; the Temple destroyed. Jews maintained connection to Jerusalem spiritually for centuries despite physical absence.

70 CE: Roman Destruction

Romans destroy the Second Temple. Jews again lose the Temple but preserve the Western Wall, becoming a pilgrimage site.

4th-5th Centuries CE: Christian Prominence

Emperor Constantine builds churches, including the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Christianity becomes dominant religion of Jerusalem.

637 CE: Islamic Conquest

Muslim forces conquer Jerusalem. The Dome of the Rock is built in 691 CE. For centuries, Muslims, Christians, and Jews coexist, though Muslims hold political authority. Pilgrimage is permitted.

1099-1187: Crusader Period

European crusaders capture Jerusalem. Violence against Muslims and Jews. In 1187, Saladin recaptures the city and generally restores coexistence.

1948-Present: Modern Conflict

Israel founded; Jerusalem becomes disputed territory. Wars in 1948, 1967, 1973. Israel gains control of West Jerusalem and the Old City in 1967. The city remains a flashpoint of Israeli-Palestinian conflict. Different religious communities sometimes clash over access to sacred sites.

Complexity Today: Jerusalem remains contested. Different populations have competing claims and rights. Religious tensions coexist with political conflict. Yet daily life shows coexistence: Muslim, Christian, and Jewish shopkeepers work in the same markets; pilgrims of different faiths pray in close proximity. Peacebuilding efforts focus on interfaith dialogue and shared recognition of all three traditions' sacredness.

Apply: Pilgrimage and Coexistence

How Pilgrims Experience Shared Sacred Space

Interfaith Encounters

In the Old City, pilgrims from different faiths naturally encounter each other. A Christian visiting the Church of the Holy Sepulchre walks past the Dome of the Rock. A Jewish pilgrim at the Western Wall is metres from Muslim-controlled territory. These encounters can create understanding or tension, depending on context and individuals involved.

Mutual Respect for Sacred Sites

Despite historical conflicts, many pilgrims and locals show respect for sites sacred to other faiths. Christian pilgrims remove shoes in the Dome of the Rock. Muslim guards may allow Christian pilgrims to photograph sacred areas. Jewish pilgrims often acknowledge Christian and Muslim pilgrimage. Such respect is crucial to coexistence.

Spiritual Tourism vs Pilgrimage

Not all visitors to Jerusalem are pilgrims. Some are spiritual tourists—curious visitors without deep religious commitment. This can cause tension, as pilgrims may feel sacred sites are being disrespected. Walking in religious dress, speaking quietly, removing shoes—these are signs of pilgrim respect, not tourist behaviour.

Barriers to Access and Tensions

Religious Control: Each faith controls its own sites. Jews cannot freely access the Temple Mount. Christians have limited times to visit the Dome of the Rock. Muslims cannot enter Jewish prayer areas. These restrictions reflect political reality and religious law but also create barriers to shared understanding.
Archaeological Disputes: Different faiths claim control of sacred ground beneath the city. Excavations risk damaging sites sacred to other religions. Archaeological discoveries can inflame tensions if they seem to validate one faith's claims over another's.
Political Control: Religious conflict in Jerusalem is inseparable from political conflict. Israeli and Palestinian claims to the same land create tensions that overflow into religious sphere. Pilgrimage access depends on political circumstances (wars, blockades, restrictions). When violence erupts, pilgrimage becomes dangerous or impossible.

Reflection: Can Sacred Spaces Foster Peace?

🔒 Consider both sides.

Optimistic view: Sacred spaces can foster understanding when pilgrims of different faiths encounter each other with respect. Interfaith dialogue and shared recognition of each site's holiness can build peace.

Realistic view: Sacred sites can also intensify conflict when different groups believe they have exclusive rights. Politics often override religious respect. Pilgrimage can be an escape from conflict or a reminder of why conflict exists.

Evaluate: Sharing Sacred Space in Conflict

Models for Coexistence

The Status Quo: Currently, each faith controls its own major sites. The Temple Mount is under Muslim authority; Christians control the Holy Sepulchre (shared by different denominations); Jews maintain the Western Wall. This separation prevents constant conflict but also limits shared access and mutual pilgrimage.
Interfaith Initiatives: Organizations like the Jerusalem Interfaith Encounter Centre bring together Jews, Christians, and Muslims for dialogue. Some religious leaders publicly affirm each faith's right to sacred sites. Shared meals, study sessions, and joint pilgrimages are attempted.
Pilgrimage as Peacebuilding: Some propose that pilgrimage could foster peace by helping people understand other traditions' spiritual needs. Allowing pilgrims of all faiths to visit all sites (with respect) could create empathy and understanding.

Challenges and Questions

Competing Historical Claims: All three faiths have long histories in Jerusalem. All can point to ancient texts and archaeological evidence. No single narrative satisfies all. How do societies acknowledge multiple legitimate historical claims?
Political Resolution First? Can religious coexistence succeed when political conflict persists? Or must political peace come first? Currently, religious and political issues are deeply intertwined. Separating them may be impossible.
Minority Rights: What about smaller religious communities (smaller Christian denominations, Samaritans, Bahá'ís)? How do societies balance majority claims with minority rights? Jerusalem shows the complexity of managing sacred space in pluralistic societies.

Critical Thinking: Is Shared Sacred Space Possible?

🔒 Consider different perspectives.

Religious Optimists: All three faiths share belief in one God and coexist in many places. With goodwill and interfaith dialogue, shared sacred space is possible.

Political Realists: Until Israeli-Palestinian political conflict is resolved, religious coexistence will remain fragile. Sacred space becomes a proxy for larger political disputes.

Practical Pragmatists: Complete sharing may be unrealistic. Instead, separate spaces with guaranteed access and mutual respect for other sites may be the realistic model forward.

Review: What You've Learned

Key Takeaways

  • Jerusalem is the only major city sacred to three world religions: Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Each faith has deep spiritual roots in the city and cannot abandon it.
  • The Western Wall is the holiest Jewish site — the remaining wall of the Temple where God dwelled. Millions of Jewish pilgrims pray there annually.
  • The Church of the Holy Sepulchre is Christianity's holiest site — marking where Jesus was crucified and rose. Pilgrims come for resurrection faith and spiritual encounter.
  • The Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa Mosque are Islam's third holiest sites — marking where Muhammad ascended to heaven. They represent Islam's connection to Abraham and previous prophets.
  • The three sacred sites are geographically close but religiously separate. Muslims control the Temple Mount; Christians control the Holy Sepulchre; Jews have access to the Western Wall. This separation both prevents conflict and limits shared understanding.
  • Jerusalem's history shows cycles of conflict and coexistence. Crusades, conquests, and modern wars have disrupted peace, but long periods of religious coexistence also occurred.
  • Jerusalem today is contested territory. Israeli-Palestinian political conflict is inseparable from religious claims. Pilgrimage access depends on political circumstances.
  • Interfaith dialogue and mutual respect are essential for coexistence. Understanding each faith's pilgrimage needs and sacred space claims is crucial for peace.
