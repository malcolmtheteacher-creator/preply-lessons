The Remarkable Rocket

Oscar Wilde's story of a firework who never once suspects that nobody is looking.

Need an easier version? Read this story at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll dissect the most self-absorbed character in English literature — and you'll learn how English puts weight where it wants it: What ruined him was his own tears. It was the goose who noticed.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Where is the line between healthy self-belief and self-importance? Can you state a test that works?
  • Why do people perform emotions they do not have — grief, outrage, sensitivity? What is the pay-off?
  • Does an audience make an achievement real? Would you still do the thing if nobody could ever know?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. He spoke for twenty minutes about his distinguished family and asked not a single question of anybody else. His vanity was almost a natural phenomenon.
vanity (n) — excessive pride in oneself. Adjective: vain. Distinguish vain (proud) from in vain (without success).
2. He wept freely over a tragedy that had not occurred, to a family that did not exist, and considered the performance proof of his refined nature. The display was entirely self-indulgent.
self-indulgent (adj) — allowing yourself pleasure or emotion without restraint or purpose. Noun: self-indulgence.
3. The other fireworks answered him politely, then briefly, then not at all, and finally turned their attention to the weather. They were pointedly unimpressed.
unimpressed (adj) — not admiring; not convinced. Note the useful understatement: "She was unimpressed" often means she was extremely annoyed.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: delusion · condescending · oblivious. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means a fixed belief that is plainly false, held despite all evidence?
a delusion (n) — "He was under the delusion that everyone admired him." Adjective: deluded. Compare an illusion, which is about perception rather than belief.
2. Which word means treating others as inferior while appearing to be polite?
condescending (adj) — "a condescending smile". Verb: to condescend to someone. Near neighbour: patronising.
3. Which word means completely unaware of what is happening around you?
oblivious (adj) — usually with to or of: "oblivious to the silence around him". Noun: oblivion, which is also where the Rocket ends up.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. "You are extremely ill-mannered," said the Rocket. "You have not asked me one question about myself." Ill-mannered means…
  (a) unwell   (b) rude   (c) badly educated
(b) — ill-mannered (adj) = rude. Note the productive prefix: ill-informed, ill-advised, ill-prepared, ill-judged.
2. His final explosion was a complete anticlimax: a bang in broad daylight, with the audience asleep. An anticlimax is…
  (a) a disappointing end after a big build-up   (b) a violent argument   (c) an unexpected success
(a) — an anticlimax (n) — the deflation after expectation. Adjective: anticlimactic. It is the shape of this entire story.

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know to boast. Give the adjective, and the informal noun for a person who does it constantly. Then complete: he boasted ___ his family (preposition).
boastful (adj) · informally a show-off · he boasted about (or of) his family. Formal near-synonym: to vaunt.
2. The title calls him remarkable. Break the word apart: what verb is inside it, and what does it literally mean to be remarkable? Now explain the joke of the title.
Inside it is remark — to be remarkable is literally to be worth remarking on, worth mentioning. The joke: the only person who ever remarks on him is himself.

  • Sort today's words into those you would say to someone's face and those you would only say behind their back. What does that tell you about register?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The conversation on the table

The King's son was to be married, and the country had been celebrating for a week. The bride had travelled from Finland in a sledge drawn by six reindeer; the wedding breakfast had lasted five hours; and the whole business was to conclude with a display of fireworks in the palace garden at nine o'clock precisely.

The fireworks, laid out on a long table, discussed the occasion among themselves. The little Squib admired the gardens. The Roman Candle preferred the moon. The Catherine Wheel, who had loved and lost, sighed at the mention of romance. And then a tall Rocket at the end of the table cleared his throat and explained that they were all extraordinarily fortunate, since it was perfectly evident that the Prince and Princess had arranged to be married on the very day that he was to be let off.

He went on. He came, he said, of a distinguished family; his mother had been the most celebrated Catherine Wheel of her generation; and when he rose into the sky he confidently expected to create a great sensation. The other fireworks attempted to change the subject to the weather, which the Rocket identified as proof of their poor breeding: not one of them had asked him a further question about himself. He was, he added, a firework of unusually sensitive feelings — and by way of demonstration he began to weep at the thought of a misfortune that might one day befall the Prince's as yet unborn son. The tears rolled down his stick like drops of rain.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the occasion, and how does the Rocket account for the date?

2. How does he demonstrate his sensitivity, and what is the physical consequence?

1. A royal wedding concluding with a firework display. He concludes that the royal couple must have chosen the date to coincide with his own performance.

2. He weeps over an imaginary future tragedy involving a child who does not exist. The result is that he soaks himself.

Discuss:
  • He manufactures grief for an audience and calls the result sensitivity. What is the modern version of that performance, and does it have the same consequence?

Part 2 — Nine o'clock, and afterwards

At nine o'clock precisely the display began. The Catherine Wheel turned; the Roman Candle threw up its gold stars; the Squib danced; the crowd shouted; the Princess laughed; and the sky above the palace filled with light and noise until the garden was as bright as noon.

One firework alone failed to go off. The Rocket was too thoroughly wet with his own tears to take a light. He lay on the table, damp and silent, while the event he had been designed for occurred without him and concluded without him, and nobody, at any point in the evening, noticed that he was missing. In the morning the gardeners came out, found a stick lying among the burnt paper, remarked that it was clearly a bad rocket, and threw it over the wall into a ditch.

He landed in the mud and lay on his back looking at the sky. This, he decided, was evidently a fashionable watering-place; he had been sent here to recover his health, being of a delicate constitution, and no doubt the court was already gravely concerned about him. A Frog swam past and talked about rain. A Dragon-fly hovered and enquired who he was. A Duck came by and offered practical advice about the value of making oneself useful. To each of them in turn he explained, at length, that he was a Remarkable Rocket. Each of them, in turn, departed in the middle of the explanation.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the Rocket miss the display, and how is his absence received?

2. How does he interpret being thrown into a ditch?

1. He is too wet with his own tears to light. Nobody notices his absence at all; the gardeners later dismiss him as a bad rocket.

2. As evidence of his importance: a fashionable place, sent to recover his delicate health, with the court anxiously enquiring after him.

Discuss:
  • Three creatures leave mid-sentence. Why does Wilde repeat the same joke three times instead of once — what does repetition do that a single instance could not?

Part 3 — "I knew I should create a great sensation"

Some days later two small boys came down to the ditch to light a fire and cook something over it. They wanted dry material, and one of them noticed the stick lying in the mud. "This will do," he said, and threw it on the flames. "What an excellent stick."

The Rocket was delighted. Plainly he was to be let off in broad daylight, before an enormous audience, precisely as he had always deserved. He resolved to go higher than any rocket in history, so high that people would speak of the occasion for the rest of their lives, and would name their children after it.

The fire warmed him through, and at last the powder inside him caught. He went off with a magnificent bang. But it was the middle of the day, so the flash was invisible; the two boys had fallen asleep beside the fire, so the bang was unheard; and the only part of him that came down landed on a goose walking near the ditch, who observed that it was raining and made for the water. The last thing the Rocket said, as he shot into the wide empty daylight, was that he had known all along that he would create a great sensation. And then nothing whatever happened, anywhere at all.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How is the Rocket finally set off, and what does he expect to happen?

2. Account for each of the three reasons his moment fails.

1. Two boys use him as firewood. He believes he is being launched before a vast audience and will be remembered for generations.

2. It is daylight, so nothing is seen; the boys are asleep, so nothing is heard; and the only fragment that lands falls on a goose, who assumes it is rain.

Discuss:
  • Wilde denies him even a moment of understanding at the end. Is that crueller than punishment, or kinder?

Grammar — emphasis & cleft sentences

The Rocket puts himself at the centre of every sentence he utters. English has precise machinery for deciding what goes at the centre — and it is one of the clearest markers of an advanced speaker.

Cleft sentences split one idea into two clauses so that you can spotlight a part of it. Start from a flat sentence: "His own tears ruined him."
• It-cleft: "It was his own tears that ruined him." (emphasises the cause)
• Wh-cleft: "What ruined him was his own tears." (builds suspense before the reveal)
• All-cleft: "All he wanted was an audience." (implies "and nothing more")
• The reason / place / person: "The reason he failed was that he was wet."
Other ways English adds weight:
• Fronting: "Into the ditch he went." · "Remarkable he certainly was not."
• Emphatic do: "He did go off in the end." · "She does talk a lot."
• Not only… but also with inversion: "Not only did nobody see it, but nobody heard it either."
• The very / precisely / of all people: "the very day he was to be let off" · "a goose, of all creatures"

Careful: in a wh-cleft the verb agrees with what follows: "What he wanted was applause." / "What he wanted were compliments."

1. Rewrite as cleft sentences

Rewrite each flat sentence using the structure given. Write only the missing words.

1. His tears ruined the whole evening. → It ruined the whole evening. (it-cleft)

2. He wanted an audience more than anything. → What an audience. (wh-cleft)

3. A goose noticed the explosion, and nobody else did. → It noticed the explosion. (it-cleft)

1. was his tears that (or "were his tears that" — both are heard; "was" is standard with a cleft)

2. he wanted was   3. was a goose who / that

2. Add the emphasis

Complete each sentence so that it carries the weight described in brackets.

1. he wanted was somebody to listen to him. (and nothing else)

2. he failed was that he had soaked himself in his own tears. (state the cause formally)

3. Not only the flash invisible, but the bang went unheard as well. (inversion after "not only")

1. All   2. The reason   3. was

After "not only" at the start of a clause, the auxiliary comes before the subject — the same inversion as in a question.

3. Analyse the failure — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap so that the paragraph emphasises what it should. Read it aloud when you finish.

made the Rocket ridiculous was not his ambition but his certainty. he ever wanted was to be looked at. was his own performance of feeling made him too wet to light. Not only he miss the display, but he never learned that he had missed it. And reason we remember him at all is that Wilde refused to give him a single moment of self-knowledge.

What · All · It · that · did · the

Read the paragraph again without the clefts and notice how much flatter it is. That difference is the whole point of the structure.

Going deeper

Wilde published this in 1888 in The Happy Prince and Other Tales, a collection written for his two small sons and read chiefly by adults ever since. The stories take the form of fairy tales and behave like satire. It is worth remembering that Wilde was himself the most celebrated talker of his age, which makes this portrait of a compulsive self-publicist rather more interesting than a simple joke at someone else's expense.

The craft. The Rocket is not stupid, and that is the point. He possesses a fully functioning intelligence which has been placed entirely at the service of one proposition: that he is the centre of events. Every contradicting fact is processed and reissued as confirmation — the ditch becomes a health resort, the firewood becomes a stage, the silence becomes admiration. Wilde's structural decision is the severe one: no recognition, ever. There is no moment of humility, no chastening, not even a flicker of doubt as he goes up. He exits insisting on his sensation while a goose concludes it is raining. And notice the precision of the cruelty in the timing: he does finally perform, magnificently, at the one moment in the entire story when there is nobody to see. The question the tale leaves behind has aged remarkably well — what exactly is a performance worth when the entire audience is inside the performer's head?

Talk it through:
  • Wilde denies him any self-knowledge whatever. Would the story be improved by one moment of doubt, or would that ruin it?
  • The other fireworks are polite, then bored, then absent. Is that how vanity is actually punished — by people quietly withdrawing?
  • He weeps for an invented tragedy to demonstrate his depth of feeling. Where do you see that today, and is the modern version more or less honest?
  • Is this satire aimed at one absurd character, or at something Wilde recognised in himself and in his audience?

Task A · Narrate the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell The Remarkable Rocket as if to someone who has never read it. Move through the arc: the royal wedding → the fireworks in conversation → the Rocket's family and his sensitive nature → the tears → nine o'clock, and the display he misses → the gardeners and the ditch → the Frog, the Dragon-fly and the Duck → the two boys and the fire → the goose.

Use at least two cleft sentences and at least five key words (vanity, self-indulgent, unimpressed, delusion, condescending, oblivious, ill-mannered, anticlimax, boastful, remarkable).

Task B · Is self-promotion a necessity or a disease? (4 minutes)

Build an argument. One position: you must advertise yourself to get anything — a job, a place, an audience — and modesty is simply losing quietly. The other: the Rocket is precisely what that logic produces, and the story is a warning. Take a side, give three reasons, deal with the strongest objection, and finish with concrete advice for someone your age about talking up their own achievements.

Then argue the opposite for one minute, using at least one cleft sentence.

Task C · Audiences, real and imagined (4 minutes)

Take the story's subject outward.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Deliver sixty seconds as the Rocket, introducing yourself as the most remarkable person present. Your partner replies as the Duck.
  • Sell something entirely ordinary — a pencil, a bus stop, a wet Tuesday — as a sensation, then describe it honestly.
  • Advertising runs on superlatives. Which claims do you actually believe, and what makes the difference?
  • Is an achievement diminished if nobody sees it? Argue with a concrete case.
  • Describe a public figure who performs feelings well. Are they insincere, or is performance part of the job?
  • Can you be proud of something without boasting? Demonstrate: say the same fact twice, two different ways.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. He finally performs brilliantly and nobody perceives it. Does the performance count?
  2. Every creature he meets is more useful than he is. Is Wilde arguing for usefulness, or laughing at that idea too?
  3. A century before anyone had a phone, Wilde described a character performing emotion for an audience that does not exist. What did he see so early, and in whom?
