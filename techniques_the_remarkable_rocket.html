SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B1/B2 · 50 min

The Remarkable Rocket

Oscar Wilde's story of a firework who is absolutely certain that everyone is talking about him.

Today: you'll tell the story of the vainest character in English literature — and you'll learn to exaggerate like a native speaker: by far the best, easily the worst, the single most ridiculous thing I've ever heard.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • What's the difference between confidence and thinking you're better than everyone else? Where exactly is the line?
  • Think of someone who talks constantly about themselves. How do people around them actually react?
  • Does wanting attention make someone ridiculous, or just human?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. He spoke for twenty minutes about his own importance, and never once asked anyone else a question. His vanity was extraordinary.
vanity (n) — excessive pride in yourself, your appearance or your abilities. Adjective: vain.
2. The other fireworks stopped talking, looked at each other, and one of them laughed out loud. Nobody was impressed; they simply thought he was absurd.
absurd (adj) — so unreasonable that it becomes ridiculous. "Don't be absurd." Noun: absurdity.
3. He lay in the wet mud at the bottom of the ditch, cold and dirty, with water soaking into him from every side. He was thoroughly damp.
damp (adj) — slightly wet, in an unpleasant way. Houses, clothes and weather can all be damp.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: ditch · sensation · court. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means a long narrow channel dug at the side of a road or field, usually holding water?
a ditch (n) — "They threw the stick into the ditch."
2. Which word means a great excitement among a lot of people, all at once?
a sensation (n) — "The news caused a sensation." Adjective: sensational.
3. Which word means a king or queen's household — the palace and all the people around the ruler?
the court (n) — "The whole court attended the wedding." (The same word also means a place of law, and a place for tennis.)

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. "You are extremely ill-mannered," said the Rocket. "You have not asked me a single question about myself." Ill-mannered means…
  (a) unwell   (b) rude, with bad manners   (c) badly dressed
(b) — ill-mannered (adj) = rude. The opposite is well-mannered. Note the pattern: ill-informed, ill-prepared, ill-advised.
2. The Rocket believed he had a sensitive nature, and wept at sad things that had not happened. Sensitive means…
  (a) feeling emotions strongly and easily   (b) very intelligent   (c) extremely quiet
(a) — sensitive (adj) = easily affected by feelings (your own or other people's). Careful: sensible means something completely different — practical and reasonable.

D · Word building & word partners

1. You know to boast. Which noun goes with it — a person who boasts is a ___? And which adjective describes their behaviour?
a boaster (or, more common today, a show-off) · boastful (adj). "He boasted about his marks all week."
2. One adjective fits all three: a ___ achievement · a ___ view · nothing ___ happened today. It's in the title of this story.
remarkable = unusual enough to be worth remarking on, i.e. worth mentioning. Adverb: remarkably. The joke of the title is that he is remarkable only in his own opinion.

Now use them — out loud

Talk it through before we read:
  • Using vanity, boastful, absurd and sensation, predict what happens to a firework who believes the royal wedding is really about him.
  • Which of today's words are compliments, which are insults, and which depend entirely on your tone? Sort them out loud.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The fireworks talk

The King's son was to be married, and the whole country had been celebrating for a week. The bride had travelled from Finland in a sledge drawn by six reindeer, and the wedding was to end with a great display of fireworks in the palace garden at nine o'clock exactly.

The fireworks were laid out on a table, waiting, and they talked among themselves. The little Squib described the beauty of the palace gardens. The Roman Candle spoke about the moon. The Catherine Wheel, who had loved and lost, sighed about romance. And then a tall Rocket at the end of the table cleared his throat and explained that they were all extremely fortunate, because it was obvious that the Prince and Princess had chosen to be married on the very day that he was to be let off.

It got better. He explained that he came from a distinguished family, that his mother had been the most celebrated Catherine Wheel of her day, and that when he went up into the sky he expected to cause a genuine sensation. The other fireworks tried to talk about the weather. This, he told them, was proof of how ill-mannered they were: nobody had asked him a single further question about himself. He was, he added, a firework of unusually sensitive feelings — and to demonstrate it, he began to weep at the thought of a tragedy that might one day happen to the Prince's future son. The tears rolled down his stick like rain.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is being celebrated, and what is the Rocket's explanation of the date?

2. How does the Rocket prove that he has a sensitive nature — and what is the practical result?

1. A royal wedding, ending with a firework display. The Rocket announces that the royal couple must have arranged the wedding for the day he was going to be let off.

2. He weeps over an imaginary future tragedy that has not happened to anyone. The result is that he makes himself thoroughly wet.

Discuss:
  • He cries over a sadness he has invented, and calls it sensitivity. Have you seen people perform feelings like that? What are they really doing?

Part 2 — Nine o'clock

At nine o'clock exactly, the display began. The Catherine Wheel went round and round. The Roman Candle threw gold stars. The Squib danced. The crowd shouted, the Princess laughed, and the sky above the palace filled with colour and noise until the whole garden was as bright as day.

Only one firework did not go off. The Rocket was too wet with his own tears to catch light at all. He lay there, damp and silent, while everything he had planned to be part of happened above him and finished without him. Nobody noticed. Nobody said his name. The gardeners came out the next morning, found a stick lying on the table, and said, "This is a bad rocket." Then they threw him over the wall into a ditch.

He landed in the mud and lay on his back looking at the sky. This, he decided, was clearly a very distinguished place. They had probably sent him here to recover, since he was known to be delicate, and no doubt the court was already anxious about him. A frog swam past and talked about rain. A dragonfly hovered and asked who he was. A duck came by and gave him practical advice about the value of being useful. He explained to each of them, at length, that he was a remarkable rocket. All three of them left in the middle of his explanation.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the Rocket miss the display entirely?

2. How does he explain being thrown into a ditch?

1. He is too wet to light — the tears he produced to prove his sensitivity have ruined him. The display goes ahead without him and nobody notices he is missing.

2. He decides it is a distinguished place where he has been sent to recover, and that the court must be worried about his health.

Discuss:
  • Three creatures leave while he is still talking. Why does Wilde repeat the same joke three times instead of once?

Part 3 — "I knew I should create a great sensation"

After some days two small boys came down to the ditch to light a fire and cook something. They needed something dry to burn, and one of them spotted the stick lying in the mud. "This will do," he said, and threw it on the flames. "What an excellent stick."

The Rocket was delighted. Clearly he was about to be let off in broad daylight, in front of an enormous audience, exactly as he had always deserved. He decided that he would go up higher than any rocket had gone before, and that people would talk about this moment for the rest of their lives.

The fire warmed him through, and at last the gunpowder inside him caught. He went off with a splendid bang. But it was the middle of the day, so nobody saw the light; the two boys had fallen asleep beside the fire, so nobody heard the noise; and the only piece of him that came down landed on a goose, who was walking near the ditch and thought it was raining. The very last thing the Rocket said, as he shot into the bright empty sky, was that he knew he would create a great sensation. And then nothing at all happened, anywhere.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does the Rocket finally go off, and what does he expect?

2. Who sees it, who hears it, and where does he land?

1. Two boys use him as firewood. He believes he is being let off in daylight before a huge audience and expects to be remembered forever.

2. Nobody sees the flash because it is daytime; the boys are asleep so nobody hears the bang; and the only piece of him falls on a goose who assumes it is rain.

Discuss:
  • He dies exactly as he lived — completely certain and completely wrong. Is that ending funny, sad, or both at once?

Grammar — superlatives & exaggeration

The Rocket speaks almost entirely in superlatives, and that is the joke. But used well, this is the language of strong opinions — and you need it every time you recommend, complain or argue.

Forming superlatives:
• Short adjectives: the loudest, the brightest, the wettest (double the consonant after a short vowel).
• Long adjectives: the most beautiful, the most ridiculous.
• Irregular: good → the best · bad → the worst · far → the furthest.
• Always with the, and often followed by in (a place) or of (a group): "the tallest building in Europe", "the best of the three".
Strengthening a superlative — the natural phrases:
• by far / easily: "That was by far the best concert I've been to."
• the single most…: "the single most useful app on my phone"
• one of the + superlative + plural noun: "one of the strangest films I've ever seen" (note the plural!)
• the + superlative + I've ever + past participle: "the funniest thing I've ever heard"

And to pull back from an exaggeration: "…or at least one of the best" · "I might be overstating it, but…" · "honestly, it wasn't that bad."

1. Build the superlative

Write the correct superlative form of the adjective in brackets.

1. The Rocket believed he was (important) firework on the table.

2. In the end, his life was (bad) possible advertisement for talking about yourself.

3. He was certainly (wet) of all the fireworks that evening.

1. the most important   2. the worst   3. the wettest

Note the doubled consonant in wet → wettest, and that "bad" is irregular.

2. Say it like a speaker — add the strengthener

Complete each sentence with two or three words so that it sounds natural.

1. That firework display was the best I have ever seen. (add emphasis in two words)

2. He is most self-important character in the whole story. (three words, beginning "the")

3. It was one of (ridiculous) speeches anyone had ever made. (careful — what must the noun be?)

1. by far / easily   2. the single most   3. the most ridiculous speeches (after "one of the", the noun is plural)

3. Retell the disaster — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct form, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

The Rocket announced that he came from (distinguished) family of fireworks in the country and that his mother had been (celebrated) Catherine Wheel of her day. He was, he said, (sensitive) firework on the table. This turned out to be (dangerous) thing about him, because his tears made him too wet to light. He was (far) the only firework that failed that night, and his final explosion was one of (quiet) endings in literature.

the most distinguished · the most celebrated · the most sensitive · the most dangerous · by far · the quietest endings

Notice the last one: after "one of the" the noun goes plural.

Hold on to these phrases — in the Speak tab you'll use them for real opinions, and then deliberately overdo them.

Going deeper

Wilde published this in 1888, in a collection he wrote for his own two small sons. The stories look like fairy tales and behave like satire: they are funny on the surface and quietly serious underneath. Wilde himself was famous for his talk, which makes this portrait of a compulsive talker rather more interesting.

The heart of it. The Rocket is not stupid. He is something more recognisable: he has no way of seeing a situation that does not put him at the centre of it. Every fact that contradicts him gets rewritten on the spot — the ditch becomes a rest cure, the firewood becomes a performance, the silence becomes admiration. Notice that Wilde never lets him learn anything. There is no moment of understanding, no humility at the end; he goes out insisting he has created a sensation while a goose thinks it is raining. And notice the cruelty of the timing: he does finally go off, spectacularly, at the exact moment when there is nobody to see it. The story asks a question that has aged extremely well — what is a performance worth if the audience is entirely inside your own head?

Talk it through:
  • Wilde gives him no moment of self-knowledge at all. Would the story be better or worse if the Rocket realised the truth at the end?
  • The other fireworks are polite, then bored, then gone. Is that how vanity is usually punished in real life — by people quietly leaving?
  • He weeps for a tragedy that has not happened, to prove how deeply he feels. Where do you see that behaviour today?
  • Is this story about one ridiculous character, or about something the writer suspected in himself?

Speak

This is the heart of the lesson — do all of it out loud. The timer is there to keep you going: fill the time with detail rather than racing through it.

Useful language: lean on today's structures — by far the best…, the single most…, one of the strangest… I've ever seen, easily the worst…. And to soften: or at least one of them, I might be exaggerating, but….

Task A · Tell the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell The Remarkable Rocket in your own words. Move through the arc: the royal wedding → the fireworks talking on the table → the Rocket's family and his sensitive nature → the tears → nine o'clock and the display he misses → the gardeners and the ditch → the frog, the dragonfly and the duck → the two boys and the fire → the goose.

Use at least three superlatives and at least five key words (vanity, absurd, damp, ditch, sensation, court, ill-mannered, sensitive, boastful, remarkable).

Task B · Is confidence the same as vanity? (4 minutes)

Take a side and argue it properly. Some people say you have to talk yourself up to get anywhere — a job, a team, a place at university. Others say the Rocket is exactly what that produces. Give three reasons, answer the strongest objection to your view, and finish with practical advice for someone your age: how much should you talk about your own achievements?

Then argue the opposite side for one minute.

Task C · Superlatives, honest and dishonest (4 minutes)

Use today's language for real — and then deliberately overdo it.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Give an honest "best and worst" list: best film, worst journey, most useless object you own — with a reason for each.
  • Sell something ordinary — a pencil, a chair, a rainy Tuesday — as if it were the greatest thing on earth. Then describe it honestly.
  • Be the Rocket for one minute: introduce yourself to the class as the most remarkable person in the room. Your partner then replies as the duck, with practical advice.
  • Advertising is built on superlatives. Which claims do you actually believe, and which do you automatically ignore?
  • Describe a celebration in your country: the best part, the most exhausting part, the single most important moment.
  • Is it possible to be proud of something without boasting about it? Show us the difference by saying the same thing twice, two ways.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The Rocket finally goes off, brilliantly, with nobody watching. Does something count if nobody sees it?
  2. Every creature he meets is more useful than he is — the duck, the boys, even the goose. Is Wilde arguing for usefulness, or laughing at that idea too?
  3. A hundred and forty years before anyone had a phone, Wilde described somebody performing his feelings for an imaginary audience. Why did he see that so early?
  4. Before you go — from memory: make one sentence with by far the, one with one of the + superlative, and one with the most … I've ever — and explain why "one of the best film" is wrong.
