MINDS WORTH MEETING · C1 · 50 min

Albert Camus

The French-Algerian writer who stared at a meaningless universe and decided the only honest answer was to live anyway — fully, defiantly, without false hope.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Look at his name and the line under this lesson's title: a man who “stared at a meaningless universe and decided the only honest answer was to live anyway”. What kind of writer do you imagine he was? And have you read anything by him — what pulled you in, or kept you away? Just talk.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

Three short facts. After each one, you do the talking.

Camus was born in 1913 in French Algeria. His father died in the First World War before Camus was a year old, and he grew up poor, raised by a nearly deaf, illiterate mother in a cramped flat in Algiers. The brilliant Mediterranean light, the heat, and the silence of that household never left his writing.

Over to you:
  • A childhood of poverty, silence and dazzling sunlight — which of those three do you think marks a writer most, and why?

The war turned the thinker into a man of action. During the Nazi occupation he edited Combat, an underground Resistance newspaper — dangerous, anonymous work that could have cost him his life. He became one of Europe's most famous writers and, in 1957 at just 43, one of the youngest ever winners of the Nobel Prize in Literature. Three years later he was killed in a car crash, aged 46, with an unused train ticket in his pocket.

Over to you:
  • He risked his life in secret, then became world-famous, then died by pure accident with a train ticket he never used. Does that life feel like it proves his ideas — or quietly mocks them?

He is often filed under “existentialism”, but he rejected the label — and he broke, publicly and bitterly, with Jean-Paul Sartre over politics and violence. Camus was his own thing: a writer who distrusted easy systems and kept asking one stubborn, almost childlike question — given that life has no built-in meaning, how, then, should we live?

He believed the universe offers us no answers — and that facing that silence honestly, without lying to ourselves, was the beginning of a kind of freedom.

Over to you:
  • He refused the “existentialist” box and fell out with his closest ally over it. Why might a thinker resist a label that would have made his life easier?

The Absurd — and you do the thinking out loud

Camus has one central idea, and almost everything he wrote circles back to it: the Absurd. I'll hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I'll mostly listen.

We humans seem built to crave meaning — we want the universe to make sense, to be fair, to add up to something. But when we look honestly, it gives us nothing back: no purpose written into the stars, no cosmic justice, no answer to “why”. Camus called the Absurd not the silence by itself, nor our craving by itself, but the collision between the two.

Over to you:
  • Do you actually feel that gap between wanting meaning and a silent universe — or does life feel meaningful enough to you as it is? Be honest.

Most people, he thought, escape that collision rather than face it. Some leap into religion or grand ideology, swapping the hard silence for a comforting story — Camus called this “philosophical suicide”, because it kills the honest question. Others, in despair, consider literal suicide. Camus opens his most famous essay by declaring this the one truly serious philosophical problem, then sets out to find a third way: neither false hope nor surrender.

Over to you:
  • He calls leaping into faith or ideology “philosophical suicide”. Is that fair — or is finding meaning in a belief simply a different, equally valid kind of courage?

His third way is the move that makes Camus Camus. He reaches for Sisyphus — condemned to roll a boulder uphill forever, only to watch it roll back. The perfect image of pointless labour. And yet Camus tells us to picture him walking back down to start again, and to imagine that, in that moment, he is happy. The answer to a meaningless universe is not despair and not false hope, but revolt: to keep living fully and lucidly, refusing both to lie about the silence and to be crushed by it. Meaning is not handed down — it is made, here, in the struggle itself.

The struggle towards the heights is itself enough to fill a person's heart — so we must imagine Sisyphus happy.

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

“We must imagine Sisyphus happy.” Argue for it or against it — is that profound, or just putting a brave face on a hopeless situation? Bring a real example from a life you know, build your case, then I'll push back and you defend it.

One more thing — pick your next Camus

He rang the same bell — the Absurd, and how to live with it — in very different ways. Glance at these, then tell me which one you would actually read, and why that one.

WorkWhat it isThe idea underneath
The Stranger / L'Étranger (1942)A short, cold novel about Meursault, a man who feels nothing at his mother's funeral and later kills a stranger almost by accident.Society condemns him less for the killing than for refusing to fake the emotions everyone expects. The Plague / La Peste (1947)A novel about a city sealed off by an epidemic, and the doctor who keeps treating the sick anyway.Quiet heroism without God or reward: decency as revolt. Often read as an allegory of resistance to occupation and tyranny.
The Rebel / L'Homme révolté (1951)A long essay tracing rebellion and revolution through history.His argument that revolt has limits — that it must not become murder or terror. This is the book that detonated his break with Sartre.
Quick reaction:
  • Which of these would you pick up next, and why?
  • The Plague shows decency as a form of revolt — quietly doing the right thing with no reward and no God watching. Is that a satisfying idea of heroism to you, or does it need something more?

Ten words you need to discuss Camus properly — and that work just as well for any serious conversation about ideas. Tap to reveal the meaning and an example.

absurd / the absurd
adjective / noun

"The absurd is not despair — it is the honest starting point."
to grapple with

adjective / noun
Pointless; certain to fail however hard you try.
"Rolling the boulder looks futile — yet Camus insists on doing it gladly."
lucid / lucidity
adjective / noun
Clear-eyed; seeing reality plainly without comforting illusions.

"For Camus, living fully is an act of defiance against a meaningless world."
indifferent / indifference

profound

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that meaning is something we invent rather than discover.
  • It might well be that revolt is just despair with better manners.
  • I suspect the truth is somewhere in between.
  • You could argue that Camus is smuggling hope back in through the side door.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say facing the silence honestly is the only courage that counts.
  • What strikes me most is how he turns a hopeless image into a happy one.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, his question matters more now than in 1942.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure I fully buy that.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that assume…?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

Three thought experiments — you do the talking

Time remaining: 3:00
A · Your Own Boulder (3–4 min)

Camus tells us to imagine Sisyphus — condemned to push a boulder uphill forever — as happy, because the struggle itself is enough to fill a life. Think about something repetitive in your own world: a commute, a job, raising children, training for something that never quite ends.

Argue one side: can you genuinely find meaning in the repetition itself, the way Camus demands — or is that just a story we tell ourselves to cope? Use a real example from your own life, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · The Honest Liar (3–4 min)

In The Stranger, Meursault is condemned partly because he refuses to fake the grief and remorse society expects. Camus admired that refusal to lie — even when the lie would have saved him.

Think of a moment when you were expected to perform an emotion you did not feel — at a funeral, a wedding, an office, online. Is the social pressure to fake feeling a harmless lubricant, or a small daily betrayal of the truth Camus prized? Make the case with a concrete example.

C · Build Your Own (4–5 min)

Camus took an ancient myth and flipped its meaning. Your turn: invent your own small thought experiment about how to live. Describe a single situation — "imagine a person who…" — that looks like defeat from the outside but might secretly be a kind of freedom or revolt.

Set it up, walk me through why most people would pity that person, and land the idea underneath it in one sentence. I will then poke holes in it, and you defend it.

Your take — the conversation that matters most

You introduced me to Camus, so you get the last word. Pick the questions that grab you — there are no model answers here.

Open discussion

  1. After today, has anything shifted in how you see Camus — or has it confirmed why you like him?
  2. Camus insists meaning is something we make, not something we find. Is that a freeing idea to you, or a frightening one?
  3. He called escaping into religion or ideology "philosophical suicide". Do you share that suspicion of comforting systems, or do you think he was too harsh?
  4. He broke with Sartre over whether revolt could ever justify violence. Where would you draw the line between justified rebellion and going too far?
  5. If you could ask Camus one question, what would it be? And what do you think he would say back?
  6. Who is the next writer or thinker you would put in front of me — and what is the one idea of theirs I need to understand?
