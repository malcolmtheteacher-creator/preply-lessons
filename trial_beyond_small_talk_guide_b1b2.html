Beyond Small Talk

English for guiding, for interviews, and for the conversations that go somewhere.

Today has one main target: taking a conversation from polite to interesting. You already talk to international guests — the goal is to stop the conversation stopping. We'll also build guiding vocabulary, structure a briefing, and practise the questions an interview panel will actually ask you.
First — let's just talk. Answer in full sentences, and take your time. There is no wrong answer here.
Tell me about:
  • The last group you guided. Where did you take them, and what did they find hardest?
  • What made you want to work in travel rather than anything else?
  • Describe your favourite place in Finland to someone who has never been north of Helsinki.
  • What is the most common question your international guests ask you?
What we're working on today, in order:
1. Your words — the vocabulary of guiding and travel work, including the words that make you sound like a professional rather than a visitor.
2. Going deeper — the exact technique for turning a two-line exchange into a five-minute conversation.
3. The briefing — how to structure sixty seconds so that a group listens, understands and feels safe.
4. Speak — interview questions, timed, out loud.
Before we go on — a question worth answering honestly:
  • When a conversation with a guest stops, what usually stops it? Not enough words? No time? Not knowing what to ask next? Something else?

Your words

Ten items you can use this week. Say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · Work it out from the sentence

1. Day one: arrival and equipment check. Day two: the long ridge walk. Day three: the lake and home by four. The guests had the whole itinerary before they booked.
an itinerary (n) — the planned schedule of a trip, day by day. /aɪˈtɪnərəri/. Useful: a packed itinerary, to stick to the itinerary, a change to the itinerary.
2. The path crossed open bog, then loose rock, then a steep section under the trees. It was demanding terrain, and two of the group were not ready for it.
terrain (n, uncountable) — the physical character of the ground. Rough / uneven / marshy / rocky terrain. Note: no plural — ✗ terrains.
3. One guest was vegetarian, one could not eat nuts, and one had brought no waterproof trousers. We had to cater for all of it.
to cater for (v) — to provide what a particular person or group needs. "We cater for all dietary requirements." In US English: cater to. Related noun: catering.

B · Which word or phrase means…?

Three items: the highlight · gear · a briefing. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means the clothing and equipment you need for an activity?
gear (n, uncountable) — hiking gear, wet-weather gear, "Have you got the right gear?" Also kit (British). Not gears in this sense.
2. Which word means a short talk that tells people what will happen and what to do?
a briefing (n) — a safety briefing, a morning briefing. Verb: to brief someone on something. You do this already — today you'll structure it.
3. Which phrase means the best part of an experience, the part people remember?
the highlight (of the trip) — "For most guests the highlight is the first night under the northern lights." Very useful in interviews too: the highlight of my season.

C · Choose the best option — a / b / c

1. We set off at six to reach the summit before the weather closed in. To set off means…
  (a) to begin a journey   (b) to stop for a rest   (c) to return home
(a) — to set off = to start a journey. Also useful: to set out (same meaning), to head back (return), to make good time.
2. In tourism, which word is safest when you speak to the people on your trip, in person?
  (a) clients   (b) guests   (c) customers
(b) guests — warm, standard in hospitality and tourism. Clients sounds like a law firm; customers sounds like a shop. Use guests with people, customers in business talk about numbers.

D · Two small fixes that make a big difference

1. Finnish has no articles, so English ones are easy to drop. Fix these: "I work as ___ wilderness guide." · "___ weather changed very fast." · "We spent ___ night in ___ hut."
"I work as a wilderness guide." · "The weather changed very fast." (the weather — always) · "We spent the night in a hut."
Quick rule: a/an = one of many, first mention · the = we both know which one.
2. Choose the natural preposition: "We went ___ nature." · "We spent three days ___ the wilderness." · "It depends ___ the weather." · "I'm interested ___ working in tourism."
"We went into nature" — but far more natural: "We got out into the countryside / we went out into the wild." · "three days in the wilderness" · "It depends on the weather." · "I'm interested in working in tourism."

Going deeper — how to stop a conversation stopping

Superficial conversation is not a vocabulary problem. It is a structure problem: both people answer, nobody opens anything. Here is the fix, and it is small enough to use tomorrow.

The three-part answer. Most learners give part one and stop. Guests then have nothing to hold on to, so the exchange dies politely.

"Where are you from?"
✗ Answer only: "From Finland."
✓ Answer + detail + return: "I'm from Finland — from the north, actually, about two hours from the Swedish border. It's the kind of place where the summer never gets dark. Whereabouts are you from?"

Three parts, five seconds longer, and completely different. Detail gives them something to ask about; return keeps it moving.
Follow-up questions — the four that always work:
• What was that like? (invites a story, not a fact)
• How did you end up doing that? (people love this question)
• What's the best/worst part of it?
• What surprised you most about…?

And the reactions that buy you thinking time:
"Oh really?" · "That sounds amazing." · "I can imagine." · "That's a good question — let me think." · "Hang on, so you mean…?"

1. Repair the dead answers

Each answer below kills the conversation. Rewrite it in three parts: answer + detail + return. Say your version out loud first.

1. "How long have you been a guide?" — "Two years." →

2. "Is it cold in winter?" — "Yes, very." →

3. "Do you like this job?" — "Yes, I like it." →

1. "About two years now — I started with summer canoe trips and then took the winter qualification, which was the hard part. Had you done anything like this before you came?"

2. "It is — minus thirty on the coldest days, though honestly the wind matters more than the number. How are you finding it so far?"

3. "I do, though not for the reason people expect. The nature is the easy part; what I actually enjoy is watching a nervous group turn into a confident one by day three. What made you book this trip?"

Notice: every model answer contains one small honest detail that invites a question.

2. The thirty-second story

Guests remember stories, and interviewers ask for them. The shape is always the same:

Set-up (where, when, who — one sentence) → Problem (what went wrong) → Turn (what you did) → Point (what it says about you or the place).

"Last September I had a group of six on the ridge. Halfway up, the weather turned much faster than forecast and one guest froze — completely stopped, wouldn't move. I sent the others ahead with my colleague, sat down with her for ten minutes and we came down slowly together. She emailed me in the winter to say it was the proudest day of her year. That's the part of the job nobody advertises."

Your turn. Build one now, using the four steps — a real day at work when something went wrong. Write only the four beats here, then tell it out loud in thirty seconds.

Turn:   Point:

Keep the set-up to one sentence — beginners spend twenty seconds on background and never reach the point. Put the problem in early. And always finish on the point: it tells the listener why you told them, and it is the sentence an interviewer writes down.

Now practise it live:
  • Tell your thirty-second story. Then tell it again with a completely different point at the end — what changes?
  • Ask me four follow-up questions about something I say. Use a different type each time.

The briefing — sixty seconds that decide the day

This is presentation skill in its most practical form: standing in front of eight strangers in the cold, telling them what is going to happen. Structure beats vocabulary here — a clear briefing in simple English is far better than an elegant one nobody can follow.

The five-step shape:
1. Welcome & who you are — "Morning everyone — I'll be your guide today."
2. The plan, in three steps — "We'll start with…, then…, and we should be back around four."
3. What to expect — terrain, weather, how hard it is, what they're carrying.
4. Safety, stated calmly — "Two things I need from you today: stay behind me on the ice, and tell me straight away if you get cold hands."
5. Invite questions — "What have I missed?" (better than "Any questions?", which usually gets silence.)
Signposting language — the words that hold a group's attention:
• Opening: "Right, gather round." · "Before we set off, two minutes on the plan."
• Sequencing: "We'll start by…" · "After that…" · "The last stretch is…"
• Emphasis: "The main thing to remember is…" · "If you take one thing from this…"
• Contingency: "If the wind picks up, we'll…" · "In the unlikely event that…"
• Warmth at the end: "It's going to be a good day. Any last thing before we go?"

Task A · The interview answer everyone gets wrong (3 minutes)

"Tell me about yourself." This is not a request for your life story — it is a chance to place yourself. Use the shape: where you are now → how you got there → where you're going.

Three sentences each, no more. Finish with the sentence you want them to remember. Then say it again, thirty seconds shorter.

Task B · The tourism interview (4 minutes)

I'll ask, you answer — full three-part answers, and use a thirty-second story wherever one fits.

  • Why do you want to work in travel rather than something more secure?
  • Tell me about a time something went wrong on a trip. What did you do?
  • A guest complains that the trip was too hard and wants their money back. How do you handle it?
  • What makes a guide good, as opposed to just qualified?
  • Your English isn't perfect yet. Why should we give you a job that needs it?

That last one is not hostile — it is the question you should have an answer ready for, and a confident answer to it is worth more than perfect grammar.

Task C · Two guests, one hour (4 minutes)

You're walking at the back of the group with a guest who has said almost nothing all morning. Get a real conversation going and keep it alive for two minutes — three-part answers, follow-up questions, one story.

Then swap: you are the guest, and you are hard work. Short answers, no questions back. I'll try to open you up — watch what I do and steal it.

1. Answer + detail + return. Every time, until it's automatic.
2. One story, ready to go — set-up, problem, turn, point. It works with guests and in interviews.
3. Two safety points, not six. Structure is what makes people listen.
