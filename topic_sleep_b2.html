The Boy Who Stayed Awake

In 1964 a San Diego teenager stayed awake for eleven days for a school science fair — and accidentally opened the science of the third of your life you spend unconscious.

Today: you'll follow the strangest experiment in sleep science — and you'll master the language of advice and necessity: you should, you'd better, you must, you don't have to.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss. (Do not try any of this at home — that warning is now official, as you'll see.)

Part 1 — The science fair project

In December 1963, two San Diego high-school students, Randy Gardner and Bruce McAllister, needed a project for the school science fair. Their idea was magnificently teenage: what happens if you simply don't sleep? They flipped a coin; Gardner, seventeen, lost — or won, depending how you tell it. The plan was to beat the existing record of around 260 hours and observe the effects. Their scientific method, by their own later admission, was two teenagers with a notebook; their equipment was cold showers, basketball games, and each other. When a proper researcher — William Dement of Stanford, one of the founders of sleep science — read about the attempt in a newspaper, he drove down to observe, partly, he admitted, because somebody responsible ought to be in the room.

The record fell on 8 January 1964: 264 hours — eleven days and nights — awake, no stimulant drugs, everything documented. What the days in between recorded became a textbook chapter. By day two, Gardner's eyes stopped focusing and he could no longer identify objects by touch. Day three brought clumsiness and flat mood; day four, the first hallucinations — the street sign that became a person — and a grandiose delusion in which he was a famous football player. By the end of the first week his concentration was so poor he could not finish counting backwards from a hundred; asked why he had stopped, he explained that he had forgotten what he was doing. The strange part, which puzzled everyone: physically, his body mostly coped — pulse, blood pressure, temperature all roughly normal. It was the mind that was dissolving, function by function, in full view of a notebook.

When it was over, Dement watched the most interesting part: recovery. Gardner slept fourteen hours, woke feeling largely himself, and within a few nights was back to normal sleep — the brain repaying only a fraction of the debt by the clock, but repaying it in the right currency: the recovery sleep went disproportionately into the deep and dreaming stages. The teenage stunt had produced two genuine findings — sleep loss attacks the mind before the body, and the sleeping brain knows exactly which kind of sleep it is owed.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the experiment begin, and who ended up supervising it?

2. What did the eleven days show — and what did the recovery show?

1. As a school science fair project decided by a coin flip; Stanford's William Dement — a founder of sleep science — read about it in the paper and came to observe.

2. The mind failed first (focus, touch recognition, mood, hallucinations, memory) while the body largely coped. Recovery took only ~14 hours, weighted heavily toward deep and REM sleep — the brain repaid the debt selectively, in the stages that mattered.

Part 2 — What the third of your life is for

Gardner's stunt landed in the middle of a revolution. Only a decade earlier, in 1953, researchers in Chicago had noticed sleeping infants' eyes darting beneath their lids and discovered REM sleep — proof that the sleeping brain was not resting at all but cycling through distinct stages, some almost as active as waking. Dement and others mapped the architecture: roughly ninety-minute cycles, deep sleep dominating the early night, dreaming dominating the late. Sleep stopped being an absence and became a process — and the question changed from "why do we switch off?" to "what is all that activity doing?"

The answers have been arriving ever since, and they read like a maintenance schedule. Memory: the day's learning is replayed and consolidated at night — students who sleep after studying reliably beat students who don't, which is why revising all night is the one strategy guaranteed to sabotage itself. Cleaning: research from 2013 showed the sleeping brain physically flushes itself, clearing the day's waste proteins through channels that open mainly during deep sleep. Emotion: dreaming sleep appears to take the sting out of difficult memories, which is why problems genuinely look different in the morning. And the immune system, appetite hormones, and judgement all run partly on the night shift. Skip it, and the bill arrives everywhere at once.

Which is why the world's quiet dependence on too little sleep has become a public-health argument. Studies put drowsy driving among the major causes of road deaths, with a sleepless twenty hours impairing a driver roughly like being over the alcohol limit. The World Health Organization's cancer agency classified long-term night-shift work as a probable carcinogen in 2007 — the body's clock, it turns out, is not a suggestion. Schools that moved their start times later, letting teenagers sleep on the schedule their biology actually runs, measured better grades, better moods and fewer car accidents. And Guinness World Records, revealingly, closed the sleep-deprivation category decades ago: some records, they concluded, should not be encouraged to fall.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the 1953 discovery change about the question science was asking?

2. Name three jobs sleep does — and two public-health consequences of skipping it.

1. REM's discovery showed sleep is an active, staged process, not an off-switch — so the question became "what is all that activity doing?"

2. Jobs: consolidating memory, physically cleaning the brain (deep sleep), processing emotion — plus immune and hormonal work. Consequences: drowsy driving comparable to drunk driving; night-shift work classified a probable carcinogen; and (positively) later school starts improving grades, mood and safety.

Discuss:

Part 3 — The bill, decades later

And Randy Gardner? He grew up, lived an ordinary life, and became a curiosity that sleep scientists quoted for sixty years — usually as evidence that even extreme sleep loss does no permanent harm, since he had seemed to walk away untouched. Then, in interviews decades later, Gardner himself complicated the happy ending: in his fifties and sixties he developed severe, years-long insomnia, which he described as unbearable and — whether science can prove the link or not — connected in his own mind to the eleven days of 1964. The man who once could not stay awake became the man who could not sleep, and the textbook's favourite reassuring example now ends with its own asterisk.

Modern sleep science has, meanwhile, swung between alarm and correction — and honesty needs both. The alarm: bestselling researchers have called short sleep an epidemic linked to almost every major disease, and the core findings — memory, cleaning, driving, shift work — stand solid. The correction: other scientists pushed back on the most dramatic claims, noting that some are overstated, that anxiety about sleep is itself now causing insomnia (clinics coined "orthosomnia" for patients made sleepless by their sleep-tracking watches), and that the healthiest relationship with sleep looks less like optimisation and more like trust: tired people who stop fighting the night mostly find it comes back.

Which leaves the story's usable ending exactly where it started — with a teenager's question, better asked. Not "how little sleep can I get away with?", which Gardner answered at seventeen and paid for at sixty, but the adult version: what would you have to change so that the third of your life that maintains the other two-thirds stopped being the first thing you spend? Every alarm you set tonight is your answer.

Grammar — advice & necessity: should, ought to, had better, must, have to

Sleep is the world's most-advised subject, and English grades its advice precisely — from gentle suggestion to non-negotiable necessity. Mixing the levels changes what you're actually saying.

The advice ladder, gentle to urgent:
• should / ought to — the standard recommendation: "You should keep regular hours." (ought to = same meaning, more formal)
• had better ('d better) — advice with a warning inside: "You'd better not drive tonight." Something bad follows if ignored. Note: had better + bare infinitive, negative 'd better not.
• must — the speaker's strong insistence: "You must see a doctor about this."
Necessity and its absence:
• have to — necessity from outside (rules, biology): "Teenagers have to sleep more; the clock is built in."
• mustn't = forbidden: "You mustn't drive after twenty sleepless hours."
• don't have to / needn't = not necessary: "You don't have to sleep eight hours exactly — the range is individual."
The classic trap, again: mustn't ≠ don't have to. One forbids; the other releases.

2. You've been awake since yesterday. You get behind the wheel. (forbidden)

3. The sleep clinic leaflet — complete it

Fill each gap with the right modal, then read the leaflet aloud in a kind but firm clinician's voice.

Good sleep is mostly habit. You (advice) keep the same bedtime, even at weekends, and you (warning) not take your phone to bed — the light and the scrolling both work against you. Going deeper

A note on the record: the Gardner experiment is unusually well documented (Dement's own accounts survive); his later insomnia is his own testimony, and the link to 1964 is his belief, not a proven finding; the science summarised here follows the mainstream, with the overstatements flagged as such.

What the story actually teaches. First, the order of collapse. Sleep loss took Gardner's judgement, focus and perception days before it touched his pulse — which means a sleep-deprived person is least able to notice what they've lost, since the noticing equipment fails first. That inverts the usual confidence: "I'm fine, I function on five hours" is testimony from the one witness the condition has already compromised.

Second, the cultural puzzle. We treat food, exercise and hygiene as non-negotiable and sleep as the flexible margin — the first thing sacrificed to work, screens or each other. The story's history suggests why: sleep produces nothing visible, and industrial culture learned to respect only what can be displayed. The boast "I'll sleep when I'm dead" is conspicuous busyness (see this dashboard's free-time lesson) applied to biology — and the research reply is uncomfortable: on average, you'll be dead sooner, and duller meanwhile.

Third, the optimisation trap. The correction chapter matters as much as the alarm: a generation armed with sleep trackers has invented a new insomnia made of performance anxiety about rest. Sleep responds to routine, darkness and calm — and resists being scored. The deepest advice hiding in the science is almost anti-modern: this is one third of life that improves when you stop measuring it and start trusting it.

  • Trackers created orthosomnia. Name another area where measuring a good thing damaged it.
