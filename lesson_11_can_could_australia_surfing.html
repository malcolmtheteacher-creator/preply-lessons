Can, Could, Be Able To — Surfing Australia's Beaches

First Thoughts

Australia has more than 10,000 beaches. If you visited one every day, it would take you nearly 30 years to see them all. Surfing isn't just a sport here — it's part of the national identity. From Sydney's city beaches to the remote coastline of Western Australia, riding waves is something millions of people do, watch, or dream about.

Quick Notice

In this lesson the language target is three small words that do a lot of work in English: can, could, and be able to. They all talk about ability — but they are not interchangeable. By the end of the lesson, you should know exactly when to use each one.

Can, Could, Be Able To

All three forms talk about ability. The differences are about time, form, and one important nuance for the past.

Form When we use it Example
can Present ability — something you know how to do now. "I can ride a bike with no hands."
can't / cannot Present inability. "I can't surf yet, but I'm learning."
could Past general ability — something you used to know how to do, over a period of time. "When I was younger, I could swim for hours without getting tired."
couldn't Past inability — works for both general and specific. "I tried for an hour, but I couldn't stand up on the board."
was / were able to Past specific success — one time, one event, you managed it. "It was huge, but I was able to catch one wave at the end."
be able to The flexible one — for tenses where "can" and "could" don't exist. "I will be able to surf properly by Christmas." / "I'd love to be able to ride a long board."
The B1/B2 trap — couldn't vs wasn't able to.

For one specific success in the past, we say was/were able to, NOT could. But for inability — failing or not knowing how — both couldn't and wasn't able to work fine.

✅ "The wave was massive, but I was able to ride it."

❌ "The wave was massive, but I could ride it." (sounds wrong to native ears)

✅ "I tried, but I couldn't ride it." / "I tried, but I wasn't able to ride it." (both fine)

Check Yourself — Choose the right form

1. "My sister ____ play three musical instruments." (present ability)

can — present, general ability. She knows how to play three instruments. We use can for present skills you have now.

2. "When I was a student, I ____ walk to class in 15 minutes." (past, general)

could — past general ability. Something that was true for the whole period when you lived there.

3. "The waves were big yesterday, but I ____ catch three of them." (past, specific success)

was able to — one specific event, you succeeded. Not "could" — that would sound like a general ability, not a one-off success.

4. "I'm sure he ____ fix the bike — just give him an hour." (future)

will be able to — "can" has no future form, so we use be able to.

5. "I'd love ____ speak English fluently." (infinitive)

to be able to — "can" has no infinitive form. You can't say "I'd love to can speak English" — it must be "to be able to".

6. "I tried the big wave but I ____ stand up — I fell off." (past, failure)

couldn't (or wasn't able to) — both work for past inability. The asymmetry is only on the positive side: success needs "was able to", but failure accepts both.

"My first day at Bondi was a disaster. When I was a child I (1)   swim like a fish, so I wasn't worried about the water. But the rip was so strong that I (2)   paddle out — I tried for ten minutes and got nowhere. In the end, I (3)   reach the line-up, but only because a local surfer showed me the right channel. I tried six waves, and on the very last one I (4)   stand up — just for two seconds, but I did it! These days I (5)   paddle out by myself, no problem. By next summer, I'm sure I (6)   ride a proper wave."

1. could — past general ability across his whole childhood.

2. couldn't — past inability/failure. (wasn't able to also works.)

3. was able to — one specific past success after a struggle. NOT could.

4. was able to — one specific success on one specific wave. NOT could.

5. can — present general ability.

6. will be able to — future ability. NOT "will can".

Common mistakes to avoid:

❌ "I will can swim by next year." → ✅ "I will be able to swim by next year." ("Can" has no future form.)

❌ "She has could surf since childhood." → ✅ "She has been able to surf since childhood." ("Can/could" don't combine with perfect tenses — use be able to.)

❌ "I'd like to can speak Spanish." → ✅ "I'd like to be able to speak Spanish." (No infinitive of "can".)

Four Beaches Every Surfer Knows

Read the passage, then answer the questions below. Use the new vocabulary and the grammar where you can.

Bondi Beach, Sydney (NSW). Bondi is Australia's most famous beach, only 7 km from the centre of Sydney. The waves are usually small to medium, which means a beginner can rent a board, take a lesson, and stand up on the same day. It's a great place to learn. The catch? Bondi is also very crowded — on a sunny weekend you might share the line-up with 200 other surfers. Australians often say: "Anyone can surf Bondi, but no one can surf Bondi alone."

Bells Beach, Victoria. Bells is the home of the longest-running surfing competition in the world — the Rip Curl Pro, held every Easter since 1961. The waves here are powerful and the water is cold (you need a wetsuit even in summer). Bells is not for beginners. To enjoy it, you need to be able to paddle hard, read the swell, and stay calm when a big set rolls in. Most people who surf Bells could already surf well before they came.

Margaret River, Western Australia. Three thousand kilometres west of Sydney, the Margaret River region has world-class waves, vineyards, and one extra ingredient — sharks. Locals say a surfer should be able to spot the difference between a curious shark and a dangerous one. Most people who surf here are experienced. But the wine is excellent, so even non-surfers come.

Snapper Rocks, Queensland. On the Gold Coast, Snapper Rocks produces one of the longest rideable waves on the planet — sometimes over a kilometre. When the conditions are right, an advanced surfer can ride for two minutes without falling off. When the conditions are wrong, even a pro can't catch a single decent wave. Reading the ocean here is everything.

Comprehension & Discussion

1. Which beach is best for a first lesson, and why?

Bondi — small to medium waves, easy access from the city, board rental and lessons are everywhere. A beginner can rent a board, take a lesson, and stand up on the same day.

2. Why is Bells Beach not for beginners?

The waves are powerful and cold. To enjoy Bells you need to be able to paddle hard, read the swell, and stay calm. Most people who surf Bells could already surf well before they arrived.

3. What is the unusual extra danger at Margaret River?

Sharks. A surfer there should be able to tell the difference between a curious shark and a dangerous one.

4. At Snapper Rocks, what can an advanced surfer do when conditions are right?

Ride for two minutes without falling — sometimes over a kilometre of unbroken wave. One of the longest rideable waves on Earth.

Roleplay: At the Surf School

The scenario. You've just arrived on the Australian coast. You've always wanted to learn to surf, and today you walk into a surf school for the first time. The assistant behind the counter needs to find out about you — your swimming, your past experience, your goals — so they can recommend the right lesson package.

🏄 Your Role — The Customer

You want to learn to surf. Be honest about what you can and can't do. The assistant will ask you questions — answer using the right modal form. You can ask questions back too.

You should naturally use all five forms during the conversation:

  • can / can't — what you can do now ("I can swim, but not very far")
  • could / couldn't — what you could do as a child or in the past
  • was / were able to — one specific past success ("Last summer I was able to stand on a paddleboard for the first time")
  • will be able to — what you hope for ("I want to know when I'll be able to ride a real wave")
  • to be able to — your bigger goal ("I'd love to be able to surf properly by next year")

📊 Useful facts about learning to surf

Real numbers from real surf schools. Use these in your roleplay so the conversation has substance.

Standing up the first time: Most beginners can stand on a soft beginner board (called a "foamie") in their very first lesson — usually within the first hour or two. Almost nobody fails.

Riding a real wave: Standing for two seconds on broken white water is one thing. Riding a proper unbroken ("green") wave takes most learners 3 to 6 months of regular practice.

Becoming confident: Reaching the level where you can paddle out on a medium beach and choose your own waves takes most people 1 to 2 years.

Equipment for beginners: First boards are long, wide, and soft — easier to balance on, and safer if you hit yourself with one.

Safety rules: All beginner lessons happen on small, sheltered beaches with lifeguards. You need to be able to swim at least 50 metres to join a class. No swimmer means no surf lesson.

Wetsuits: Needed year-round in southern Australia (Victoria, southern NSW). Often optional in winter on the Gold Coast and tropical north.

Group size: A typical beginner class is one instructor for every 4-6 students. Private lessons cost about double the group price.

  • Is there a beach near here where I could practise alone safely?
