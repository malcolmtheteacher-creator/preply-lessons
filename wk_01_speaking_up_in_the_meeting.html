Speaking Up in the Meeting

The room is fast, fluent and full of opinions. You know your subject cold — now you just have to get a word in, in English, before the moment passes.

The Scene 🎬

It's the second morning of the conference, and you're in a working session with a dozen people from six countries. The shared language is English, and it's flying — quick, overlapping, confident. Someone makes a claim you know is wrong. You have a better idea, a genuinely good one, and the room would benefit from hearing it.

And here's the quiet frustration every capable professional knows: it isn't that you can't speak English. It's that by the time you've built the perfect sentence in your head, the conversation has already moved two topics on. Fluency in a meeting isn't about big vocabulary or flawless grammar. It's about staying in the room — getting in, buying yourself a second to think, disagreeing without a fight, and never, ever freezing when someone turns to you and says "and what do you think?"

Across the table: a chair who runs a brisk meeting, moves things along, and — like every good chair — will happily give the floor to anyone confident enough to ask for it.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. In your own language, are you someone who speaks up early in a meeting or hangs back? Does that change in English — and if so, what exactly makes the difference?
  2. What's the hardest moment for you in an English meeting: getting in, being interrupted, being put on the spot, or losing the thread when people speak fast? Be specific — that's today's target.

Who's who

You
The Expert Finding Their Voice 💼

You've got the knowledge and the ideas — in your own language you'd own this room. Today's job is to carry that same authority into English: to get in, hold the floor, and think out loud without panicking.

The other voice
The Chair 🗓️

Efficient, fair, and quick to move on. They're not unkind — they simply give the floor to whoever claims it. Ask well, and it's yours; hesitate, and the moment's gone.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't watch what the speaker knows — watch the small phrases they use to get in, buy time, and stay afloat. Those phrases, not perfect grammar, are what fluency is made of.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Notice how the speaker keeps the floor with short, automatic phrases, so their brain is free to think about the idea, not the English.

Chair…so the data's clear, we should push the launch to Q3. I think that's settled. Right, moving on to budget—
YouSorry — can I just come in here before we move on?
ChairOf course. Go ahead.
YouThanks. So — how shall I put this — I see it slightly differently. The data's solid, I agree, but I'm not sure it's telling us to delay. Can I take thirty seconds to explain why?
ChairPlease do.
YouThe numbers show low awareness, yes. But to me that's not a reason to wait — it's a reason to launch and build the awareness. If we delay, we hand that head start to — sorry, I don't know the exact word — the people doing the same thing as us. Our competitors.
ChairThat's a fair point. Petra, you led the original analysis — is that a risk you'd share?
PetraPossibly, but the awareness gap is significant. I still think launching now is premature and frankly a little risky.
YouI take your point, Petra — and you know this data far better than I do. Just so I've understood, though: when you say "premature", do you mean the product isn't ready, or that the market isn't?
PetraThe market. The product's ready.
YouThen I think we might actually agree more than it sounds. What if we launched on time, but softly — a smaller first market — and used what we learn to go big in Q3?
ChairInteresting. Say a bit more — how would that work in practice?
YouAh — can I come back to you on the detail? I'd rather give you real numbers than guess now. But the principle is: launch to learn, not to wait.
ChairGood. So, if I've got this right — the proposal on the table is now a soft launch on schedule, full launch in Q3. Let's park it there and come back with figures. Thank you, that was a useful challenge.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The speaker gets the floor, then immediately says "how shall I put this". They're not lost for words — so why say it? What does that little phrase buy them?

2. Mid-sentence, the speaker doesn't know the word "competitors". What do they do instead of stopping — and why does it keep them sounding fluent rather than stuck?

3. Petra pushes back with "premature". Rather than argue, the speaker asks a question. What was clever about that move — and where did it lead?

1. It buys a second of thinking time while still holding the floor. A confident filler like "how shall I put this…" sounds thoughtful, not hesitant — far better than a silent freeze or a panicky rush. The room waits; you think.
2. They talk around the gap — "the people doing the same thing as us" — and keep going, then land the real word when it arrives. Paraphrasing past a missing word is the single most fluency-saving skill there is: never stop, describe your way through.
3. Instead of head-to-head disagreement, they clarified — "when you say premature, do you mean…?". It lowered the temperature, showed respect for Petra's expertise, and revealed that the two of them actually agreed. A question can win a point an argument can't.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that keep you afloat in any fast English meeting. Learn them until they're automatic — that's the point. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Fluency is flow, not perfection. These phrases work because they're automatic — you don't build them, you just reach for them, which frees your brain to think about your idea instead of your English. And a confident pause ("let me think for a moment") sounds far more fluent than rushing and freezing. Slow is smooth; smooth is fluent.

🙋 Getting in & holding the floor

Phrase 1

The chair is about to move on, and you need to get in — politely, but before the moment's gone. Which line claims the floor without sounding rude?

A. "Wait, wait, I want to say something."
B. "Sorry, can I just come in here?"
C. "You are wrong about this."
B — "Sorry, can I just come in here?"
"Just come in here" is the standard, well-mannered way to break into a discussion. The "sorry" and "just" make it polite and small; it asks for the floor rather than grabbing it. A sounds flustered; C starts a fight before you've made your point.
Phrase 2

Someone tries to cut across you before you've finished. You want to keep the floor, warmly but firmly. Complete the chunk: "If I could just ___ ___ ___…"

"If I could just finish that thought…"
Polite, but it holds your ground — it signals you're nearly done and asks them to let you land it. Cousins: "Let me just finish this point…", "Bear with me one second…"

⏳ Buying time — the fluency lifesaver

Phrase 3

The chair turns to you: "And what do you think?" You need a moment to gather your answer — without an awkward silence. What do you say to buy that second and still sound in control?

"That's a good question — let me think for a moment."
It's completely normal, even at the top level, and it buys real thinking time while sounding considered. Native speakers do this constantly. The confident pause is your friend; the panicked rush is the enemy.
Phrase 4

You've started answering but you're still shaping the idea as you speak. There's a three-word filler that buys a beat while you find the right way to say it: "___ ___ I put this…"

"How shall I put this…"
A thoughtful, fluent-sounding filler that says "I'm choosing my words with care", not "I'm stuck". Relatives: "How can I best explain this…", "Let me put it another way…"

🌉 Talking around a word you don't know

Phrase 5

Mid-sentence, the exact English word vanishes. The worst thing you can do is stop. Instead, you describe your way through it. In your own words, keep going past a word you've forgotten — how might you start?

e.g. "I don't know the exact word, but it's the thing that…" / "You know when…" / "What I'm trying to say is…"
This is the number-one fluency skill: never stop at a missing word. Describe it, point at it, approximate it — "the people who do the same job as us", "the machine that makes the copies". Nine times out of ten someone supplies the word, and you never lost your flow.

🤝 Giving a view & disagreeing well

Phrase 6

You want to offer your opinion as your perspective — open, not a hard claim of fact. Complete it: "___ ___ ___ sit, the bigger risk is waiting."

"From where I sit, the bigger risk is waiting."
"From where I sit…" frames it as your honest viewpoint, which is harder to attack than a flat "you're wrong". Cousins: "The way I see it…", "My sense is…", "To my mind…"
Phrase 7

A respected colleague has just disagreed with you. You want to push back — but keep them on side. Which opener disagrees diplomatically?

A. "No, that's not right."
B. "I take your point, but I see it slightly differently."
C. "Obviously you haven't thought this through."
B — "I take your point, but I see it slightly differently."
"I take your point" concedes something first, so they feel heard; "slightly differently" softens the disagreement. You can hold a firm position with warm words. A is blunt; C burns the bridge.

🔍 Staying with it — and landing it

Phrase 8

Someone used a word or made a point you're not 100% sure of — and guessing is risky. You want to check, without looking like you weren't following. Put it in order:

do you mean?when you sayexactly"premature",what
"When you say 'premature', what exactly do you mean?"
Checking a key word isn't weakness — it's control. It buys time, shows you're listening closely, and often reveals you agree more than you thought. Cousins: "Sorry, could you run that by me again?", "Just so I've understood…"
Phrase 9

You're put on the spot for detail you don't have to hand. Rather than waffle or freeze, you gracefully defer. What phrase lets you do that and stay credible?

"Can I come back to you on that?"
Far stronger than guessing. It says you'd rather be accurate than quick — which reads as confidence, not avoidance. Add "I'd rather give you real numbers than guess" and you sound positively senior.
Phrase 10

Before the group moves on, you want to check everyone's landed in the same place — and quietly show you were steering. Complete it: "So, ___ ___ ___ ___ ___, the proposal is now a soft launch."

"So, if I've got this right, the proposal is now a soft launch."
Summarising is a power move disguised as a favour — the person who says "so, to sum up…" quietly frames the outcome. Cousins: "So, where does that leave us?", "Let me just check we're all agreed…"

Your Turn 🎭

Now you're in the room. Speak your answers out loud FIRST — this is fluency practice, so keep talking even when it's not perfect. Use the timer: a real meeting won't wait while you craft the ideal sentence.

Round 1 — you are in the meeting 💼

The chair speaks — you answer, out loud. No looking back at the phrases; reach for them from memory. Remember: get in → buy time → give your view → don't freeze.

Chair…so I think we're agreed, we'll go with the original supplier. Good. Next item—
ChairSure, go ahead — what's your concern?
ChairHmm. But the original supplier is cheaper and we know them. Why change now?
ChairAnd what would this actually cost us over a year?
ChairOkay. So where does that leave us?

Round 2 — now YOU chair the meeting 🗓️

Swap seats. You're running it. Keep it moving, bring quieter people in, and handle someone who disagrees — all while staying warm and in control. Invent the topic; give the meeting some life.

ColleagueSorry, can I just come in here? I'm not sure we've thought about the timing.
ColleagueWell — from where I sit, launching in December is a mistake. It's our busiest month.
ColleagueI just think we're rushing this, honestly.
ColleagueOkay, that's fair. So what are we deciding?
The Twist

🎯 Put on the spot

One more round — no script, no warning. You're only half-listening when the chair suddenly turns to you: "You've worked in this market — you must have a strong view. What would you do?" Everyone looks at you. You don't have a prepared answer. Start the timer and talk your way through it live: buy your second, think out loud, give a real opinion, admit what you'd need to check — and land somewhere. The goal isn't the perfect answer; it's to stay calm, keep talking, and sound like someone worth listening to. One rule: no freezing and no apologising for your English — just handle it.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I bought thinking time out loud rather than freezing or rushing.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target for the conference — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Here's the secret that quietly levels the room: most of the confident-sounding people in an international meeting are also working in their second, third or fourth language. English is simply the bridge everyone agreed to meet on — and almost nobody on that bridge is walking in their mother tongue.

Which means the person who wins the room usually isn't the one with the best grammar. It's the one who stays in the conversation — who gets in, holds a calm pause, talks around the words they don't have, and keeps their authority even while hunting for a phrase.

Your ideas are already good enough for that table. This week, the only job is to let them out — unhurried, unapologetic, and in English.
