Day of the Dead: Mexican Halloween?

Skulls, costumes and 2 November — many outsiders call the Day of the Dead "Mexican Halloween". In reality it is a family celebration of remembering, with very different roots.

The stereotype: "The Day of the Dead is just Mexican Halloween." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Remembering, not fearing

The Day of the Dead (Día de Muertos) is celebrated mainly on 1 and 2 November. Families build ofrendas — home altars with photos, candles, the favourite food and drink of the dead, and orange cempasúchil marigolds, whose colour and smell are said to guide spirits home. Many families also clean and decorate graves and spend the night in cemeteries. The mood is warm and festive, not scary.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When is the Day of the Dead?

2. What is on an ofrenda?

1. Mainly 1 and 2 November.

2. Photos, candles, food and drink, marigolds.

Discuss:
  • Is it healthy to celebrate the dead?

Part 2 — Mixed roots

The festival blends Indigenous traditions of honouring the dead, from cultures such as the Aztecs, with the Catholic feasts of All Saints' and All Souls' Days on 1 and 2 November. The famous skeleton lady, La Catrina, began as satire: the engraver José Guadalupe Posada drew her in the early 20th century to mock people who copied European fashions, and the painter Diego Rivera later made her famous. In 2008 UNESCO inscribed the festival on its intangible heritage list.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two traditions does the festival blend?

2. Who first drew La Catrina?

1. Indigenous traditions and Catholic All Saints' and All Souls' Days.

2. José Guadalupe Posada.

Discuss:
  • Why might satire become a national symbol?

Part 3 — Halloween and James Bond

Halloween, which comes from Celtic and Christian traditions, is about scary costumes and fun; the Day of the Dead is about memory and family. The two have influenced each other, though. Mexico City's big Day of the Dead parade was inspired by an opening scene in the 2015 James Bond film Spectre — the first official parade took place in 2016, after the film showed one that didn't exist.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How is Halloween different?

2. What inspired Mexico City's parade?

1. It's about scary fun; the Day of the Dead is about memory and family.

2. The 2015 James Bond film Spectre.

Discuss:
  • Can a film create a tradition?

Grammar — Past Modals of Deduction

When we explain where a label or belief came from, we often deduce. Past modals show how certain we are.

How it works:
• must have + past participle — nearly certain: "The label must have come from the Cold War."
• might / may / could have — possible.
• can't have — nearly impossible.

Going deeper

The "Mexican Halloween" idea exists because both festivals fall at the same time and use skeletons and costumes. Global media, commercial products and films have strengthened the link. But the confusion hides a different attitude to death — remembering and welcoming the dead rather than fearing them — and the festival is also celebrated in other countries, such as Guatemala and Bolivia, in their own ways.

Worth noticing: "Must have" = nearly certain; "might/may have" = possible; "can't/couldn't have" = nearly impossible.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Show respect. If you're invited to see an ofrenda, ask about the people in the photos — it's a sign of respect. Don't call it "Mexican Halloween", and if you wear Catrina face paint at a celebration, learn what it means first.
