Today: We're learning the logline technique — the skill of compressing any complex idea into a single sentence that creates curiosity, clarity, and momentum.

Hollywood screenwriters have known this for decades: if you can't describe your story in one sentence, you don't understand it yet. They call it the logline — and it's the single most important sentence in any pitch.

The same principle applies in every boardroom, investor meeting, and strategy presentation you'll ever give. The person who can say in one sentence what others need a paragraph to explain is the person who controls the room.

This isn't about dumbing things down. It's about compression under pressure — finding the essential shape of an idea and expressing it with precision.

The Logline Formula

Four elements. One sentence. Infinite applications.

THE LOGLINE FORMULA

Somebody wants something but something's in the way so they must act

Every compelling one-sentence story has these four elements, even when they're disguised:

Somebody — a specific actor (person, team, company). Not "we" or "the industry." Someone the listener can picture.

Wants something — a clear goal or desire. The clearer the want, the more the listener leans in.

But — the obstacle, tension, or complication. This is the engine of the sentence. Without "but," you have a press release, not a story.

So — the action, the resolution, the forward momentum. What happens next? What must be done?

See it in action

A startup pitch

Small business owners need to understand their finances, but accounting software is built for accountants, not humans, so we built a tool that turns your bank feed into plain-English insights every morning.

A change management narrative

Our engineering team has the talent to lead the industry, but our release cycle is so slow that competitors ship in weeks what takes us months, so we're restructuring into autonomous squads that own their code from commit to customer.

A personal brand statement

I spent twenty years watching brilliant leaders lose rooms full of people because they couldn't turn their expertise into a story anyone cared about, so now I teach the architecture of strategic storytelling.

The power of "but": Notice that in every example, removing the "but" clause kills the sentence. "Small business owners need to understand their finances, so we built a tool" — flat. The obstacle is what creates the gap the listener wants to see closed. It's the same principle as the "Complication" in SCR from Lesson 1.

What's wrong with these?

Each of these attempts is missing something. Identify the problem and fix it — out loud first, then write your improved version.

Attempt 1

"We're building an AI-powered platform that uses machine learning and natural language processing to transform how enterprises manage their customer relationships through automated, data-driven insights."

0 words

No "but." There's no tension, no obstacle, no reason to care. It's also drowning in jargon — "AI-powered platform" tells you nothing about who it helps or why it matters. And "we" is faceless. A stronger version might be: "Sales teams lose deals because their CRM tells them what happened yesterday but not what to do tomorrow — so we built one that predicts what each customer needs before they ask."

Attempt 2

"The healthcare system is broken and patients suffer."

0 words

No "somebody" and no "so." It's a complaint, not a story. There's no specific actor, no forward momentum, no action. Who is suffering? What kind of suffering? What can be done? A logline needs a protagonist and a direction.

Final Review

1. What are the four elements of the logline formula?

2. Why is the "but" the most important element?

3. What's the difference between compression and oversimplification?

1. Somebody + wants something + but something's in the way + so they must act.

2. Because without the obstacle, there's no tension — and without tension, there's no story. The "but" is what makes someone lean in. It creates the gap the listener wants to see closed.

3. Compression preserves the essential truth and adds precision. Oversimplification strips away meaning. The test: does your one sentence make someone want to hear more, or does it make them shrug?
