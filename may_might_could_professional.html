May, Might & Could

The language of possibility, risk and diplomatic hedging

Modals of Possibility · Forecasting & Speculation

Reading What Isn't Certain

When an outcome isn't certain, we reach for may, might and could. In senior business conversation this is essential language: it lets you discuss forecasts, weigh risks and raise concerns without over-committing — "margins could tighten" lands very differently from "margins will fall."

Opening Questions

Talk these through (use: might, may, could):

  • What might happen to your main market over the next year?
  • Where could the business be in five years?
  • Why might a promising deal stall at the last minute?
  • What may happen if a key competitor cuts prices?

How the Grammar Works

Select each example to reveal the explanation.

MAY — Possibility (more formal)

~50% Possible Something is possible (formal, measured)

"We may enter the Asian market next year."
✓ A genuine possibility — roughly 50/50. "May" sounds formal and considered, ideal for board-level discussion.
"The board may not approve the budget."
✓ A negative possibility — it's possible they won't approve it.
"May I suggest an alternative?"
✓ A very polite, formal way to make a move in a meeting without imposing.

MIGHT — Possibility (a little less certain)

~30-50% Possible Something is possible (slightly more tentative)

"We might delay the launch until Q3."
✓ It's on the table, but not decided. Useful for floating an idea without committing.
"The supplier might not meet the deadline."
✓ A tentative warning — possible, but you're not certain.
"You might be right about the pricing."
✓ A diplomatic way to half-agree — conceding the point may be valid without fully committing.

COULD — Possibility (any option on the table)

Any % Possible Something is possible, or one option among several

"That decision could backfire."
✓ Flagging a risk worth considering — a possible downside.
"Where's the bottleneck?" "It could be in distribution."
✓ Suggesting one likely explanation among several.
"We could grow organically or through acquisition."
✓ Laying out options — both routes are possible.

The Key Distinction

Look at these three. How does the feel change?

  • "Margins may tighten next quarter."
  • "Margins might tighten next quarter."
  • "Margins could tighten next quarter."

They're close — but the level of certainty and the tone shift slightly. Talk through which you'd use in a board paper versus a quick corridor conversation.

May vs Might vs Could

At a Glance

Modal Certainty Use Example
may ~50% — Possible Formal possibility "The board may approve it."
might ~30-50% — Less certain Slightly more tentative "We might postpone the launch."
could Any possibility Option or potential outcome "That could work."
will 100% — Certain A firm commitment "I will send the figures by Friday."

Speculating About the Past

may have, might have, could have + past participle

Present / Future Past
"The deal may fall through." "The deal may have fallen through."
"They might underestimate demand." "They might have underestimated demand."
"We could move faster." "We could have moved faster."

What might explain it?

  • There could be...

  • Something may have...

Situation 3: The Sudden Resignation

The picture: A reliable, engaged senior manager resigns with no explanation and declines an exit conversation.

Why might they be leaving?

Offer at least five possibilities:

  • They might have...
  • They could have...
  • They may have...

Situation 4: The Overnight Drop

The picture: Online orders fell 30% overnight. Nothing changed on your side — same price, same marketing, same site.

What could be behind it?

Explore different explanations:

  • A competitor might have...
  • It could have been...
  • Something may have...

    Common Mistakes

    ❌ WRONG: "The deal may collapsed."

    ✅ RIGHT: "The deal may have collapsed." (may + have + past participle)

    ❌ WRONG: "Margins might to tighten next quarter."

    ✅ RIGHT: "Margins might tighten next quarter." (no "to"!)

    ❌ WRONG: "We could has moved faster."

    ✅ RIGHT: "We could have moved faster." (could + have + past participle)

    May vs Might — In Practice

    A useful note:

    In modern English, may and might are very close in meaning. The difference is subtle:

    • May is slightly more formal and slightly more certain (~50%) — it suits board papers and written reports.
    • Might is slightly more tentative (~40%) — it suits floating an idea or softening a warning.
    • In everyday speech they're often interchangeable.
    • For a polite request or offer, "may" is the natural choice: "May I...?"
