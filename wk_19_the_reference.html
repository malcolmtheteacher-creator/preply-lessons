The Reference

Someone you liked working with has put your name down as a referee. He's expecting a yes. You can't give him one — not the kind he's imagining.

The Scene 🎬

A message arrives on a Tuesday evening from Danny Okonkwo, who you managed for about eighteen months and haven't seen in two years. He's down to the last two for a job he badly wants, and he's put you down as a referee. He isn't really asking — he's telling you, cheerfully, the way you tell a friend good news.

And there's the problem, which you knew before you'd finished reading it. You liked Danny enormously. Everybody did. He was also, on the whole, not much good — late with everything, brilliant in a room, hopeless the minute he left it. Write him a reference and you'll either lie or damage him; refuse and you're the one person who wouldn't help. He has no power over you at all. There's no manager to appeal to, no process, nothing to be fixed later. There's just a friendly man who thinks you're about to say yes.

So you ring him, because a message would be cowardly, and now you've got about four minutes to say no in a way that leaves him something — some route to a reference, and some reason to still like you afterwards.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Has anyone ever asked you for a favour you couldn't honestly give? What did you actually do — say no, say yes and regret it, or go quiet and hope it went away?
  2. Which is worse to be on the receiving end of: a straight no, or a yes that turns out to be half-hearted? Use a real example if you have one, and say what made the difference.

Who's who

You
His Old Manager 💼

You liked him, you still do, and you're about to disappoint him. You've no authority here and nothing to hide behind — only a choice between four awkward minutes now and a quietly damaging reference later.

The other voice
Danny Okonkwo 📞

Warm, funny, impossible to dislike, and expecting a yes. He half-knows what you're going to say, which makes it harder rather than easier — he'd much rather you didn't say it out loud.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Notice how long the no takes to arrive, and how much of the work is done by the things that aren't the no.

Danny…anyway, it's the final stage, they just want two referees, and I put you straight down. That's all right, isn't it? You had me for what, two years?
YouEighteen months, about. Danny — can I be honest with you before I say yes?
DannyOh. Right. Yeah, course. Course you can.
YouI could give you one, but I'm not sure it'd be the one you want. Sorry, that's — that came out worse than I meant it. What I mean is —
DannyNo, no. Go on. Say it.
YouIt's not that you were bad — it's that I couldn't say you were strong. And they don't just ring up and ask was he all right. They ask about specific things, and I'd have to answer honestly if they asked me about deadlines.
DannyThe Renner account. That's what this is. That's it, isn't it.
YouPartly. Look, I'd rather tell you now than write you something lukewarm. They can smell a lukewarm one, honestly. Does more harm than nothing at all.
DannyRight. …Right. God. Okay.
YouWhat I can say, hand on heart, is that you were brilliant with clients. Genuinely. You'd walk into a room that had gone off and just, sort of — nobody else in that office could do that.
DannyThat's not what goes on the form, though, is it.
YouSome of it does. I'd write that bit gladly. It's the rest of it — there's always a box for reliability and I'd be sat there — yeah. Look, is there someone who saw more of your good stuff than I did? Priya ran the client side, didn't she?
DannyShe's gone. Lisbon, or — somewhere over there.
YouPeople answer emails in Lisbon, Danny. And listen — I'm not doing this to be difficult. Would a factual one do — dates, role, that kind of thing? HR send those out without blinking. It fills the slot, and nobody reads anything into it.
DannyThat's the coward's one, that is.
YouMaybe. It's the one that doesn't hurt you, though.
Danny…Yeah. All right. I'll email Priya. I'm not — I'm not cross with you, by the way. I just thought — no. Never mind.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Danny names the real problem before you get anywhere near naming it. What does he name — and what does that tell you about why there's no argument?

2. You close one door and open two others. What are the two other doors, and who has to do the work in each?

3. Danny calls the factual reference something rude. What does he call it, and what do you do with that — argue, apologise, or something else?

1. He names the Renner account, which means he already knew where the weak spot was and had been hoping nobody would say it out loud. That's why the call doesn't turn into a row: you aren't telling him news, you're only refusing to pretend, and it's hard to argue with something you already believe.
2. Priya, who saw the work he was genuinely good at and could write about it warmly, and the factual reference from HR, which fills the slot without anybody having to judge him. The first door needs Danny to send an email; the second needs nothing from him at all.
3. He calls it the coward's one. You don't argue and you don't apologise — you agree it might well be, then point out it's also the one that leaves him undamaged, which ends the argument rather than winning it.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the favour you can't do. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — the guessing is what makes them stick.

Say it like you mean it. The temptation is to go bright and brisk, because brisk feels kinder and gets you out faster, but a cheerful no sounds like someone who doesn't much care. Go the other way — slower than is comfortable, flatter than you'd like, warm in the pauses rather than in the words. Let the awkward silences sit there instead of filling them, because the silence is doing the work: it's the thing that tells him you'd rather not be saying this.

🚦 Stopping the yes before it happens

Phrase 1

He's already assuming you'll agree. You need to stop the conversation rolling past you — without sounding like a refusal is coming, because you want him listening rather than defending. Which line opens the conversation instead of closing it?

A. "Yeah, no problem — just send me the form."
B. "Can I be honest with you before I say yes?"
C. "I'd rather you asked somebody else, if I'm honest."
B — "Can I be honest with you before I say yes?"
The clever part is "before I say yes", because it refuses nothing — it holds the yes up in the air a moment longer while you explain what saying it would actually mean, so he hears the problem while he's still hopeful rather than already wounded. A hands him a yes you'll have to take back later, which is worse than a no now. C is a refusal wearing honesty as a coat, and it lands before he's had any chance to understand it.
Phrase 2

You'd sooner have this awkward phone call than send off a reference nobody is happy with. Put the chunks in order:

than write youI'd rathersomething lukewarmtell you now
"I'd rather tell you now than write you something lukewarm."
Lukewarm water is neither hot nor cold, and a lukewarm reference is the written version of that — polite, mild, praising nothing in particular, and every recruiter who reads one knows precisely what it means. Saying the word out loud is what turns the refusal into a kindness, because you're measuring your no against the alternative he was actually going to get, which was worse.

🪞 Saying the thing you can't say

Phrase 3

You want him to understand that a reference from you exists — it just isn't the glowing thing in his head. Which version is honest without being cruel?

A. "I'll do it, don't worry about it."
B. "You really wouldn't like what I'd have to write."
C. "I could give you one, but I'm not sure it'd be the one you want."
C — "I could give you one, but I'm not sure it'd be the one you want."
It's built out of two hedges doing quiet work: "could" admits you're perfectly able to, so you're not hiding behind a rule, and "I'm not sure" softens something you are in fact completely sure about, which leaves him room to ask rather than react. B says the same thing with the padding stripped out, which is why it lands like a threat. A postpones the whole problem onto paper, where you can't take it back.
Phrase 4

The danger isn't your opinion of him — it's the questions they'll put to you, which you can't dodge. Fill the gap with two words: "I'd have to answer honestly ___ ___ asked me about deadlines."

"I'd have to answer honestly if they asked me about deadlines."
The whole sentence is a conditional, which is why it works — "I'd" pairs with "if they asked" to describe something that hasn't happened yet, so you aren't accusing him of anything, you're describing a phone call. And "have to" shifts the pressure off your judgement and onto the situation, because it's the recruiter asking rather than you volunteering.
Phrase 5

He's braced for you to tell him he was useless. He wasn't, and the distinction matters to him enormously. Put the chunks in order:

it's that I couldn'tIt's not thatsay you were strongyou were bad —
"It's not that you were bad — it's that I couldn't say you were strong."
This pulls apart two things people normally hear as one, which is the entire point: being bad is a verdict on him, whereas not being able to call him strong is a limit on what you're able to write. The gap between "bad" and "not strong" is where most of us actually live at work, so naming it lets him keep his self-respect while still hearing the answer.

💛 Saying the true good thing

Phrase 6

There's one thing about him you'd defend anywhere, and he needs to hear it said as firmly as the bad news was. The gap takes a three-word phrase made of a body part and a gesture — the one people make when they swear something is true: "What I can say, ___ ___ ___, is that you were brilliant with clients."

"What I can say, hand on heart, is that you were brilliant with clients."
Putting a hand on your heart is the gesture for a promise, so "hand on heart" means roughly "I'm swearing to this, I'm not just being nice to you". You need it exactly because you've been critical a moment earlier — without it the compliment sounds like the soft padding that always follows bad news, and he'd discount it. "What I can say" is the other half of the trick, since it quietly admits there are things you can't.
Phrase 7

He's gone quiet, and you can hear him deciding that you're punishing him for something. Say something out loud, in about six words, that names what he's thinking and denies it — no explanation, no defence.

"I'm not doing this to be difficult."
It works because it goes straight at the suspicion rather than round it, and short sentences are believable in a way long ones aren't — the moment you start explaining why you're not being difficult, you sound like somebody who is. Say it, leave the silence after it, and let him decide.

🚪 Leaving him somewhere to go

Phrase 8

You can't be his referee. Somebody else could be — probably somebody who saw the half of him you didn't. Which line hands him something, rather than simply refusing him?

A. "Is there someone who saw more of your good stuff than I did?"
B. "You should probably ask somebody else, then."
C. "I'm not really the right person for this."
A — "Is there someone who saw more of your good stuff than I did?"
It's a question, so he has to think rather than just absorb, and it quietly locates the problem in how narrow your view was rather than how small his talent is — "more of your good stuff than I did" takes for granted that the good stuff exists. B and C say much the same thing as instructions, which leaves him standing outside a closed door working out where to knock next.
Phrase 9

There is a reference you can give: the bare one, with no judgement in it, that any HR department will send out. Offer it as a question rather than a consolation prize. Put the chunks in order:

dates, role,Would athat kind of thing?factual one do —
"Would a factual one do — dates, role, that kind of thing?"
"Would … do?" is how you offer something smaller than what was asked for while letting the other person decide whether it's enough, which is why it doesn't feel like being fobbed off. Listing the contents — dates, role, that kind of thing — makes it concrete and unfrightening, so he can picture the actual document instead of imagining what you might have put in it.
Phrase 10

It's over, it went as well as it could, and he's about to go. Say the thing that makes this a not now rather than a not ever — then add one short sentence that stops it sounding like the polite noise people make at the end of phone calls.

"Ask me again for the next one. I mean that."
The first sentence turns your no into a date rather than a verdict, because you're inviting the next request instead of merely surviving this one. The second exists because everybody says warm things at the end of a difficult call and nobody believes any of them — "I mean that" is you noticing that in advance and refusing it, which is the only reason he might take you at your word.

Now it's your phone call. Say every answer out loud before you write anything — and resist the urge to be quick. Use the timer: this is a conversation to give its full four minutes, not one to escape from in ninety seconds.

The Twist

🎯 They ring you anyway

The call ended about as well as it could. Danny took it, said he'd email Priya, and you both settled on the factual reference from HR — everything tidy, everything agreed, nothing left for you to do. Then on Thursday your phone goes and it's the recruiter, because Danny had listed you before that conversation and never got round to changing it. She's brisk, she's got four minutes, and her second question is: "And would you say he consistently hits his deadlines?" There's no HR form to hide behind now and no Priya to point her at — it's just you, live, and she's waiting. Start the timer and answer her out loud. You can't lie to her and you can't quietly sink him, and those are the only two easy options. Find the third one.

Before you close the tab…

Go back over what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I stopped him assuming a yes before he'd heard what a yes would cost him.
  • I said the true good thing about him as firmly as I said the bad news.
  • I gave him at least one real route to a reference instead of only a refusal.
  • I let the awkward silences sit there instead of talking over them.
  • I left the door open for next time, and said something that made him believe it.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one to run again — take the twist a second time and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Almost nobody says no to this. Most people write the reference, keep it mild, praise the friendliness, say nothing whatever about the work, and the recruiter reads it in nine seconds and understands perfectly. The candidate never finds out. Everyone stays comfortable, and the man who could have fixed the one thing holding him back carries on not knowing what it is.

Four awkward minutes buy something the comfortable version can't: he now knows what the problem is, in words, from someone who liked him. That isn't a gift, exactly, and he won't thank you this week. But it's more than he had on Tuesday.

The friendship might not survive it, which is a real risk and worth saying rather than pretending otherwise. It's only that the alternative was never keeping the friendship — it was keeping the appearance of one, at his expense, without telling him.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is a reference meant to be honest, or is it understood by everyone involved to be a formality that nobody quite believes? If it's really the second, does refusing to write a mild one make you principled — or just someone who hasn't understood the rules of the game?
  2. We tend to treat honesty as a favour we're doing someone. But Danny didn't ask for the truth about his work; he asked for a reference. Is there a point at which telling somebody what's wrong with them is something you're doing for your own comfort rather than theirs — and how would you know which one you were doing?
