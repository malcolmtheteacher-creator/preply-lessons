The Bridge Between Lessons

The Normans who conquered England in 1066 were themselves descendants of those same Vikings and North Europeans. Their ancestors had invaded northern France (Normandy) just as the Saxons had invaded Britain five centuries earlier. Now, in 1337, nearly 300 years after the Norman Conquest, the Norman Kings of England—now thoroughly English—turned their eyes to France.

A young king named Edward III decided he had a claim to be King of France as well as King of England. And for the next 116 years, the two kingdoms would bleed fighting over that claim. This is the Hundred Years' War.

II. Crécy, Poitiers & the Black Prince

Edward III and the Claim to France

H.E. Marshall tells it with her characteristic clarity:

The war which began is called the "Hundred Years' War" because it lasted, with times of peace between, for a hundred years, and it began because Edward said that he had a right to be King of France as well as King of England. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter on the Hundred Years' War

Whether Edward's claim was just or not, it drove him to France with an army to fight for it. And what he found there changed the nature of warfare itself.

The Battle of Crécy, 1346

Edward had been marching through France for some time when he heard that King Philip was close behind him with an army of one hundred and twenty thousand men. He himself had only twenty thousand men, but he resolved to camp where he was, on a rising ground near the little French village of Crécy, and there conquer or be conquered. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter on Crécy

On August 26, 1346, the armies met. The French outnumbered the English six to one. But Edward had something the French did not: the English longbow.

The Longbow Revolution

The English longbow was not a new weapon, but it had never been used in such numbers with such deadly effect. Made of yew wood, six feet tall, it could pierce armour at distance. A skilled archer could loose six arrows per minute. When thousands of archers fired together, the sky literally darkened with arrows.

The result was catastrophic for the French knights in their heavy plate armour on horseback. They could not advance; they could not retreat. They died by thousands.

Marshall vividly describes Edward's command of the battle:

Edward gave command of one part of his army to his young son Edward, the Black Prince. Prince Edward took his name from the black armour which he always wore, and at this time he was only seventeen years old. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter on Crécy

The Black Prince fought with such courage that King Edward, watching his son's part of the battle, refused to send him reinforcements. "Let the boy win his spurs," Edward said. And the boy did. The Black Prince, only seventeen, proved himself a warrior prince that day.

When after the battle the Black Prince was made a knight, he took the motto and the crest of the King of Bohemia for his own, and it has been borne ever since by the eldest son of the King of England. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter on Crécy
The historian's note: The King of Bohemia, John of Luxembourg, fought on the French side at Crécy. Blind with age, he rode into the thickest of the battle and was killed. The Black Prince, impressed by his valour, adopted the Bohemian motto "Ich diene" (I serve) along with the three feathers crest. This emblem still marks the heir to the British throne.

Poitiers, 1356: The Black Prince's Greatest Victory

Ten years later, the Black Prince would achieve an even more spectacular victory. At Poitiers, he faced a French army again, and again won against the odds. This time, he captured the French king himself.

The story of Poitiers: The Black Prince, now a seasoned warrior, was retreating when the French army caught him near the town of Poitiers. Rather than flee, he turned and gave battle. Using the same tactics as Crécy—the longbow, strong positions, disciplined troops—he shattered the French cavalry. The King of France, John II, was captured, along with many nobles. The ransom that England extracted from France was so vast that it crippled the French kingdom for years.

For two decades, the Black Prince seemed invincible. But glory does not last forever. He would die in 1376, just a year before his father, having never become king.

III. The Black Death & Agincourt

The Black Death, 1348-1350

Just as Edward's armies were winning their great victories, a shadow swept across Europe far more terrible than any army. In 1348, the Black Death arrived in England.

The Plague That Changed Everything

The Black Death was bubonic plague, carried by rats and fleas. It killed perhaps one-third to one-half of England's population. In some villages, every single person died. Mass graves were dug. Priests died faster than they could bury the dead. Entire families were wiped out in days.

The social fabric of England tore apart. With so many dead, there were not enough workers. Peasants, suddenly valuable, demanded higher wages and more freedom. The rigid feudal system that had lasted for centuries began to crack.

The long consequence: The Black Death led, within 30 years, to the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. Desperate peasants marched on London demanding an end to serfdom. The young King Richard II met with them at Mile End and promised reforms. The revolt was crushed, but something had shifted. The medieval world of absolute lords and hopeless peasants was beginning to change. The plague, paradoxically, had given power to the poor.

Henry V and Agincourt, 1415

Sixty-seven years after the Black Death, a new king arose who would revive England's claim to France with stunning force. His name was Henry V, and he was only 17 when he became king. By the time he was 27, he had won the greatest English victory of the entire war.

In October 1415, his army of about 8,000 men—tired, sick, and hungry—came face to face with a French army of perhaps 30,000 near the village of Agincourt.

The French had an army ten times greater than that of the English; many of the English, too, were sick and ill, weary, ragged and half fed, and yet they won the battle. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter on Agincourt

Once again, the longbow proved decisive. Once again, English discipline and courage defeated French numbers. In one afternoon, perhaps 10,000 Frenchmen lay dead on a muddy field. English losses were fewer than 2,000.

Henry V on the Field

Marshall describes a moment that captures Henry's character. When he had ridden all along the lines encouraging his soldiers, he dismounted his horse. He took his place among his soldiers, not apart from them. With the royal standard waving above him, the young king fought as a common soldier fights. This kind of leadership—the leader who shares danger with the led—is the stuff of legend.

The legend grows: Henry V's victory at Agincourt became so famous that it echoed through literature and legend. Four centuries later, William Shakespeare would immortalise it, putting into Henry's mouth the words "We few, we happy few, we band of brothers." Whether Shakespeare's language was Marshall's source or not, the sentiment captures what Agincourt meant: a small, desperate band of Englishmen winning against impossible odds through courage, discipline, and unity.

Joan of Arc and the French Recovery

Yet Agincourt did not end the war. The French, humbled and nearly broken, found new hope in an unexpected form: a peasant girl from Domrémy who said she heard voices from heaven telling her to save France.

Joan of Arc, 1429-1431

Joan of Arc was perhaps 17 years old when she claimed to have had divine visions. She convinced the French Dauphin (the uncrowned king) to let her fight. Dressed in armour, she led French forces to victory after victory. English and French alike were astounded. But after about a year, she was captured, tried by English and French clergy alike, and burned at the stake for heresy in 1431.

Yet her brief life changed everything. The French, inspired by her sacrifice, found the will to continue. Slowly, over the next 20 years, they pushed the English out of France.

The End of the War, 1453

By 1453, it was over. England, which had held vast territories in France, was left with only Calais. And even Calais would be lost within a century.

The defeat within victory: By 1453, England had lost almost everything it had fought for. Yet the Hundred Years' War was not entirely futile. It forged an English identity separate from France. It showed that the English could win against the odds. It made the longbow legendary. And it created stories—of the Black Prince, of Henry V, of Joan of Arc—that would echo through the centuries.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Why did the longbow matter?

The longbow was not a new invention, but at Crécy and Agincourt it changed everything. Why is a weapon that is just a stick and string so much more powerful than armoured knights on horseback? What does this tell us about strategy and technology?

The Black Death's strange gift

The plague killed a third of Europe. Yet it led to higher wages for workers, more freedom, and the eventual end of feudalism. How can a catastrophe ever be beneficial? Is there any good that comes from mass death?

Leaders who fight with their soldiers

The Black Prince and Henry V both fought from horseback in the thick of battle, not from a safe position. Is this brave leadership or reckless? What do soldiers need from their leaders?

Joan of Arc's impossible claim

A peasant girl says she hears the voice of God telling her to save France. Everyone thinks she's mad. Yet she wins battles and becomes a hero. How do we distinguish between madness and genuine faith or vision?

Victory that feels like defeat

England won nearly every battle in the Hundred Years' War but ended up losing almost all of France. If you win the battles but lose the war, did you really win? What's the difference between tactical and strategic success?

The stories we tell

Marshall tells the story of Crécy and Agincourt as tales of English courage against odds. A French historian would tell a different story. Whose version is true? Does every war have multiple truths?
