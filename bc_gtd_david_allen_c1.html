Opening Questions

The premise in one paragraph

Allen argues that most modern stress is not caused by having too much to do, but by carrying unfinished commitments around in your head. Every promise you have made to yourself — a phone call to return, a birthday to remember, a project to finish — sits there as what he calls an open loop. The cure, he says, is not to work harder; it is to get those loops out of your head and into a trusted external system.

The Five Stages of Mastering Workflow

Allen breaks the whole system down into a five-stage process. Read the passage carefully — the questions afterwards check both your understanding and the language used to describe each stage.

1. Capture

The first move is to gather absolutely everything that has your attention — tasks, ideas, half-formed worries — into trusted “in” trays. The point here is comprehensiveness, not organisation. Until your head is empty, the rest of the system cannot be trusted.

2. Clarify

Next, you process what you have captured, item by item. For each one, you ask: what is it, and is it actionable? If it is not actionable, it goes to the bin, into reference, or onto a someday/maybe list. If it is actionable, you identify the very next physical action — the smallest concrete step you could take.

3. Organise

Each clarified item is then placed into the right list: a calendar entry if it must happen on a specific day, a next-actions list grouped by context (at the computer, on the phone, on errands), a waiting-for list if you are tracking someone else’s response, or a project list if the outcome will take more than one step.

4. Reflect

Once a week you sit down for the weekly review. You glance at your calendar, refresh your lists, look ahead to the coming week, and check that nothing has slipped through the cracks. Allen calls this the critical success factor of the whole method — without it, the system silently goes stale.

5. Engage

Finally, you do the work. With everything captured and clarified, choosing what to do becomes much simpler. You scan your context-based lists, weigh time available, energy and priority, and pick the most appropriate next action. The aim is not heroic productivity but appropriate engagement — the right thing, at the right moment, with a clear head.

Comprehension & language

1. Why does Allen insist that capture must be comprehensive before anything else happens?

Because the mind cannot trust a partial system. As long as some commitments are still floating around in your head, it will keep reminding you of them — and the rest of the system feels unsafe by association.

2. What is the difference between a project and a next action in GTD?

A project is any outcome that needs more than one step (e.g. “organise birthday party”). A next action is the single physical thing you would do next to move it forward (e.g. “email the venue to ask about availability”).

3. What does Allen mean by “context”, and why is it useful?

Context is the situation or tool an action requires — at the computer, on the phone, in town. Grouping next actions by context means that whenever you find yourself in one, you can see at a glance everything that could be done from there.

4. Allen calls the weekly review “the critical success factor”. What goes wrong without it?

Lists drift out of date, items quietly slip through the cracks, and the system loses your trust. Once you stop trusting it, you start carrying things in your head again — and the open loops return.

5. What is meant by the phrase “slipped through the cracks”?

Things that should have been dealt with but were quietly forgotten or overlooked — not because of bad intent, but because they fell between systems.

6. Rephrase “the system silently goes stale” in your own words.

Without you noticing, the system stops being accurate or useful. The lists are still there, but they no longer reflect what is actually happening or what really needs doing.

How to process each item

For every item, decide and say aloud:

  • Is it actionable? If no — bin, reference, or someday/maybe?
  • If yes — what is the very next physical action? Try to phrase it as a verb plus an object (e.g. “ring the garage”, not “sort out the car”).
  • Will it take less than two minutes? If so, do it now. If not — defer it (which list?), delegate it (to whom?), or schedule it (when?).
  • Is it part of a bigger project? If so, what is the project called?

Reporting Allen’s Ideas

“Allen says…” only gets you so far. The right reporting verb signals exactly how you stand in relation to the writer — whether you find them careful, defensive, persuasive, or overstated. Below are the verbs we will use today.

The toolkit

claim that… State as true — often hinting that the speaker is not yet convinced. Allen claims that the mind is for having ideas, not holding them.
argue that… Put forward with reasoning. Neutral and respectful. Allen argues that stress comes from unfinished commitments.
contend that… Argue strongly, often against opposition. Formal. Allen contends that capture must come before organisation.
maintain that… Keep insisting on a view, especially over time. Allen maintains that the weekly review is non-negotiable.
insist that… Press a point with emphasis — sometimes a little too firmly. Allen insists that everything be captured, with no exceptions.
advocate (for) / advocate + ing Publicly recommend a course of action. Allen advocates externalising every commitment.
propose that… Put forward as a solution. Often takes the subjunctive. Allen proposes that we ask one question of every item: is it actionable?
concede that… Reluctantly admit a point — usually one that weakens your own argument. Even Allen concedes that the system requires discipline to maintain.
caution against… Warn people away from something. Allen cautions against keeping any list inside your head.
draw a distinction between Carefully separate two things that are often confused. Allen draws a distinction between projects and next actions.

Choose the best reporting verb

For each sentence, decide which verb fits best and why — then check.

1. Allen ____ that most modern stress is not caused by having too much to do, but by carrying unfinished commitments around in your head.

Options: claims / argues / concedes

argues — he is putting forward reasoning, not making a slippery claim and not admitting a weakness. Claims would suggest you doubt him; concedes would imply this point hurts his own case, which it does not.

2. Allen ____ against trying to remember everything, on the grounds that the mind is too unreliable a storage device.

Options: cautions / proposes / maintains

cautions — the structure is caution against + gerund/noun, used when warning someone away from a behaviour.

3. Allen ____ that the weekly review is the single critical success factor of the whole system.

Options: insists / proposes / concedes

insists — the language he uses around the weekly review (“non-negotiable”, “the critical success factor”) is emphatic. Maintains would also work but is gentler. Insists matches the strength of the original.

4. Allen ____ a useful distinction between a project and a next action.

Options: draws / claims / proposes

draws — the standard collocation is draw a distinction between X and Y. The other verbs do not collocate with distinction in this way.

5. Even Allen ____ that without the weekly review the whole system will quietly fall apart.

Options: concedes / advocates / argues

concedes — the signal is “Even Allen…”, which prepares the reader for an admission that slightly weakens his case. The verb has to do that work.

Now paraphrase Allen yourself

Each statement below comes from GTD. Rewrite it using the most appropriate reporting verb. Try not to use the same verb twice.

a. “You should never trust your head as a storage device.”

Allen cautions against using your head as a storage device. (Or: Allen insists that the head is not a reliable storage device.)

b. “If something will take less than two minutes, do it now.”

Allen proposes that any task taking less than two minutes be done immediately. (Subjunctive: be done, not is done.)

c. “Even with a perfect system, you still have to sit down once a week and look at it honestly.”

Allen concedes that even a well-built system requires a weekly, honest review.

d. “Capturing everything is the absolute foundation. You cannot skip it.”

Allen maintains that capture is the foundation of the entire method. (Or contends, if you want a stronger, more debate-flavoured verb.)

Critique — using the toolkit

Cal Newport, author of Deep Work, is not entirely convinced by Allen. Read the four bullet points below. Then have the discussion that follows — using a fresh reporting verb for each speaker each time.

Newport’s position, in four points:

• A perfectly organised inbox is still just a beautifully organised distraction.

• The real question is not how we work but what we work on.

• GTD encourages a kind of frantic responsiveness that crowds out deep, sustained thinking.

• Saying no to most things matters more than processing everything that arrives.
