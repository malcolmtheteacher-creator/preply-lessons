Opening Questions

David Allen’s book Getting Things Done (often shortened to GTD) was first published in 2001. More than two decades later it is still treated as the standard reference for personal productivity. Before we look at why, a few questions about your own situation.

The premise in one paragraph

Allen argues that most modern stress is not caused by having too much to do, but by carrying unfinished commitments around in your head. Every promise you have made to yourself — a phone call to return, a birthday to remember, a project to finish — sits there as what he calls an open loop. The cure, he says, is not to work harder; it is to get those loops out of your head and into a trusted external system.

The Five Stages of Mastering Workflow

Allen breaks the whole system down into a five-stage process. Read the passage carefully — the questions afterwards check both your understanding and the language used to describe each stage.

1. Capture

The first move is to gather absolutely everything that has your attention — tasks, ideas, half-formed worries — into trusted “in” trays. The point here is comprehensiveness, not organisation. Until your head is empty, the rest of the system cannot be trusted.

2. Clarify

Next, you process what you have captured, item by item. For each one, you ask: what is it, and is it actionable? If it is not actionable, it goes to the bin, into reference, or onto a someday/maybe list. If it is actionable, you identify the very next physical action — the smallest concrete step you could take.

3. Organise

Each clarified item is then placed into the right list: a calendar entry if it must happen on a specific day, a next-actions list grouped by context (at the computer, on the phone, on errands), a waiting-for list if you are tracking someone else’s response, or a project list if the outcome will take more than one step.

4. Reflect

Once a week you sit down for the weekly review. You glance at your calendar, refresh your lists, look ahead to the coming week, and check that nothing has slipped through the cracks. Allen calls this the critical success factor of the whole method — without it, the system silently goes stale.

5. Engage

Finally, you do the work. With everything captured and clarified, choosing what to do becomes much simpler. You scan your context-based lists, weigh time available, energy and priority, and pick the most appropriate next action. The aim is not heroic productivity but appropriate engagement — the right thing, at the right moment, with a clear head.

Comprehension & language

1. Why does Allen insist that capture must be comprehensive before anything else happens?

Because the mind cannot trust a partial system. As long as some commitments are still floating around in your head, it will keep reminding you of them — and the rest of the system feels unsafe by association.

2. What is the difference between a project and a next action in GTD?

A project is any outcome that needs more than one step (e.g. “organise birthday party”). A next action is the single physical thing you would do next to move it forward (e.g. “email the venue to ask about availability”).

3. What does Allen mean by “context”, and why is it useful?

Context is the situation or tool an action requires — at the computer, on the phone, in town. Grouping next actions by context means that whenever you find yourself in one, you can see at a glance everything that could be done from there.

4. Allen calls the weekly review “the critical success factor”. What goes wrong without it?

Lists drift out of date, items quietly slip through the cracks, and the system loses your trust. Once you stop trusting it, you start carrying things in your head again — and the open loops return.

5. What is meant by the phrase “slipped through the cracks”?

Things that should have been dealt with but were quietly forgotten or overlooked — not because of bad intent, but because they fell between systems.

6. Rephrase “the system silently goes stale” in your own words.

Without you noticing, the system stops being accurate or useful. The lists are still there, but they no longer reflect what is actually happening or what really needs doing.

Your Inbox

Now you try the method on a real inbox. Below are ten items that have just “landed” in your head. Your task is to walk through them out loud, applying the GTD process to each one.

The ten items

  1. You have not replied to a friend’s WhatsApp message from last Tuesday.
  2. The car is making a strange noise when you brake.
  3. You should probably learn Italian one day.
  4. Your mother’s birthday is in three weeks.
  5. Your laptop battery only lasts about two hours now.
  6. You promised your boss a draft of the quarterly report by Friday.
  7. The kitchen tap is dripping.
  8. You meant to read that book a colleague recommended six months ago.
  9. You owe your brother £40 from the weekend.
  10. You want to start running again before the summer.

How to process each item

For every item, decide and say aloud:

  • Is it actionable? If no — bin, reference, or someday/maybe?
  • If yes — what is the very next physical action? Try to phrase it as a verb plus an object (e.g. “ring the garage”, not “sort out the car”).
  • Will it take less than two minutes? If so, do it now. If not — defer it (which list?), delegate it (to whom?), or schedule it (when?).
  • Is it part of a bigger project? If so, what is the project called?

Critique & Discussion

GTD has admirers and critics. The most common complaint is that it is too reactive — that it processes whatever lands on you efficiently, but does not, on its own, tell you whether you are working on the right things. Cal Newport, for instance, argues that without “deep work” on a small number of important projects, even a perfect inbox is just a beautifully organised distraction.

For discussion:

Which part of the GTD method do you think would help you most right now — capture, clarify, organise, reflect, or engage? Why that one?

Which part do you think you would resist or quietly skip? What does that resistance tell you?

Is “mind like water” a realistic goal, or does it sound a bit too tidy — the kind of calm that only people without small children or chaotic jobs can afford?

Allen built GTD before smartphones existed. Has the constant ping of notifications made the method more necessary, or impossible?

If you had to choose between Allen’s gentle, comprehensive system and Newport’s ruthless “say no to almost everything” approach, which would suit your temperament better — and why?
