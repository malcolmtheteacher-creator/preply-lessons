Is lying always wrong, even when the truth would hurt someone?

You're in a hurry, so you jump the queue for the bus. It's only one person, and no harm done, surely? But what if everyone on the street did the same thing? About 250 years ago, a professor in Prussia turned that simple question into a test for right and wrong.

Today: You'll meet Kant and his test for right and wrong, so you can talk about rules and duty and decide whether some actions are wrong for everyone, always.

Meet Kant

The man who stayed at home

Immanuel Kant was born in 1724 in Königsberg, in Prussia. Today the city is called Kaliningrad. He lived there almost his whole life and hardly ever travelled far from it. He taught at the local university for decades. A famous story says that his afternoon walk was so regular that his neighbours set their clocks by it. Yet his mind travelled further than almost anyone's.

What is the famous story about Kant's afternoon walk?

It was so regular that, the story says, his neighbours set their clocks by it.
💡

Woken up by Hume

Kant later said that reading the Scottish thinker David Hume woke him from his 'dogmatic slumber': a deep sleep of believing things without questioning them. He spent years thinking and publishing very little. Then, in 1781, at the age of 57, he published the Critique of Pure Reason, one of the most important and most difficult books in philosophy. It asks what the human mind can really know, and where its limits are.

What did Kant mean by his 'dogmatic slumber'?

Believing things without questioning them. Reading Hume woke him up and made him think again from the beginning.
⚖️

The moral law

In 1785 Kant turned to right and wrong. He said a good action is not about results or feelings: it's about duty. Before you act, ask: could I want everyone to do this? If everyone lied, nobody could trust a promise, so lying fails the test. He added a second rule: never treat a person only as a tool. These ideas still shape how we think about human rights.

What question should you ask before you act, according to Kant?

Could I want everyone to do this? If the answer is no, the action is wrong.
Talk: Kant argued that you should not lie even to protect someone from harm. Does that sound wise to you, or too strict? What would you do?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. She didn't want to visit her difficult uncle, but she went because she felt it was her duty.
duty (n) — something you must do because it is morally or legally right. Example: "Many people feel it is their duty to vote in elections."
2. Kant wanted rules that are universal: they must apply to every person in every country, with no special cases.
universal (adj) — true for, or involving, everyone everywhere. Example: "Smiling is an almost universal sign of friendliness."
3. He refused the bribe on principle, even though nobody would ever have found out.
principle (n) — a basic belief or rule that guides how you behave. Example: "It's against my principles to buy fake designer products."
4. Even in prison, Kant would say, a person keeps their dignity and must not be treated like an object.
dignity (n) — the value and respect that every human being deserves. Example: "Good nurses treat every patient with dignity."
5. Kant said a good action is not judged by its consequences, but by the reason you do it.
consequence (n) — a result of an action, often a bad one. Example: "Missing the deadline had serious consequences for the whole team."
6. He only made friends with rich people so he could exploit them for free holidays.
exploit (v) — to use someone unfairly for your own advantage. Example: "The company exploited young workers by paying them almost nothing."
7. Kant's rule against lying had no exception: it was wrong even when it seemed kind.
exception (n) — a case that does not follow the usual rule. Example: "Everyone has to wear a helmet on the building site, and there are no exceptions."

See it in action

Here is the test in action. You want to borrow money from a friend and say, "I'll pay you back next week," although you know you can't. Kant asks: could everyone make false promises whenever they need money? If they did, nobody would believe any promise, and borrowing this way would stop working. So the plan fails the test. There's a second problem too: you are using your friend only as a tool to get cash, not respecting them as a person.

Kant believed that right and wrong depend on duty, not on feelings or results. Before you act, you should ask whether you could want everyone to do the same. For example, if you want to make a false promise, imagine everyone doing it: nobody would trust promises any more, so it's wrong. He also said you must never treat a person only as a tool for your own goals.

Connect

Compare

Hume and Kant: Hume said that feelings, not reason, drive our moral choices. Kant, who said reading Hume woke him up, believed right and wrong must come from reason and duty. When you decide what is right, do you mostly feel it or think it through?
For Hume, we feel that cruelty is wrong, and reason only explains it afterwards. For Kant, feelings change from person to person, so only reason can give rules that are the same for everyone. Many people use both: a feeling warns them, and reason checks it.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Jean-Jacques Rousseau

What was Rousseau's big idea about people and society?

People are born good and free, but society, with its envy, comparing and inequality, spoils them: 'Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains.'
From Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

What did Leibniz mean by 'the best of all possible worlds'?

That God looked at every possible world and chose the best one. Even with its evil, a world with free people is better than any other.
From René Descartes

What was the one thing Descartes could not doubt?

That he was thinking, and so that he existed: "I think, therefore I am."
From Augustine of Hippo

What did Augustine mean by a divided will?

That part of us wants to do what is good while another part says 'not yet', so the will fights against itself. Looking back honestly helps us see this and change.

Final review

From memory, out loud. Then check.

1. What is Kant's test for right and wrong?

Before you act, ask: could I want everyone to do this? If the action would stop working if everyone did it, it's wrong. And never treat people only as tools.

2. Where did Kant spend almost his whole life?

In his home town, Königsberg in Prussia (today Kaliningrad).

3. Why does lying fail Kant's test?

If everyone lied, nobody could trust what anyone said, so lying itself would stop working.

4. What does 'dignity' mean? Give an example.

The value and respect every human being deserves. For example, nurses treat patients with dignity even when they are very ill.
