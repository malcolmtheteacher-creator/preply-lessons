Setting the Scene

You are a senior leader in a company with a strong brand and strong values. This week, you discovered something: a profitable business practice—one that generates millions in annual revenue and is completely legal—is causing real, measurable harm to a vulnerable group of customers.

You bring the evidence to the board. You expect them to care. You're wrong. The board's response is clear: the practice is profitable. The customers in question are a small segment. The legal and regulatory risk is minimal. The board wants to continue.

You disagree fundamentally. So do three other senior leaders. But you're outnumbered. You face a choice: accept the decision and stay, challenge the board and risk your career, or resign on principle.

Why this matters at C-suite level: Ethical leadership at senior levels is not about avoiding bad behavior. It's about standing alone when the institution has decided that profit matters more than harm. The moves you make in this conversation define both your integrity and your influence. Most leaders never face this test. Some of us do.

Ethical leadership at C-suite level is not a speech. It's a decision. Do you stay and try to change the system from within? Do you leave and signal that the problem is serious? Do you blow the whistle publicly? Each choice has consequences—for you, for your career, for the customers, for the organization. The language you use matters less than the action you take. But language is how you prepare the world for that action.

Language Moves for Ethical Leadership

These moves separate principled stands from performative ethics. The language here has to carry weight because the stakes are real.

1 The Ethical Framing +

This is the move that reframes a business question as an ethical one. You're not saying "this is unprofitable." You're saying "this causes harm." You're shifting the conversation from risk/reward to right/wrong.

Weak

"I'm concerned about this practice. It could have reputational risks."

Strong

"This practice causes measurable harm to customers who have no choice and no awareness. We profit from their vulnerability. That's not a business risk to manage—that's a moral problem to solve. I can't be part of the team that continues this with full knowledge of what we're doing."

2 Moral Courage Language +

This is the move that names what you're doing and what it costs. You're not hiding behind policy or process. You're saying "I am choosing principle over profit, and I understand the cost."

Weak

"I feel strongly about this, but I understand if we disagree."

Strong

"I understand this is profitable. I understand the board has decided to continue. I'm telling you clearly: I can't endorse this decision. I'm prepared to step aside if that's what you decide. But I won't be complicit by staying silent."

3 Challenging the Board +

This is the move where you question the board's judgment without attacking the board's character. You're saying "your decision is wrong" without saying "you are bad people."

What doesn't work

"You're all unethical." This makes it about character and puts people on the defensive.

Strong

"You've chosen to prioritize this revenue stream over the harm it causes. I think that's the wrong choice—not because you're bad people, but because profit shouldn't be the deciding factor when we have evidence of real harm. In five years, if this becomes public—and harm always becomes public eventually—this decision will define us. I think we should decide now who we want to be."

4 Values-Based Decision Language +

The move is to ground your position in values that are bigger than you. You're not saying "I believe." You're saying "our company has always stood for X, and this contradicts X."

Strong move

"We talk about putting customers first. We talk about operating with integrity. This practice does the opposite. Either those values mean something, or they don't. If they mean something, we can't continue this. If they don't, we should stop saying them, because it's dishonest."

5 Public Accountability Statements +

If you choose to go public—to the media, regulators, or whistleblower channels—your language needs to be precise and factual. This is not about venting. It's about creating a record.

What fails

"The company is corrupt and terrible." This is opinion, not fact. It weakens your case.

Strong

"Between March 2023 and March 2024, the company generated $50m in revenue from a specific practice. Our internal analysis showed this practice disproportionately affected customers in low-income segments. When I presented this analysis to the board, they voted to continue the practice. I have documentation of this decision and of the analysis that informed it."

6 The Resignation-as-Last-Resort Conversation +

If you decide to resign, the language is crucial. You're not burning bridges. You're making a clear statement about why you can't stay. You're also protecting yourself—making sure your reasons are on the record.

Structure

1. I've made a decision: I'm stepping down.
2. Here's why (facts, not anger).
3. Here's when (sufficient notice to be professional).
4. Here's what I need (a statement of my concerns in my departure letter, to protect myself and signal the seriousness).

What matters

Your departure letter becomes a historical record. Write it carefully. Be factual. Be complete. Make sure your concerns are documented in a way that can't be disputed later.

Live Scenario: Ethical Dilemma at the Board Table

The Situation: A profitable practice is causing measurable harm to a vulnerable customer segment. You've presented evidence to the board. The board has voted to continue. You have 24 hours to decide whether to accept this, challenge it formally, resign, or go external.

Your constraints: You have 3 internal allies who agree with you. The CFO disagrees strongly—the practice is profitable and legal. The CEO is on the fence but doesn't want internal conflict.

PART A: The Board Conversation (6 minutes)

You're speaking to the full board. You know most of them won't agree. Your goal is not to convince them (they won't be convinced). Your goal is to create a record of your position and to appeal to their values.

Speak & Record Make your case to the board. You're acknowledging their decision. But you're also saying clearly that you think it's wrong—not just risky, but morally wrong. You're asking them to reconsider, knowing they probably won't. Speak for 6 minutes.
6:00

Framing scaffold

FACT What the practice is. What harm it causes. Who is harmed.
VALUES How this contradicts who we say we are.
CHOICE You've chosen profit over harm. That's your choice. But own it.
CONSEQUENCE "I can't be part of continuing this with full knowledge."

PART B: The Private Conversation With the CEO (4 minutes)

After the board meeting, the CEO pulls you aside. They say: "I get it. I respect your position. But if you resign over this, it's going to create panic. Everyone will think there's a crisis. Is that really what you want to do?"

Speak & Record This is the moment where you make your final decision. Do you stay or do you go? How do you respond to the CEO's concern while being true to yourself? Speak for 4 minutes.
4:00

Framing scaffold

ACKNOWLEDGE Their concern about panic is real and legitimate.
BUT Your integrity matters more than organizational comfort.
OFFER Is there a path forward that honors both? Or have you reached the end?

Ethical Leadership Pressure Tests

Five scenarios that force you to decide what you're willing to do for your principles.

A board member: "This practice is legal. We have lawyers who've reviewed it. If we change it, we're essentially admitting we did something wrong, and that opens us to liability. Are you asking us to take on legal risk for a moral principle?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) They're using legality as a shield. You need to separate legal from ethical, and you need to address the liability concern honestly. Go.
The CFO: "I hear your values. But if we stop this practice, we lose $50m in revenue. That affects everyone's bonus. That affects our ability to invest in new products. Are you asking everyone to sacrifice their compensation for a principle that might not even resonate with customers?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) This is the hardest argument because it's partly true. Principles do cost money. You can't pretend they don't. But you also can't use cost as an excuse to abandon ethics.
A loyal board member: "I respect your integrity. But if you resign over this, you're making it about you. You're saying 'my principles matter more than the team.' Isn't that selfish?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) This is a guilt trip. You need to stand firm without being defensive. You're not abandoning the team—you're refusing to be complicit.
Another senior leader, privately: "I agree with you completely. But I have four kids and a mortgage. If I speak up, my career here is over. I can't afford that. I hope you understand."
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) You do understand. Economic vulnerability is real. You can't judge them for protecting their family. But you also can't accept their silence as a reason for your own.
A journalist calls. They've heard rumors about the practice. They want to know if it's real and if you'll go on record. If you don't, they'll go with the story anyway, without your perspective.
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) This is where principle meets public action. Going public is a bridge you can't uncross. How do you decide whether to cross it?

Synthesis & Reflection

Discussion 1: The Cost of Integrity

Ethical stands cost something. Your career, your relationships, your financial security. At what point is the cost too high? Is there a point where you say "I can't afford to take this stand"? And if so, what does that say about your values?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) Have you ever chosen security over principle? How do you live with that choice? What would it take for you to choose differently?
2:00

Discussion 2: Whistleblowing and Loyalty

There's a real tension between being loyal to your organization and being loyal to your values. Some people see whistleblowing as betrayal. Others see it as the highest form of loyalty—loyalty to what the organization should be. Which is it? When is it which?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) Have you ever seen someone go public about something their organization did? What was the outcome? Did going public help or hurt the cause?
2:00

Discussion 3: Complicity and Silence

If you stay and say nothing, are you complicit? Some argue that staying and trying to change the system from within is ethical. Others argue that staying is tacit approval. Where's the line?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) When you've compromised your values to stay somewhere, what was the narrative you told yourself? Was it convincing?
