Ethical Leadership Vocabulary

Integrity The quality of being honest, consistent, and principled; doing what you say you'll do and what you believe is right, even when difficult.
Whistleblowing The act of reporting unethical or illegal activity within an organisation, typically through formal channels or to external authorities.
Moral Courage The ability to act according to one's values despite fear of personal or professional consequences.
Utilitarianism An ethical framework that judges actions by their consequences; the right action maximises overall happiness or well-being for the greatest number.
Deontology An ethical framework focused on duties and rules; certain actions are intrinsically right or wrong, regardless of consequences.
Virtue Ethics An ethical framework focused on character and virtues like honesty, courage, and fairness; asks "What would a virtuous person do?"
Conflict of Interest A situation where a person's private interests could compromise their judgment or ability to act objectively in the organisation's interest.
Transparency Open, honest disclosure of relevant information and decision-making processes to stakeholders.
Accountability Responsibility for one's actions; being answerable to others for decisions and their consequences.
Greenwashing Making misleading claims about environmental practices to appear more sustainable or ethical than one's actual conduct warrants.

Three Ethical Frameworks Applied to Business

Different ethical frameworks can yield different conclusions. Mature ethical reasoning draws on all three.

Framework 1: Utilitarianism

Core principle: The right action is the one that maximises overall well-being or happiness for the greatest number.

Applied to business: A utilitarian might support cost-cutting that benefits shareholders and consumers (lower prices) even if it means job losses, because the aggregate benefit is greater. Cost-benefit analysis and ROI calculations are utilitarian frameworks.

Strength: Forces you to consider broad consequences and stakeholder impact.

Weakness: Can justify harm to minorities for majority benefit; difficult to quantify "happiness"; ignores individual rights.

Framework 2: Deontology

Core principle: Certain duties and rules are binding regardless of consequences. Some actions are inherently right or wrong.

Applied to business: A deontologist would refuse to cheat or deceive customers even if it increased profits. They believe honesty, keeping promises, and respecting individual rights are binding duties.

Strength: Protects individual rights; provides clear moral rules; resists "the end justifies the means."

Weakness: Can be rigid; rules can conflict with each other; ignores consequences.

Framework 3: Virtue Ethics

Core principle: The focus is on developing good character and virtues (honesty, courage, justice, temperance). Ask: "What would a virtuous person do?"

Applied to business: A virtue ethicist considers: Does this decision reflect integrity? Would I be comfortable if this became public? Am I acting with courage, fairness, and wisdom?

Strength: Develops character; integrates personal and professional morality; more flexible than deontology.

Weakness: Less actionable (character guidance is abstract); depends on cultural definitions of virtue.

Integrating the Frameworks

Sophisticated ethical reasoning draws on all three:

  • Utilitarian analysis: What are the broader consequences for stakeholders?
  • Deontological check: Are we violating any core duties or individual rights?
  • Virtue ethics reflection: What kind of leader/company does this decision reflect?
Key insight: When these three frameworks conflict, that's when ethics becomes real. Utilitarian benefit might clash with individual rights; duty might clash with practical consequences. Mature ethical judgment navigates these tensions.

Real Ethical Dilemmas

At C2 level, ethical reasoning must grapple with genuine dilemmas—situations where values conflict and no choice is purely right.

Dilemma 1: Concealing a Product Defect

The Scenario

Your company manufactures automotive components. An internal test reveals a defect that could cause safety issues in rare conditions (estimated 0.5% of installed units under specific circumstances). Fixing the defect would cost £8 million and delay your flagship product launch by 6 months, jeopardising £50 million in expected revenue and potentially your company's survival. The defect has never been seen in field conditions, and the risk is genuinely small. Your legal team says disclosure might trigger recalls and lawsuits that bankrupt the company.

Ethical analysis:

Utilitarian view: If aggregate harm (potential bankruptcy, job losses) exceeds the harm from the rare defect, non-disclosure might be justified.

Deontological view: You have a duty to disclose known safety risks. Hiding information violates customers' right to informed consent, regardless of probability.

Virtue ethics view: What does integrity and courage demand? What would a virtuous leader do?

Dilemma 2: Paying a Bribe in a Foreign Market

The Scenario

Your company is bidding for a £100 million infrastructure contract in a developing country. A local government official makes clear that a "facilitation payment" (essentially a bribe) of £2 million would secure the deal. Without this payment, a competitor (from a country with weaker anti-corruption enforcement) will win the contract, employ local workers, and generate tax revenue for the country. Your payment is small relative to the contract and is routine in this market. However, it violates your company's code of conduct and potentially anti-corruption laws in your home country.

Ethical analysis:

Utilitarian view: The bribe is small relative to economic benefit; non-payment might result in the contract going to a competitor, yielding no benefit.

Deontological view: Bribery is intrinsically corrupt and illegal. Consequences don't justify violating the rule against corruption.

Virtue ethics view: Integrity means refusing corruption even when it costs you. What reputation do you build—as someone willing to bend rules for profit?

Dilemma 3: Sacrificing Jobs to Save the Company

The Scenario

Economic conditions have deteriorated sharply. To keep the company viable and preserve jobs long-term, you need to cut 20% of your workforce (500 people). The workers affected will struggle to find equivalent employment in your region. However, without these cuts, the company will likely fail within 18 months, resulting in the loss of all 2,500 jobs and potentially devastating the local economy. As CEO, you can secure executive bonuses through the restructuring process, or you can forgo them to soften the impact on workers.

Ethical analysis:

Utilitarian view: Saving 2,500 jobs long-term justifies cutting 500 jobs now. The aggregate benefit is greater.

Deontological view: Do the workers who lose jobs have a right not to be sacrificed? Can the company ethically prioritise shareholder survival over worker welfare?

Virtue ethics view: A virtuous leader shares the burden. Forgoing executive bonuses demonstrates integrity and fairness, even if the layoffs remain necessary.

Reflection: Notice that in each dilemma, all three frameworks have something to offer, but they point in different directions. True ethical maturity is recognising this complexity and making a defensible choice while acknowledging the costs.

Building Your Ethical Leadership Philosophy

Ethical leadership is not about having all the answers. It's about developing rigorous, defensible principles and the courage to act on them.

Your Task: Develop a Personal Ethical Leadership Framework

You are preparing to step into a senior leadership role. Before you do, clarify your ethical stance on three critical areas:

10:00

Your Framework (10 minutes)

1. Core Ethical Principles

What are your non-negotiable principles? (e.g., honesty, integrity, fairness, respect for people) Which frameworks resonate most with you—utilitarian, deontological, or virtue ethics? Why?

2. Where You Will Stand Firm

Identify three areas where you will not compromise, regardless of cost: e.g., "I will never knowingly deceive stakeholders," or "I will prioritise worker welfare over shareholder returns in a true crisis." What gives you the courage to take these stands?

3. Where You Accept Moral Ambiguity

Acknowledge areas where you recognise genuine ethical tension and don't have clear answers. How will you navigate these? What counsel will you seek? How will you ensure your decision-making process is rigorous even when the answer is unclear?

Critical reflection: The most dangerous leaders are those who believe they have all the ethical answers. The most trustworthy leaders are those who hold strong principles but remain humble about complexity, seek counsel, and are willing to acknowledge when they've made mistakes. Which type of leader will you be?
