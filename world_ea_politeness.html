Bowing Everywhere? Politeness in East Asia

Bows, honorific language and the polite refusal that never says “no”. What is true — and what changes with the situation?

The stereotype: “East Asians are endlessly polite — everyone bows and nobody ever says no.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The bow

In Japan the bow, called ojigi, is a greeting, a thank-you, an apology and a goodbye. The depth shows the level of respect: a light nod of about 15 degrees is casual, around 30 degrees is polite, and 45 degrees is very formal. Visitors are not expected to bow perfectly; a nod and a smile are fine, and handshakes are common in business with international visitors. In China a handshake or a nod is more usual, and in Korea people often combine a small bow with a handshake.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does a 45-degree bow show?

2. Do visitors have to bow perfectly in Japan?

1. Very formal respect.

2. No — a nod and a smile are fine.

Discuss:
  • How would you greet a stranger in a country where you do not know the custom?

Part 2 — Language and respect

Japanese and Korean build respect into the grammar. Japanese has levels of polite speech known as keigo, and Korean has speech levels and honorific endings that change depending on who you are speaking to. Choosing the right level shows respect, and mistakes can feel awkward. Many people also avoid a blunt “no”. A Japanese colleague may say “that would be a little difficult”, and everyone understands it as a refusal.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is keigo?

2. What might a Japanese colleague say instead of “no”?

1. A system of polite and respectful speech in Japanese.

2. “That would be a little difficult.”

Discuss:
  • Is a polite “no” kinder than a direct one?

Part 3 — Not the whole picture

The rules depend on the situation. Friends in Seoul, Osaka or Shanghai joke, tease and speak directly. Busy markets, rush-hour stations and online arguments are not always gentle. Politeness has a cost too: people may feel pressure to hide disagreement, and young people in all three countries say the rules can feel heavy. So politeness is real, but it is a skill people switch on and off, not a national personality.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Do people always follow the formal rules?

2. What can pressure to be polite cause?

1. No — it depends on the situation.

2. People may hide their disagreement.

Discuss:
  • In which situations are you more formal and in which more relaxed?

Grammar — Generalising Carefully — Tend to, On the Whole

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
To describe a pattern without saying “all”:
• tend to + verb: “Japanese speakers tend to avoid a very direct “no”.”
• on the whole / in general / by and large: “On the whole, bowing is more common in Japan than in China.”
• often, many, some, most instead of “all” or “everyone”.

Going deeper

The “polite East Asian” idea is partly true because many societies in the region put a high value on harmony and on understanding your place in a relationship — ideas linked with Confucian thinking. But politeness is not the same everywhere. What counts as polite on a quiet Japanese train would look odd in a lively Chinese market, where loud, friendly conversation is normal. Treating all of this as one “Asian politeness” hides the variety.

Worth noticing: Generalising carefully: tend to, on the whole, in general, by and large, often, many, some — and avoid “all”, “always” and “never”.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Watch first, then follow. In a new workplace, notice how people greet each other and how disagreement is handled, then match it. A small bow or nod and a warm tone are welcome anywhere in the region. If you work with Japanese colleagues, learn a polite way to say “I will think about it” or “that may be difficult” instead of a flat “no”.
