A. Guess from the sentence

Read the sentence. What does the bold word mean? Then reveal.

1. The seller walked down the street every morning with grapes and nuts in his hands, and people came out to buy.
a seller (n) — a person who sells things. a fruit-seller
2. The little girl chattered all day. She never stopped talking, not even for a minute.
to chatter (v) — to talk quickly and a lot, about small things.
3. The man had a big bag on his back. The little girl thought there were children inside it!
a bag (n) — a soft container for carrying things.
4. The policeman took him away. He was in prison for many years.
prison (n) — the place where people are kept when they break the law.

B. Which word is it?

Read the meaning. Say the word. The first letter helps you.

5. Small, dry fruits from grapes — sweet and brown. It begins with r.
raisins (n, plural). He gave her nuts and raisins.
6. Something funny you say to make people laugh. It begins with j.
a joke (n). They had the same two jokes every day.
7. The day when two people get married, with music and food and family. It begins with w.
a wedding (n). Mini's wedding was that night.

C. Choose the meaning

Read the sentence and choose a, b or c. Then reveal.

8. She was afraid of the big man and ran to her mother. — afraid means: (a) frightened (b) happy (c) tired
(a) — afraid (adj) — frightened, scared.
9. He hit the man who did not pay him. — to hit means: (a) to help someone (b) to strike someone with your hand (c) to talk to someone
(b) — to hit (v) — to strike with your hand or an object. hit – hit – hit
10. The man took a piece of paper out of his coat. On it was the print of a small hand. — a print is: (a) a photograph (b) a drawing made with a pencil (c) a mark made by pressing something on paper
(c) — a print (n) — a mark made by pressing something (a hand, a foot) onto paper. a handprint

The story, in three parts

This is a famous Indian story, told here in simple English. A father tells it. Read each part, then try the questions from memory.

Part 1 — The man at the window

My daughter Mini is five years old. She chatters all day. She never stops talking, and I like it. Her mother is sometimes tired of it, but I am not.

One morning I was writing my book in my room. Mini was playing near my table. Suddenly she ran to the window and shouted: "A Cabuliwallah! A Cabuliwallah!"

In the street there was a tall man from Cabul, far away in the mountains. He wore loose clothes and a big turban. He had a big bag on his back, and he carried boxes of grapes.

Mini called him. But when the man looked up at her, she was afraid. She ran to her mother. She thought there were children inside his big bag!

The seller came to my door. I bought some fruit. Then he asked: "Where is the little girl, sir?" I brought Mini out. He gave her nuts and raisins, but she did not take them. She held my hand and looked at his bag. That was their first meeting.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was Mini afraid of the Cabuliwallah?

2. What did he give her, and what did she do?

1. She thought there were children inside his big bag.

2. He gave her nuts and raisins, but she did not take them. She held her father's hand.

Discuss:
  • Why do small children think a stranger is dangerous? Were you afraid of any grown-up when you were small?

Part 2 — Two friends and two jokes

A few days later, I came out of the house and saw Mini on a bench near the door. She was laughing and talking, and the big Cabuliwallah was sitting at her feet. He was listening to everything she said. Her pockets were full of nuts and raisins.

Now they were great friends. Every day they had the same two jokes. Mini asked: "Cabuliwallah, what have you got in your bag?" And he answered: "An elephant!" Then they both laughed.

Then he asked: "Little one, when are you going to your father-in-law's house?" Mini did not understand. So she asked him: "Are YOU going there?" For a man from Cabul, 'the father-in-law's house' was a joke. It meant prison. "I will hit that father-in-law!" he said, and Mini laughed and laughed.

Mini's mother did not like the man. She was afraid of everything. "Maybe he will take our child away," she said. I said: "No, that is not possible." And the friendship went on.

Every year in January, the Cabuliwallah — his name was Rahmun — went home to his mountains. Before he left, he came to see Mini every day.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the two jokes?

2. What was Mini's mother afraid of?

1. 'What have you got in your bag?' — 'An elephant!' And 'When are you going to your father-in-law's house?' — which, for Rahmun, meant prison.

2. She was afraid that the man would take Mini away.

Discuss:
  • The mother is afraid; the father is not. Who is right? What would you do?

Part 3 — Eight years later

One morning, a few days before Rahmun went home, I heard noise in the street. Two policemen were taking Rahmun away. A man did not pay him for a shawl, they had a fight, and Rahmun hit him with a knife. The man was hurt, and Rahmun went to prison for many years.

Mini ran out and shouted: "Cabuliwallah! Are you going to your father-in-law's house?" Rahmun laughed. "Yes, little one. That is where I am going!"

Years passed. We forgot Rahmun. Mini grew up. She did not come to my room any more. Eight years later, it was the day of her wedding.

That morning, a man came into my room. It was Rahmun. He had no bag, no long hair, and he looked old. "I came out of prison last night," he said. "May I see the little one?" He had some nuts and grapes in a piece of paper for her. He thought she was still a small girl.

I said: "There is a wedding today. You cannot see her." He went to the door. Then he came back. He took a small, dirty piece of paper from his coat and opened it on my table. It was the print of a little hand. His own daughter's hand, in the mountains far away. He carried it every year, close to his heart.

I looked at the print. He was a father, too — just like me. I sent for Mini. She came in her red wedding dress. Rahmun looked at her and could not speak. At last he said: "Little one, are you going to your father-in-law's house?" Mini understood the words now. She went red and looked down.

Then I gave Rahmun money and said: "Go home to your own daughter, Rahmun." Because of that money, the wedding had no electric lights and no band. But for me, the wedding was brighter — because far away, a father was meeting his daughter again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Rahmun go to prison?

2. What was on the piece of paper, and what did the father do after he saw it?

1. A man did not pay him for a shawl, they fought, and Rahmun hit him with a knife.

2. The print of Rahmun's own daughter's hand. The father sent for Mini, and then gave Rahmun money to go home to his daughter.

Discuss:
  • The father gave the money, so the wedding had no lights and no band. Was that a good decision?

The grammar this story runs on: the past simple and time words

A story that covers eight years needs time words to move it along: one morning, every day, a few days later, years later. The verbs stay in the past simple.

Past simple: one finished action or habit in the past.
• regular: walk → walked, laugh → laughed, stop → stopped
• irregular: go → went, come → came, give → gave, see → saw, take → took, run → ran, say → said, think → thought
Time words tell the reader WHEN.
• A single moment: one morning, one day, that night, suddenly
• A habit: every day, every year, always, often
• Moving forward: a few days later, years later, after that, at last
Negative and question: She didn't take the nuts. / Did he go home every year? — Yes, he did.

Why people still love this story

Rabindranath Tagore wrote this story in 1892 in Calcutta, India. Men from Cabul (Kabul, in Afghanistan) came to the city every winter to sell dried fruit. In 1913 Tagore won the Nobel Prize for Literature — the first writer from Asia to win it.

The story looks like a story about a little girl and a stranger. But at the end we understand: it is a story about two fathers. One is rich, one is poor. One is Indian, one is Afghan. But both of them love a daughter who is far away — Mini is going to her husband's house; Rahmun's daughter is in the mountains.

Look at the handprint. Rahmun does not have a photo of his daughter. He has the print of her hand. Why is that more powerful than a photo?

Go deeper — talk it through

Discuss:
  • The father says: 'He was a father, too — just like me.' Why did he not see this before?
  • Mini forgot her old friend. Is that normal for a child? Is it sad?
  • The mother was afraid of Rahmun — and Rahmun really did hurt a man with a knife. Was she right?
  • What do you think happened when Rahmun got home to his daughter?
