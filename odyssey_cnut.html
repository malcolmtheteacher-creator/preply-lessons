The words for today's story

1. Cnut ruled a North Sea empire of England, Denmark and Norway.
an empire (n) — a group of countries ruled by one person.
2. He began his reign by choosing to purge dangerous English nobles.
to purge (v) — to remove enemies, often violently.
3. Cnut chose to marry Emma of Normandy, Æthelred's widow.
to marry (v) — to become someone's husband or wife.
4. In 1027 he made a pilgrimage to Rome.
a pilgrimage (n) — a journey to a holy place.

humble (adj) — not proud; aware of your limits.

8. He wanted to teach his flatterers a lesson.

9. He was a generous patron of the Church.
generous (adj) — giving freely.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The conqueror

Cnut, son of Sweyn Forkbeard, became king of all England after Edmund Ironside died in 1016. He began harshly: some leading English nobles were killed, and the country paid a huge tribute of over 80,000 pounds to pay off his fleet.

But Cnut wanted to be accepted, not just obeyed. In 1017 he married Emma of Normandy, Æthelred's widow, linking himself to the old dynasty. He ruled by old English laws and divided the kingdom into great earldoms, one of which went to an Englishman, Godwin.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Cnut's reign begin?

2. Why did he marry Emma?

1. Harshly: nobles killed and a huge tribute paid.

2. To link himself to the old English royal family and win acceptance.

Discuss:
  • Why would a conqueror marry the widow of his enemy?

Part 2 — Emperor of the North Sea

In 1018 Cnut became king of Denmark, and in 1028 he took Norway too. In 1027 he travelled to Rome for the coronation of the Holy Roman Emperor and wrote a letter home, saying that he had gone to pray for his people and promising to govern justly.

England was largely peaceful under him for nearly twenty years.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which countries did Cnut rule?

2. What did his letter from Rome promise?

1. England, Denmark and Norway (and parts of Sweden).

2. That he would govern justly and had prayed for his people.

Discuss:
  • Was it good for England to be part of a larger empire?

Part 3 — The king and the waves

The most famous story of Cnut was first written down about a hundred years later by Henry of Huntingdon. He wrote that Cnut placed his chair on the shore and ordered the tide not to wet his feet. When the sea rose, the king jumped back and said that the power of kings was empty compared with God's.

People today often use "Canute" to describe someone foolishly trying to stop the unstoppable — but the original story shows the opposite: a king teaching his flatterers humility. Cnut died in 1035 and was buried at Winchester.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who first recorded the story, and when?

2. What did Cnut mean by the experiment?

1. Henry of Huntingdon, about a hundred years after Cnut's death.

2. That kings have no real power compared with God — a lesson in humility for his flatterers.

Discuss:
  • Why do people often misremember this story?

Grammar — Reported Speech

Much of what we know about the past comes from chroniclers reporting what people said. Reported speech moves the tense back and changes the pronouns.

How it works:
• "I will return," he said → He said that he would return.
• "We have lost," she said → She said that they had lost.
• Reporting verbs: claimed, promised, warned, insisted, denied.

1. Report the words

1. Cnut said, "The power of kings is empty." → Cnut said that the power of kings empty.

2. He wrote, "I have prayed for my people." → He wrote that he for his people.

3. He promised, "I will rule justly." → He promised that he justly.

Going deeper

Cnut was a conqueror who chose to rule as an insider: English laws, an English queen, English earls, and generous gifts to English churches. The story of the waves, though probably invented, captures something real — a ruler aware of the limits of power.

Worth noticing: modern commentators use "King Canute" to mean someone trying to hold back change — exactly the opposite of the legend's moral.
Learning from the past — for today: Know your limits — and say so. Leaders who admit what they cannot control win more trust than those who pretend. And remember: a story often means the opposite of how people now use it. Check the original.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Was Cnut a foreign occupier or an English king?
  • Why do flatterers surround powerful people?
  • Which modern "tide" do leaders pretend they can stop?
