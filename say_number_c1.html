The Diagram That Spoke for the Soldiers

In the 1850s a nurse turned thousands of deaths into one picture. The lesson for speakers is what she did with the numbers before she drew it.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The hospital at Scutari

In 1854, Britain was at war with Russia in the Crimea. Thousands of British soldiers were sick or wounded, and the army sent them to a huge hospital at Scutari, across the water from Constantinople. Florence Nightingale, a nurse from a wealthy English family, arrived there with a group of other nurses.

The hospital was overcrowded and dirty. Many of the men who arrived with wounds or fevers died there of diseases they caught inside the building. By the figures she used, as many as four in ten of the men in that hospital were dying in the early months of 1855.

In March 1855, a government sanitary commission arrived to inspect the buildings. It had sewers cleared and ventilation improved. Soon afterwards, the death rate at the hospital fell to a small fraction of what it had been.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where was Nightingale working, and why?

2. Roughly what share of the men in the hospital were dying in early 1855?

3. What did the sanitary commission change?

1. At the hospital in Scutari, treating British soldiers during the Crimean War.

2. As many as four in ten, according to the figures she used.

3. It had the sewers cleared and the ventilation improved, after which the death rate fell sharply.

Discuss:
  • A figure like 'four in ten' is easy to hear and hard to forget. How would the same fact sound if the speaker said 'forty-two point three per cent'?

Part 2 — The comparison

After the war, Nightingale went back to England and worked on a government inquiry into the health of the army. She was a gifted mathematician, and she gathered the army's records and worked through them herself. She then did something that made the figures meaningful: she compared them with the death rates for civilian men of the same age, using tables compiled by the statistician William Farr.

The comparison showed that soldiers in peacetime were dying at a much higher rate than civilians. It also showed that, in the Crimean War, far more soldiers had died from disease than from their wounds. Neither fact was obvious from the raw numbers alone. Each needed a baseline.

This was the move that made her case. A number on its own tells the listener little. A number next to another number tells them how big, how bad and how different.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Nightingale compare the army's death rates with?

2. What did the comparison show about peacetime?

3. What did it show about the Crimean War?

1. The death rates for civilian men of the same age, using tables compiled by William Farr.

2. That soldiers in peacetime were dying at a much higher rate than civilians.

3. That far more soldiers died of disease than of their wounds.

Discuss:
  • She chose civilian men as her baseline. What would have happened to her argument if she had chosen a different group? How do you check that a comparison is fair?

Part 3 — The rose

In 1858 Nightingale published a long report that included a coloured diagram, now called her rose diagram. Each wedge showed a month of the war, and the size of the wedge showed how many soldiers died. Blue showed deaths from disease, red showed deaths from wounds, and black showed other causes.

The diagram was designed for readers who would never read tables of figures: politicians and officials who had the power to change things. At a glance, the blue wedges were far bigger than the red.

The lesson for a speaker is that she did not simply show the figure. She rounded it into an idea, compared it with something, and told the reader what it meant. A listener has no diagram. The rounded number, the comparison and the meaning have to be said aloud, in that order.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the size of each wedge show?

2. What did the colours blue and red stand for?

3. Who was the diagram designed for, and why?

1. How many soldiers died in each month of the war.

2. Blue showed deaths from disease; red showed deaths from wounds.

3. Politicians and officials who would not read tables of figures, but who could see the pattern at a glance.

Discuss:
  • A speaker has no diagram, only words. How could you make a listener 'see' a figure in the way the blue wedges made readers see it?

How the tool works

Nightingale made figures visible with colour. A speaker has to make them audible. Statisticians call it being numerate; communicators call it making a number mean something. The tool below has three moves, in order, and one rule: the exact figure waits until somebody asks for it.

Using it, step by step

  1. Round it. Turn the exact figure into a number the ear can hold: nearly a third, just over four in ten, roughly double. Exact figures sound precise but are forgotten at once.
  2. Compare it. Give the listener a baseline: compared with last year…, twice the national average, the highest in ten years. A number with no comparison cannot be judged.
  3. Mean it. Say what it tells us: which means…, in other words…. The meaning is why the number matters.
  4. Keep the exact figure ready. If someone asks, give it at once: Precisely, it was 26.3 per cent. You get credit for being exact because you did not lead with it.

Try it yourself

These sentences are all ways of saying a figure. Which step of the tool does each one belong to? Choose, then check.

1. Revenue was £3,412,806.
2. Revenue was about three and a half million.
3. That is nearly double what we made two years ago.
4. In other words, we have finally covered our costs.
5. Just over a quarter of staff said they would leave.
6. Staff turnover was 26.3 per cent.
7. That is the highest figure in our sector.
8. Which means we are losing about one person a week.
9. Roughly one in three trains arrived late.
10. Late trains rose from 18.4 per cent to 33.1 per cent.

1. Exact — every digit given; accurate, but it is hard to hold in the ear.

2. Rounded — a number the listener can remember, with about signalling that it is approximate.

3. Compared — it gives the figure a baseline, so the listener can judge its size.

4. Meaning — in other words says what the figures amount to.

5. Rounded — a fraction instead of a percentage, with just over to show it is approximate.

6. Exact — a figure with a decimal; keep it for when someone asks.

7. Compared — it compares one figure with all the others in the group.

8. Meaning — it turns the figure into something concrete that the listener can picture.

9. Rounded — one in three is much easier to hold in the ear than a percentage with decimals.

10. Exact — two exact figures; accurate, but nearly impossible to remember after one hearing.

Where the tool breaks

When precision matters. In medicine, law and accounting, the exact number is the message. Drug doses, deadlines and legal limits must be stated in full.

When rounding changes the story. Rounding 29 per cent to nearly a third is fair; rounding 41 per cent to about half is not. Round only as far as the truth allows.

When the comparison is chosen to flatter you. Comparing this month with the worst month of last year tells the listener nothing. Pick a baseline the listener would accept as fair.

Talk it through:
  • Which is harder for you: rounding a number, finding a fair comparison, or saying what it means? Why?
  • Have you ever been persuaded by a number that turned out to be misleading? What had the speaker done?

Language — Saying numbers naturally

Spoken numbers use a different grammar from written ones. The patterns below let you round, compare and describe change in language that sounds natural.

Approximators: about, roughly, around, in the region of for either side; nearly, almost, just under for slightly below; just over, a little more than for slightly above. Nearly a third is a little less than 33 per cent; just over a third is a little more.
Fractions and ratios: a quarter, a third, two thirds, three quarters; one in four, nine out of ten. Multiples: twice as many as, three times higher than, has doubled / tripled / halved.
Change: by gives the difference (rose by 12 per cent), to gives the new level (rose to 56 million), from…to gives both (rose from 50 to 56 million), and of follows the noun (an increase of nine per cent).

1. By, to, from, of

Use each word from the box once: by · to · from · of.

1. Profits rose twelve per cent, from 50 to 56 million.

2. Unemployment has fallen 6.1 per cent, its lowest level in a decade.

3. Costs have gone up 40 to 52 thousand a month.

4. There has been an increase nine per cent in sick leave.

1. by — gives the size of the change.

2. to — gives the new level.

3. from — gives the starting level; to 52 thousand completes it.

4. of — follows the noun increase.

2. Say the change

Choose the word or phrase that matches the arithmetic.

1. Applications rose from 200 to 600, so they have (tripled / doubled).

2. Out of 40 staff, 10 left, so (a quarter / a third) of them left.

3. A price went from £50 to £100, so it is (half / twice) as expensive as before.

4. The cost fell from £90 to £60, a fall of (a third / a half).

5. Of 500 customers, 495 said yes, so (nearly all / about half) of them did.

1. tripled — 600 is three times 200.

2. a quarter — 10 out of 40.

3. twice — £100 is double £50.

4. a third — £30 off £90.

5. nearly all — 495 out of 500 is 99 per cent.

How it sounds

Six examples of the shape. Try the question first, then open the answer.

Staff turnover was 26.3 per cent this year and 14.1 per cent last year.

Rounded: More than one person in four left this year.

Compared: That is almost double last year's figure, when it was about one in seven.

Meaning: In other words, we are replacing a quarter of the department every year.

Out of 1,000 patients, 83 had a complication.

Rounded: Roughly one patient in twelve had a complication.

Compared: That is higher than the one in twenty that we expect.

Meaning: Which means we should look at what changed in the way we do the procedure.

A coffee used to cost £1.80 and now costs £3.40.

Rounded: A coffee now costs nearly double what it used to.

Compared: Prices in general have risen far less, by a fifth or so over the same years.

Meaning: So coffee has become a luxury in a way that bread and milk have not.

The average rent is £1,486 a month and the average salary is £3,720 after tax.

Rounded: The average rent takes about forty per cent of the average salary.

Compared: A rule of thumb says rent should be around a third, so this is well above the guide.

Meaning: In other words, there is not much left over for saving.

The treatment reduces the risk from 2.1 per cent to 1.4 per cent.

Rounded: It lowers the risk from about two in a hundred to about one and a half in a hundred.

Compared: That is a fall of roughly a third, but only about seven in a thousand patients.

Meaning: So it matters most for people who are already at high risk.

My commute takes 1 hour 17 minutes each way.

Rounded: I spend about two and a half hours a day travelling.

Compared: That is nearly thirteen hours a week, which is more than two full days a month.

Meaning: In other words, it is like having a second part-time job that I do not get paid for.

Make it harder

  • Shorter: say the figure with a comparison in a single sentence.
  • Interrupted: your partner says Can you be more exact? after you round. Give the exact figure at once, without apologising.
  • Opposite comparison: choose a different baseline that makes the same figure look smaller. Which baseline is fairer, and why?
  • For a different listener: say the figure to an expert, to a customer, and to a child. The number stays the same; the comparison changes.
  • Without numbers: describe the same fact using only words such as most, few, about half, almost none. What do you lose?
