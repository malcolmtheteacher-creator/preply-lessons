You Are a Walking Galaxy

Bill Bryson's The Body — and the deeply strange truth about the 38 trillion passengers you have been carrying without knowing it.

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from context

1. Your gut harbours something in the region of a thousand different bacterial species — a population that outnumbers the cells of a small city.
to harbour (v) — to give a home to; to shelter something inside. Often used for things that are hidden or not quite welcome: to harbour bacteria · to harbour doubts · to harbour a fugitive. The noun is a harbour — a port where ships shelter.
2. Bacteria, viruses, fungi and archaea — collectively, the microbes that live with and inside us — have been evolving alongside animals for hundreds of millions of years.
a microbe (n) — any microscopic living thing; a micro-organism. The adjective is microbial: microbial diversity · a microbial ecosystem. Related: the microbiome — the full community of microbes in a particular environment (your gut, your skin, the ocean).
3. Microbial cells outnumber human cells in the body by a rough ratio of one to one — which means you are, depending on how you count, only about half human.
to outnumber (v) — to be greater in number than something else. Women outnumber men in the profession. Errors outnumber corrections. Stars outnumber grains of sand. The prefix out- often means "to do more than": outrun, outperform, outlast.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: to metabolise · a neuron · to thrive. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means to process food or a substance to release energy?
to metabolise (v) — to break down a substance through chemical processes. Noun: metabolism. The liver metabolises alcohol · a fast/slow metabolism · metabolic rate.
2. Which word means a nerve cell that carries electrical signals in the brain or nervous system?
a neuron (n) — the basic unit of the nervous system; also spelled neurone (British). The brain contains roughly 86 billion neurons. Adjective: neural — a neural pathway, neural network. Related: neuroscience, neurology.
3. Which word means to grow or develop well and vigorously?
to thrive (v) — to do very well; to flourish. Bacteria thrive in warm, damp conditions. Children thrive on routine. The city thrived after the railway arrived. Opposite: to struggle, to wither.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The balance of species in the gut is precarious — disturb it with antibiotics, and some species may never return. Precarious means…
  (a) complicated   (b) dangerous and unstable; easily upset   (c) well-established
(b) — precarious (adj) — not safe or secure; likely to change for the worse. A precarious balance · a precarious living · precarious employment. Noun: precariousness.
2. The immune system can mobilise millions of white blood cells within hours of detecting an invasion. Mobilise means…
  (a) to slow down   (b) to make available and move into action   (c) to create for the first time
(b) — to mobilise (v) — to organise and move into action, especially quickly. Mobilise troops · mobilise resources · mobilise public opinion. Noun: mobilisation.

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know diverse. Build the science family: a gut with many species has high ___ (noun) · scientists are working to ___ their research samples (verb) · the ___ of species in the rainforest took millions of years to evolve.
diversity · diversify · the diversity of species. All from the same Latin root. The adjective diverse and the noun diversity are common in science writing: a diverse microbiome · microbial diversity · gut diversity.
2. Immune collocates with many words. Complete: the body's ___ system · an ___ response · a ___ cell · a person who is ___ to chickenpox. And what is the medical field called?
immune system · immune response · immune cell · immune to. The medical field: immunology; a specialist is an immunologist. The verb: the vaccine immunises you against the disease.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — You Are Not Alone

Here is a fact that most people find slightly disturbing: you are not, in the strictest sense, one creature. You are, depending on who is doing the counting and in what mood, somewhere between 37 and 72 trillion human cells — a figure so large it is essentially meaningless, like being told the distance to a star. You are also, as a secondary and largely unremarked matter, home to approximately 38 trillion microbial cells: bacteria, viruses, fungi and things that have no decent common name. That is very slightly more than your human cells. You are, it turns out, not quite a human being — you are a kind of collaborative project, and you are running just barely ahead on points.

The good news is that the bacteria are almost entirely committed to your survival. They have been with the human body for so long — evolving alongside us for hundreds of millions of years — that many of them have become indispensable. Some produce vitamins your own cells cannot manufacture. Others train your immune system to tell friend from foe. Several dozen species in the gut process substances your body cannot digest at all. Remove them all, and you would not survive for long. The Body's central argument, stated quietly on its first page, is simply that you are not what you think you are. You are a city.

What is striking is how recently we learned any of this. The scientific study of the microbiome — the technical term for the full community of microbes in a given environment — only became serious in the 1990s, and the pace of discovery since then has been extraordinary. The number of bacterial species known to inhabit the human gut has gone from a handful to roughly one thousand in thirty years. We are, as Bryson puts it, in the middle of a revolution, and most of us are sitting through it without noticing.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. In what sense are you "not quite human", according to this passage?

2. What do the gut bacteria actually do? Name three things.

1. Microbial cells slightly outnumber human cells in the body — meaning you are, by numbers, roughly half human and half microbial. You are a collaboration rather than a single organism.

2. Any three of: producing vitamins the body cannot make; training the immune system to distinguish friend from foe; processing substances the body cannot digest; (and implicitly) keeping you alive — their removal would be fatal.

Discuss:
  • "You are a city." What does this metaphor make you think differently about — health, illness, medicine, identity?

Part 2 — The Invisible City

The most populous address in this city is the colon — what most of us call the large intestine and doctors call various things depending on which part they mean. Here, in a space about the size of a large bottle, something in the region of a thousand bacterial species are packed together so densely that a teaspoon of colon contents contains more living organisms than there are stars in the Milky Way. They are, mostly, thriving. And they have opinions about the neighbourhood.

What gut bacteria are found to be doing grows stranger by the year. The old picture — bacteria process fibre, the body absorbs the result — has been replaced by something far more complicated. Gut bacteria produce chemicals that influence mood. They communicate with the brain along a pathway that did not, until recently, appear in any textbook — the gut-brain axis — via the vagus nerve, a wandering cable that connects the digestive system to the brainstem. Studies have found associations between particular gut microbiomes and anxiety, depression, and certain neurological conditions. This does not mean the bacteria are running your life. It means the conversation between your gut and your brain is longer, older and stranger than anyone had supposed.

What harms the microbiome is, unfortunately, a list of modern conveniences. Antibiotics — which are genuinely life-saving — cannot distinguish between the bacteria they are sent to kill and the bacteria doing useful work elsewhere. A course of antibiotics will clear an infection and clear rather a lot of bystanders. Some species will return; some will not. A highly processed diet feeds a narrow range of bacteria, crowding out the rest. Excessive cleanliness in early childhood — specifically, the absence of contact with soil, animals and the minor infections of outdoor play — means the immune system develops with fewer species to work with, and may respond to its reduced diet with allergies: attacking things it ought to ignore. The microbiome is, like a rainforest, far easier to damage than to restore.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the gut-brain axis, and why is it significant?

2. What three modern habits are described as harmful to the microbiome?

1. A communication pathway between the gut and the brain, running along the vagus nerve, through which gut bacteria appear to influence mood and neurological function. It is significant because it suggests bacteria are not just processing food — they may be affecting how we think and feel.

2. Antibiotics (which kill beneficial species alongside harmful ones); a highly processed diet (which limits microbial diversity); and excessive cleanliness in childhood (which prevents the immune system encountering enough species to develop properly).

Discuss:
  • If your gut bacteria influence your mood, does that change how you think about your emotions — or about personal responsibility for them?

Part 3 — The Daily Miracle

While you have been reading this, your immune system has been having a quietly productive morning. Several cells that had developed the kind of genetic errors that, if left unchecked, eventually become cancer have been identified and destroyed. Bacteria that entered through a minor scratch you did not notice have been surrounded, overwhelmed and cleared. Several thousand cells have been monitored for signs of viral invasion and found to be satisfactory. All of this was managed without your knowledge, without your gratitude, and largely without error. The immune system is the body's most underappreciated employee — it works a twelve-hour day, every day, without a single sick day, and it asks for nothing except that you try not to eat too much sugar.

The system is two systems, really, running simultaneously. The innate immune system is ancient, blunt and fast: when something foreign appears, it triggers inflammation, pours in a wave of white blood cells, and tries to overwhelm the intruder by numbers. The adaptive immune system is slower but extraordinary: it identifies the specific threat, engineers a custom weapon against it, and — here is the part that makes immunologists genuinely excited — it remembers the encounter for decades. The same pathogen, returning years later, is recognised and destroyed before you notice. This is what vaccination mimics: introducing the enemy under safe conditions so the adaptive system can file it away for future reference.

What goes wrong is well-known but worth restating. Allergies are the immune system attacking harmless things — pollen, peanuts, domestic animals — with a ferocity that is, immunologically speaking, entirely disproportionate. Autoimmune diseases are the immune system attacking the body itself. These represent a system of such sensitivity that the slightest miscalibration produces an immediate and visible result. The same precision that destroys a mutant cell before it becomes a tumour turns, occasionally, against the wrong target. As design flaws go, it is the kind you might be expected to pay considerably more than nothing for — which is to say, it comes free, with the body, and given everything else it does, the trade seems fair.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the difference between the innate and the adaptive immune systems?

2. How does vaccination relate to the adaptive immune system?

1. The innate system is fast, ancient and non-specific — it responds to any foreign threat with overwhelming numbers. The adaptive system is slower but specific — it identifies the exact enemy, creates a targeted response, and remembers that enemy for decades.

2. Vaccination introduces a harmless version of a pathogen so the adaptive system can learn to recognise it and store the memory — ready to respond immediately if the real pathogen appears later.

Discuss:
  • "It comes free, with the body, and the trade seems fair." What does this tone — wonder mixed with wry acceptance — do to the reader? Is it effective popular science?

Grammar — the passive voice in popular science

Science writing loves the passive — and Bryson, unusually for a popular writer, uses it deliberately to create tone: it is estimated · a thousand species have been identified · you are built from · errors are destroyed before they can multiply. Mastering it means you can both read and produce scientific English with authority.

The forms:
• Simple present passive: is/are + past participle — "The gut is colonised by bacteria."
• Simple past passive: was/were + past participle — "The microbiome was not studied seriously until the 1990s."
• Present perfect passive: has/have been + past participle — "Over a thousand species have been identified."
• Impersonal passive: It is estimated / believed / thought / said that… — used when no single person is responsible for the claim.

When to choose passive: (a) when the agent (the doer) is unknown, unimportant, or obvious; (b) when the topic is science — the focus is the fact, not the scientist; (c) for formal, objective tone.
Two upgrades:
• Passive + agent: keep by only when the agent is surprising or important — "The microbiome was first described in detail by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in 1676."
• Passive in relative clauses: "The bacteria that are found in the colon process fibre your body cannot digest."

Classic errors to kill: ✗ "It was discovered bacteria are important" → "It was discovered that…" ✗ "The cell was destroy" → "The cell was destroyed" (use the past participle, not the base form).

1. Identify and transform

Rewrite each active sentence as a passive one. The agent is not needed.

1. Scientists estimate that your body contains 38 trillion microbial cells. → It

2. Gut bacteria produce chemicals that can affect mood. → Chemicals that can affect mood by gut bacteria.

3. Researchers have not fully mapped the gut-brain axis yet. → The gut-brain axis yet.

1. It is estimated that the body contains 38 trillion microbial cells.

2. Chemicals that can affect mood are produced by gut bacteria.

3. The gut-brain axis has not been fully mapped yet.

2. Spot the passive — and analyse it

Find the passive verb in each sentence. Say what tense it is and why passive was chosen.

1. "The number of bacterial species known to inhabit the human gut has gone from a handful to roughly one thousand in thirty years — and new species are being identified every month."

2. "The same pathogen, returning years later, is recognised and destroyed before you notice."

3. "The microbiome is, like a rainforest, far easier to damage than to restore."

1. are being identified — present continuous passive. Chosen because the agent (many different research teams worldwide) is diffuse and unimportant; the discovery is the focus.

2. is recognised and destroyed — present simple passive. The immune system is the agent but the focus is what happens to the pathogen; passive keeps the pathogen as the topic.

3. easier to damage… to restore — infinitive passive. The agent (humans, modern life) is left vague deliberately — an accusation without an accuser.

3. Build the science paragraph

Fill each gap using the verb in brackets in the most natural passive form.

The human gut (now / recognise) as an organ in its own right. For centuries, it (consider) little more than a tube for processing food. That picture (overturn) since the 1990s, as the bacteria that (harbour) there (find) to influence immunity, mood, and even cognition. It (estimate) that in the coming decade, more species (describe) than in all previous centuries combined.

is now recognised · was considered · has been overturned · are harboured · have been found · It is estimated · will be described

Read the whole paragraph aloud. Notice how passive keeps the focus on the gut and the science throughout — no single human agent interrupts the view.

Going deeper

A note on Bryson's method: The Body (2019) is not a textbook — it is a guided tour conducted by someone who finds everything astonishing. His deliberate technique is to present a surprising fact, stand back, and let its implications unfold through conversation rather than lecture. The figures in this lesson are accurate as of publication but the microbiome field moves so fast that some details will have been revised; the broader argument — that the microbiome is central to health, and that we have been inadvertently disrupting it — has only grown stronger.

Three things the story is really about.

First, the question of identity. If 38 trillion of your cells are microbial, in what sense are you one person? The legal, medical and philosophical systems we have built all assume a single, bounded human body. The microbiome does not. Some philosophers have started calling the human body a "holobiont" — an organism made of many organisms — and the word has not been universally welcomed, partly because it is uncomfortable and partly because it is accurate.

Second, the shape of medical knowledge. The microbiome's importance was visible in the data for decades before the field took it seriously. Early hints in the 1970s and 1980s — that gut bacteria might affect allergies, that C-section babies had different microbial profiles — were largely ignored. The pattern here is the same as the penicillin story, the Semmelweis story, the John Snow story: a truth that disrupts the current model tends to wait for the model to become embarrassing before it displaces it. The lesson for any field is uncomfortable.

Third, what we are doing to ourselves. The modern Western microbiome — shaped by antibiotics, processed food, hospital births and clean water (which has also saved millions of lives: this is not a simple story) — is measurably less diverse than those of people living in less industrialised conditions. The prevalence of allergies, autoimmune diseases and inflammatory conditions in wealthy countries maps, imperfectly but suggestively, onto this loss of diversity. We may be paying, in the bodies of individuals, for decisions made at the level of societies.
Talk it through:
  • If the microbiome influences mood and cognition, what does that mean for the idea of individual identity — the sense that your thoughts are entirely your own?
  • Doctors routinely prescribe antibiotics; processed food is cheaper and more available than a diverse diet; hospitals cannot stop using antiseptics. What would it actually take to change these things — and who would have to change them?
  • The microbiome field changed medicine's picture of what a human body is. What other fundamental assumptions in science, medicine or society do you think will be overturned in the next thirty years?
  • Series thread: Semmelweis died before his handwashing discovery was accepted; Snow's cholera map was ignored for years; the microbiome was visible in the data for decades. Why does medicine — a field built on evidence — resist evidence so reliably?
