The Signal-Man

Charles Dickens (1866) — a man works alone by a dark tunnel, and he sees something he cannot explain

Original version available: read this lesson at B2/C1 level →

Read the story in three parts

This is a famous ghost story, told here in simple English. Read each part, then stop and talk about the questions. The end is a surprise — read slowly and do not jump ahead.

Part 1 — The man by the tunnel

One evening, a man was walking near a railway. He stood at the top of a deep, dark place in the ground. Down below, he saw a railway worker. The worker had a red flag in his hand.

This worker was a signalman. His job was to keep the trains safe. He worked alone in a deep, dark place next to a big black tunnel. There was a red light near the tunnel. It was a cold, wet, sad place, far from people.

The visitor went down to talk to him. The signalman seemed worried and tired. He looked again and again at the red light by the tunnel, as if something was wrong.

Stop and talk:
  • Where does the signalman work? Is it a nice place?
  • What is the signalman's job?
  • How does the signalman look — happy or worried?

Part 2 — The secret

After a while, the signalman told his secret. Sometimes, he said, he saw a strange figure near the red light by the tunnel.

The figure waved its arm. One arm was across its face, and the other arm moved fast in the air. It seemed to say: "Be careful! Move away!"

And every time the signalman saw the figure, something bad happened on the railway a short time later. One time, there was a big accident. Another time, a young woman died on a train.

Now the figure had come again. But this time, the signalman did not understand what it wanted to tell him. He did not know what was wrong, or where, or when. This made him very worried.

The visitor listened. He did not know what to say. He felt sorry for the lonely man. Then he said good night, and he went home.

Stop and talk:
  • What does the signalman see near the red light?
  • What does the figure do with its arm? What does it want to say?
  • Why is the signalman so worried this time?

Part 3 — The next evening

The next evening, the visitor came back to the railway. He wanted to help the signalman.

But this time he found some people there. They were standing near the tunnel. Something was wrong.

There had been an accident on the line. The signalman was dead. A train had hit him that morning, near the tunnel.

The visitor asked the train driver what happened. The driver was very sad. He said he saw the signalman on the line. He tried to warn him. He waved his arm and called out to him.

"What did you say?" asked the visitor.

The driver put one arm across his eyes, and he waved his other arm. "I called out: 'Be careful! Move away!'"

The visitor went cold. The driver's arm and the driver's words were the same as the strange figure's. The warning had come true.

The end — stop and talk:
  • What did the visitor find at the railway the next evening?
  • What did the train driver do and say to warn the signalman?
  • The driver's words were the same as the figure's words. Why is this a big surprise?

Past simple, and "before / after"

This story tells us about things that happened in the past. We use the past simple for finished actions. He walked. She saw. The train stopped.

To show the order of events, we use before and after:

before = this thing happens first. The figure came before the accident. (Figure first, then accident.)
after = this thing happens later. The accident came after the figure. (Figure first, then accident.)

4. The figure its arm. (wave)

How the writer surprises you

A good story is more than just words. The writer makes us feel something. Here are two simple ideas from this story.

1. The surprise ending (the "twist")

For the whole story, we think the figure is a ghost that warns the signalman. We do not know who the warning is for. Then, at the end, we learn the truth: the warning was about the signalman himself. He was the one who would die. The train driver waved his arm and said the same words as the figure. This big surprise at the end is called a twist.

2. The spooky feeling

Dickens makes us feel afraid before anything happens. The place is deep, dark, cold and wet, next to a black tunnel. The signalman is alone. We feel that something bad is coming. This scary feeling is called atmosphere. The writer does not show us blood — he makes us feel the fear.

Task A · Tell the story in your own words (3 minutes)

Now tell the story again in your own words. Use short, simple sentences. Try to use four or five words from the Words tab, and tell the events in order.

These steps will help you: a man meets a lonely railway worker by a tunnel · the worker sees a strange figure before bad things happen · the figure comes again, but he does not understand it · the visitor comes back the next day · the worker has died, and the driver used the same words as the figure.

Task B · A time someone felt worried (4 minutes)

Talk about a time when someone felt worried that something bad would happen — you, someone you know, or a person from a book or film. Say what happened and how they felt.

Sentence starters
  • "One day, ..."
  • "Before that, I felt ..."
  • "After that, ..."
Task C · What do you think? (3 minutes)

The signalman saw a strange figure that warned him. Do you think it was a ghost, or only in his mind? Say what you think.

Sentence starters
  • "I think it was ... because ..."
  • "In my opinion, ..."
  • "Maybe ..., but ..."

To finish — easy questions
