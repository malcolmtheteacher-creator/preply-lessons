The Vision Premium

WeWork was valued at forty-seven billion dollars for renting desks. Then it wrote the story down, and the story stopped working.

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll take apart the fastest valuation collapse of the venture era — and you'll master the two dialects it was conducted in: the language of hype, and the language that deflates it: at its heart, this is a property company, albeit a well-marketed one.

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. The company signed fifteen-year commitments on office towers and sold the space by the month. When demand dipped, the rent still fell due. The whole model was a mismatch of long liabilities and short revenues.
a mismatch (n) — two things that should correspond but don't; here the classic property trap: long-term liabilities funded by short-term income. Verb: to mismatch; adjective: mismatched.
2. Investors were not pricing the desks; they were pricing the founder's account of a world-changing movement. The gap between the two was the vision premium.
a premium (n) — the extra paid above underlying value: a brand premium, a scarcity premium, to trade at a premium (opposite: at a discount). "Vision premium" names what evaporates when the story fails.
3. Before selling shares to the public, a company must publish a document disclosing its finances, risks and governance in full. WeWork's prospectus was its undoing.
a prospectus (n) — the formal offering document (in the US, the S-1 filing). To file / publish a prospectus. The genre's problem: it is legally required to tell the truth.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: burn rate · due diligence · governance. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the investigation an investor performs before committing money?
due diligence (n) — to conduct / perform due diligence on. The case's running joke is how little of it forty-seven billion dollars required.
2. Which term means the rules and structures controlling how a company is run and who holds power?
governance (n) — board composition, voting rights, related-party rules. Corporate governance, a governance red flag. This case is the modern catalogue of the red flags.
3. Which term means the speed at which a company consumes its cash?
burn rate (n) — usually monthly. Related: runway = how long the cash lasts at the current burn. "They were burning a billion a quarter" needs no further explanation.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Selling the "We" trademark to his own company, leasing his own buildings back to it — each deal was a related-party transaction. This means…
  (a) a deal between the company and someone who controls or is close to it   (b) an international transaction   (c) a deal between two subsidiaries
(a) — a related-party transaction — not automatically illegal, always requiring disclosure, and in quantity the loudest governance alarm there is.
2. The prospectus offered a home-made profit measure that stripped out most real costs — instantly nicknamed and ridiculed. Inventing flattering metrics is called massaging the numbers. It means…
  (a) auditing carefully   (b) presenting figures selectively so they look better than reality   (c) rounding errors
(b) — to massage the numbers — softer than fraud, harder than presentation. The case's exhibit: "community-adjusted EBITDA", profit adjusted until it appeared.

D · Collocation & word building

1. You know hype. Complete the life cycle, three verbs: to ___ up expectations · the bubble ___ · investors were left nursing ___.
To talk / pump up expectations · the bubble burst · investors were left nursing losses. Also: overhyped, to live up to the hype, to believe your own hype — the founder's occupational disease.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ a valuation (support it with evidence) · to ___ a claim · unable to ___ the numbers. Formal, beloved of analysts.
to justify — or one register up: to substantiate. "Nothing in the filing substantiated the valuation." The S-1's fatal property: it invited exactly this verb.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Elevating the world's consciousness

Strip the story to its transactions and it is almost dull: from 2010, WeWork leased office buildings on long contracts, fitted them out beautifully, and sublet the space by the desk and the month. Serviced offices were a decades-old industry with well-understood economics and famously thin margins. What WeWork added was not a new business model but a new description: it was not a landlord, it insisted, but a technology platform, a global network, a community — in the founder's preferred phrasing, a company whose mission was to elevate the world's consciousness.

The founder was the product. Adam Neumann — six foot five, magnetic, an Israeli former naval officer with a gift for making the person in front of him feel present at the founding of something historic — raised money the way the company rented desks: quickly, and at scale. The description commanded a different multiple than the transactions did. Technology platforms were being valued at ten or more times revenue; property companies at a fraction of it. Every fundraising round therefore had one strategic imperative: keep the story on the technology side of the line, whatever the buildings said.

The decisive believer arrived in 2017. Masayoshi Son of SoftBank — deploying the largest technology fund ever assembled, and famous for urging founders to be crazier — invested billions, then billions more, reportedly agreeing the outline of the first investment in a car ride after a tour cut short for lack of time. By January 2019 SoftBank's final round valued the company at forty-seven billion dollars — more than any American startup had ever been worth on paper. WeWork by then was the largest private office tenant in Manhattan and several other cities, opening locations at a run rate of two per day, and losing roughly a dollar for every dollar it earned.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the business underneath the story, and why did the description matter so much?

2. What did the January 2019 valuation rest on?

1. Long leases sublet short — a classic serviced-office model with thin margins. Described as property it earned a property multiple; described as a tech platform it earned ten times more. The valuation lived in the description.

2. SoftBank's conviction — above all Masayoshi Son's personal belief in Neumann — rather than the unit economics: the company was losing roughly a dollar per dollar of revenue while opening two locations a day.

Discuss:
  • "The valuation lived in the description." What in your own industry is currently priced on its description — and who benefits from keeping the description vague?

Part 2 — The document that told the truth

In August 2019 the company filed its prospectus for a public listing — and performed the rarest act in the hype cycle: it published its own audit. The S-1 opened like a manifesto ("We dedicate this to the energy of We") and then, as the law requires, disclosed the machinery. Losses of about 1.9 billion dollars on 1.8 billion of revenue the previous year. Lease obligations stretching decades into the future against customers committed for months. A home-made profit metric — "community-adjusted EBITDA" — that excluded most real costs and was ridiculed within hours of publication.

And the governance section read like a case study assembling itself in real time. The founder had taken personal loans from the company; held ownership stakes in buildings that WeWork then leased, putting him on both sides of his own rent negotiations; and had sold the trademark to the word "We" to his own company for 5.9 million dollars — returned after public outcry, but printed forever. His shares carried twenty votes each. In an earlier version of the documents, succession planning had contemplated a role for his wife in choosing an eventual successor. Each item had been known to insiders and tolerated for years; printed together, in the sober typography of a legal filing, they stopped being colour and became evidence.

The six weeks that followed were a landslide in slow motion. Public-market investors, asked to buy at forty-seven billion, indicated interest at numbers that fell by the week — twenty, then perhaps fifteen, then below ten. In September the listing was withdrawn; days later Neumann was persuaded to resign as chief executive, exiting — after negotiation with SoftBank, which needed his votes — with a package reported around 1.7 billion dollars, later restructured amid litigation. SoftBank took control at a valuation of roughly eight billion: an eighty-per-cent markdown in about six weeks, during which the desks, the buildings and the members had scarcely changed at all. What had changed was the paperwork.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the S-1 disclose, financially and in governance terms?

2. What collapsed between August and October 2019 — and what notably did not?

1. Losses of ~$1.9bn on ~$1.8bn revenue; decades of lease obligations against monthly customers; an invented profit metric; and a governance catalogue — loans to the founder, the founder as his company's landlord, the $5.9m "We" trademark sale, 20-vote shares.

2. The valuation — from $47bn to roughly $8bn in about six weeks, with the IPO withdrawn and the founder out. The underlying business — desks, buildings, members — was essentially unchanged; only the story had failed.

Discuss:
  • Every red flag had been known to insiders for years and tolerated. What converts a tolerated fact into a fatal one — the audience, the format, or the moment?

Part 3 — The discipline of the boring truth

The aftermath ran for years. SoftBank's rescue and write-downs made WeWork one of the most expensive misjudgements in the history of venture investing — Son himself told his own shareholders, with unusual bluntness, that his judgement in the investment had been poor. The company, under new management, cut costs, renegotiated leases and finally listed in 2021 through a merger vehicle at around nine billion dollars. Then the pandemic's long tail did to the office market what no prospectus could: in November 2023 WeWork filed for bankruptcy protection in the United States, its equity effectively worthless. It emerged in 2024, smaller, as a conventional flexible-office operator — which is to say, as the thing it had always been underneath the story. Neumann, remarkably, raised fresh venture money for a new property venture and even mounted an unsuccessful bid to buy WeWork back.

What gives the case its edge is that the sceptics did not win on information. The numbers were never secret; the leases were public; property analysts had said "this is a landlord with a app" for years, correctly, and been ignored through round after round. The hype did not survive an unmasking — there was nothing masked. It survived until the audience changed: private investors buy the next chapter of a story, while public markets price the current one, and the S-1 was the airlock between those two worlds. The forty-seven billion was real in one room and impossible in the other.

And one uncomfortable footnote resists the morality tale. The believers were not entirely wrong about the world — flexible work, the unbundling of the office, the demand for space by the month: all of it materialised, accelerated by the pandemic. The vision was broadly right; the vehicle was priced as if vision were a business model. Which leaves the question every investor and every executive selling a transformation must answer: how do you back the future without paying the vision premium — and when you catch yourself using words like community, platform and mission, who in the room is being paid to translate them back into rent?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the story end for SoftBank, the company, and Neumann?

2. Why is "the sceptics unmasked the fraud" the wrong summary?

1. SoftBank absorbed enormous write-downs and public self-criticism from Son; WeWork listed in 2021, went bankrupt in 2023 and re-emerged smaller as an ordinary flexible-office firm; Neumann left with a heavily negotiated package and later raised money for a new venture.

2. Because nothing was hidden: the numbers and leases were known throughout, and property analysts had called it correctly for years. The valuation didn't survive on secrecy but on audience — private capital paid for the story's next chapter until the public market, via the S-1, priced the current one.

Discuss:
  • "The vision was right; the vehicle was priced as if vision were a business model." Where do you see that exact error being repeated today?

Grammar — the language of hype, and how to deflate it

This case was fought between two registers: inflation and deflation. A C1 speaker needs both — the first to recognise and, occasionally, deploy; the second to puncture politely, which is a boardroom survival skill.

The inflation toolkit (recognise it, use it knowingly):
• Emphatic fronting & clefts: "What we're building is nothing less than a new way of working."
• Negative inversion: "Never before has a company grown at this pace." · "Not since the railways has infrastructure changed this fast."
• Absolute quantifiers & scale-words: every, all, the world's first, category-defining, a once-in-a-generation opportunity
• Abstract nouns doing concrete work: community, ecosystem, consciousness, mission — the tell is that removing them changes no operational fact.
The deflation toolkit (downtoners & precision):
• Downtoning adverbs: essentially, at bottom, in effect, for practical purposes, when all is said and done — "This is, at bottom, a property company."
• Reclassification: "What is described as a platform is, in effect, a lease portfolio."
• Quantified modesty: replace the scale-word with a number — "'explosive growth' here means forty per cent, from a small base."
• The polite puncture: "Help me understand how this differs, operationally, from…" — a question that deflates without accusing.

The skill is switching: inflation for the vision slide, deflation for the numbers slide — and knowing which one the moment requires.

3. The two-register memo — complete the paragraph

An analyst writes the honest note. Fill each gap so the register lands where marked, then read it aloud.

The pitch deserves its due: (cleft: What the founder has built) is a brand tenants genuinely love, and not since the serviced-office boom of the nineties (inversion: have/has) demand grown this fast. That said, the company is, (downtoner), a lease portfolio: (reclassify: what is described as) a technology platform is a property business with an app attached. The memo concedes in the inflated register and concludes in the deflated one — the exact reverse of the pitch, and the reason analysts who write like this keep their jobs.

Going deeper

A note on the record: figures are rounded; several famous details (the car-ride investment, the exit package) come from journalistic reconstruction and were disputed or renegotiated afterwards. Treat the anecdotes as documented folklore and the S-1 as the primary source it is.

What the case actually tests. First, the audience theory of valuation. Nothing was hidden, so nothing could be revealed; what changed in August 2019 was not information but audience. Private capital, structurally, buys the story's next chapter — it profits by selling to a greater believer. Public markets price the current chapter. Every bubble in this series' background — and arguably every hype cycle since — is a dispute between those two audiences about which chapter is for sale.

Second, the enabling structure. It is comfortable to file this under one founder's charisma, and false. Every red flag was approved by sophisticated parties with full information: board members who waved through related-party deals, bankers who pitched valuations near a hundred billion, a lead investor whose fund design — enormous, concentrated, speed-rewarding — made writing bigger cheques rational. Charisma was the ignition; the fuel was other people's incentives. The governance lesson is therefore not "beware magnetic founders" but "map who in the structure is paid to keep believing."

Third, the vision-vehicle distinction, this series' recurring blade. As with Ørsted's wind thesis, the underlying prediction was broadly correct — flexible work arrived on schedule. Being right about the future and being a good investment are separated by price, structure and timing. The discipline the case teaches is to hold the two sentences apart: the vision is right and this vehicle, at this price, with this governance, is how we back it — and to notice when a pitch is using the first sentence to prevent examination of the second.

Talk it through:
  • Map the believers: for a hyped asset you know today, list who is structurally paid to keep believing — and who is paid to doubt. Which list is longer?
  • The S-1 was the airlock between audiences. What is the equivalent airlock inside your organisation — the document or forum where the story must finally meet the numbers? Does it work?
  • Son's public self-criticism versus the standard non-apology: what did his bluntness cost and buy? Compare with Snabe's "average" in the Maersk case.
  • Construct the steelman: make the best honest case that WeWork at $20bn (not $47bn) was a rational investment in 2019. Where exactly does the case break?

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

Answer properly: refuse the false binary honestly, explain what concentrated conviction investing actually is, own the misjudgement in plain words, and name the specific control you changed. Then compress it to ninety seconds.
