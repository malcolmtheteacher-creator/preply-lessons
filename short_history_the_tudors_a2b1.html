The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A king who wanted a son

Henry VIII became King of England in 1509. He was young, strong and clever. Soon he married a Spanish princess called Catherine of Aragon. For many years, people thought they were happy.

But there was a big problem. Henry wanted a son. A son could be the next king after him. Henry and Catherine had a daughter, called Mary, but their other babies died. Henry believed that England needed a king, not a queen, to stay strong and safe.

The years passed, and Catherine grew older. Henry was not happy. He wanted a young wife who could give him a son. So he made a hard decision: he would end his marriage to Catherine.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Henry VIII want a son so much?

2. What was the problem with his marriage to Catherine?

1. He wanted a son to be the next king after him. He believed England needed a king to stay strong and safe.

2. They had a daughter, Mary, but no son. Their other babies died, and Catherine was getting older.

Talk about it:
  • Henry thought a country needed a king, not a queen. Do you think that idea was right or wrong? Why?

Part 2 — A new love, and a big fight

Henry fell in love with a young woman called Anne Boleyn. Anne was clever and strong. She did not want to be just the king's girlfriend. She wanted to be his wife and the queen.

But there was a problem. To marry Anne, Henry first had to end his marriage to Catherine. In those days, only the Pope, the leader of the Church in Rome, could say "yes" to this. So Henry asked the Pope for help.

The Pope said no. Catherine's family was very powerful, and the Pope was afraid of them. Henry was angry. He would rather leave the Church in Rome than lose Anne. So he made a plan that no English king had ever made before.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Anne Boleyn want?

2. Why did the Pope say no to Henry?

1. She did not want to be just the king's girlfriend. She wanted to be his wife and the queen.

2. Catherine's family was very powerful, and the Pope was afraid of them. So he would not end Henry's marriage.

Talk about it:
  • Henry was ready to change his whole country for one woman. Was that a good reason or a bad reason? Why?

Part 3 — A new church, and a sad end

Henry did something huge. He said that England did not need the Pope. Henry himself would be the head of a new church — the Church of England. Now he could end his marriage to Catherine. In 1533, he married Anne Boleyn, and she became queen. To say "no" to the king's new church was now a crime, and people could die for it.

But then Anne had a daughter, not a son. Her name was Elizabeth. Henry was very disappointed. He wanted a son so much, and again he did not have one. Soon Henry stopped loving Anne, and he wanted a new wife.

In 1536, Anne's enemies said she had done terrible crimes. Most people today think these crimes were not true. But Anne was put in the Tower of London, and there she was killed. Henry married four more times. But here is the surprise: Anne's daughter, the little girl Elizabeth, grew up to be Elizabeth I — one of the greatest queens in English history.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Henry end his marriage and marry Anne?

2. What happened to Anne Boleyn — and what is the surprise at the end?

1. He said England did not need the Pope. He made a new church, the Church of England, with himself as the head. Then he ended his marriage and married Anne in 1533.

2. Anne's enemies said she had done crimes (most people today think they were not true). She was killed in the Tower of London. The surprise: her daughter Elizabeth became Elizabeth I, one of England's greatest queens.

Talk about it:
  • Henry wanted a son, but his daughter Elizabeth became a great queen. What does this tell us about life and surprises?

Grammar: had better & would rather

This story is full of strong choices and warnings. English has two easy ways to say them. Both are followed by the base verb (the simple form, with no to and no -ing).

had better + base verb = strong advice or a warning. There is often a hidden "or something bad will happen".
• "Anne had better have a son." (or her position is in danger)
• "Henry had better not wait too long."
We often say 'd better: "You'd better be careful."
would rather + base verb = a preference (the thing you want more). We use than for the other choice.
• "Henry would rather leave the Church than lose Anne."
• "Anne would rather be queen than the king's girlfriend."
We often say 'd rather: "I'd rather stay home."
Careful with the verb! Both use the base verb, not to and not -ing.
• "You'd better leave." (NOT "to leave" / "leaving")
• "I'd rather walk." (NOT "to walk" / "walking")
