August Heat

An artist draws a stranger after a murder trial, walks into the heat—and finds the stranger carving his gravestone.

Adapted for B2/C1 from W. F. Harvey's 1910 story

Memory first

Have a go without notes; uncertainty is useful here.

  1. Complete: “The meeting was supposed to…”

    One possible answer: “The meeting was supposed to start at six.” This describes an expectation or arrangement.

  2. Explain whether meant to describes purpose or destiny in “They were meant to meet.”

    In “They were meant to meet,” meant to suggests destiny. In “This tool is meant to cut glass,” it describes purpose.

  3. Change “People expect the storm to break” into a passive structure.

    “The storm is expected to break.” The expected event becomes the subject of the passive structure.

The story in three movements

After each movement, answer from memory before revealing the response. The turn remains hidden until you commit to a prediction.

1 · A drawing without a model

On 20 August, during the hottest afternoon anyone could remember, the artist James Clarence Withencroft completed his work early. He should have gone out, but the heat held him in his chair.

He took a pencil and began drawing without any plan. A courtroom appeared beneath his hand. In the centre stood an enormous man with a square head and thick neck. The man had just heard a verdict. His face showed neither innocence nor guilt—only a terrible acceptance.

Withencroft worked for nearly an hour. When he stopped, the stranger on the paper seemed more real than many people he knew.

“Where did you come from?” he asked the drawing.

The room had become stifling. He left the picture on his desk and walked outside, hoping to find air.

Without deciding where to go, he crossed unfamiliar streets. Each turn felt accidental, yet he never considered turning back. At last he entered a narrow yard filled with gravestones.

The sun emptied the streets. Shop blinds were lowered, dogs slept beneath carts, and even the city noise seemed exhausted. Only the tap of metal on stone continued ahead of him, regular as a clock marking his progress.

A large man was cutting letters into a slab of stone. He looked up.

Withencroft stopped at the gate. It was the man from the drawing.

The stonemason stared back with equal shock. Slowly, he put down his hammer.

“I think,” he said, “you had better come inside.”

Answer from memory

What makes the drawing feel like a premonition rather than an ordinary act of imagination?

Its detail, emotional certainty and unfamiliar face seem to arrive without conscious choice.

2 · The stone yard

The man's name was Charles Atkinson. He led Withencroft across the yard to the stone he had been working on.

The inscription read:

Sacred to the memory of James Clarence Withencroft, born 18 January 1860, died 20 August 190—.

Withencroft read his own name twice. The final date was that day's date.

“Who ordered this?”

“No one.” Atkinson wiped his forehead. “I saw the name in a dream. This morning I felt I had to carve it.”

Withencroft showed him the courtroom drawing. Atkinson's great hand began to shake. The likeness was exact—not only the face, but the expression of a man awaiting judgement.

They searched for sensible explanations. Perhaps they had met and forgotten. Perhaps one had seen the other's name. Perhaps the heat had created a shared confusion. Each explanation failed against some small detail.

Atkinson fetched water, but neither drank much. Each man was afraid that an ordinary movement might begin the scene shown in the drawing. They had become polite prisoners of objects they themselves had made.

Thunder sounded beyond the rooftops. A storm was expected before midnight.

Atkinson asked the artist to remain. Withencroft would rather have left, but the thought of walking alone through the hot streets was worse. They carried two chairs into the workshop and placed the drawing and gravestone where both could see them.

Answer from memory

List the possible explanations from most rational to most supernatural.

Coincidence or forgotten recognition; mutual trick; psychological suggestion; shared premonition; an event that has somehow sent information backwards.

3 · Waiting for midnight

Evening brought no coolness. The two men sat opposite each other while the sky darkened. Between them lay the stone-carving tools: chisels, a wooden mallet and a heavy hammer.

Atkinson tried to talk about his family. Withencroft answered, but his attention kept returning to the drawing. Why did it show a trial? Who was accused? The man's expression might belong to a murderer—or to someone horrified by what he was about to do.

At nine o'clock the first rain struck the roof. At ten, lightning filled the workshop. Neither man suggested moving the hammer.

Once Atkinson reached towards it to move a cup. Withencroft stood so quickly that his chair fell backwards. The stonemason withdrew his hand. They apologised to each other, and the politeness made the moment more frightening.

Withencroft began writing an account of the afternoon. If nothing happened, the whole affair would look foolish in daylight. If the stone proved correct, someone should know how the day had begun.

He recorded the drawing, the walk, Atkinson's face and the inscription. Then he added the final facts.

It was nearly midnight. The heat remained intense. Atkinson was watching him from the other side of the table. The hammer lay within the stonemason's reach.

The written account ends there.

It does not say whether the date on the grave became true, whether the drawing showed the future, or whether two frightened men created the event they feared. The reader is left in the workshop with them, listening to the storm and waiting for midnight.

Answer from memory

Why is the absence of an ending more effective than a confirmed death?

The reader must remain inside the period of expectation. Every explanation and outcome stays possible.

Grammar — supposed to / meant to / expected to

be supposed to

The day was supposed to be ordinary.

Expectation, arrangement or rule.

be meant to

The stone was meant to mark a grave.

Purpose; sometimes interpreted destiny.

be expected to

The storm was expected to break that night.

Prediction based on evidence or social expectation.

Mixed practice

Complete all six before revealing. Explain the choice, not only the missing words.

The artist ___ finish routine work that afternoon. (supposed)

The courtroom sketch was ___ capture an imagined face, not predict the future. (meant)

The temperature ___ fall after the storm. (expected)

They were ___ strangers, yet each had created the other. (supposed)

Was the drawing ___ warn him or cause the event? (meant)

The evening did not develop as anyone ___. (expected)

  1. was supposed to — Was supposed to describes the expected plan for that afternoon.
  2. meant to — Was meant to expresses the intended purpose of the sketch.
  3. was expected to — Was expected to reports a prediction based on the coming storm.
  4. supposed to be — Supposed to be contrasts the expected situation with the uncanny reality.
  5. meant to — Meant to asks about purpose or possible destiny.
  6. expected — After did not, the main verb stays in its base form: expect.

Going deeper

Documentary voice

A precise date, weather and first-person account make the impossible feel recorded rather than invented.

Reciprocal evidence

Each man possesses an object that independently identifies the other.

Loaded objects

A drawing, gravestone and hammer become evidence, prophecy and possible weapon at once.

Radical omission

The story ends before the predicted time, transferring authorship of the outcome to the reader.

A short analysis

Give your answer first. Then reveal a model and identify one idea you handled differently.

The stone may function less as prophecy than as pressure. Once both men see it, ordinary actions become threatening and the hammer becomes a possible weapon. Harvey withholds the outcome so that expectation itself—not confirmed fate—produces the fear.
