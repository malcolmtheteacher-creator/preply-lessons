Two Things: Both, Either, Neither

For exactly two people, places or options.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "Both my flatmates cook really well."
  • "Either bus gets you into town."
  • "Neither of us wants to do the dishes tonight."

Before you check: which one uses a plural verb, and which two use a singular verb?

Check the pattern CLICK
Both + plural noun + plural verb ("Both...cook"). Either / neither + singular noun + singular verb ("Either bus gets...", "Neither of us wants...") — even though we're still talking about two things.
both + plural noun (+ plural verb)
either / neither + singular noun (+ singular verb)
both of / either of / neither of + the / us / them

With pronouns it works the same way: "Both of us love spicy food." "Neither of them has a car."

Joining two things: both...and

"My flatmate cooks well. I cook well too."
→
"Both my flatmate and I cook well."

With "both...and", the two things joined take a plural verb. The same pattern works with "either...or" for a choice ("You can pay either by card or in cash") and "neither...nor" for ruling both out ("Neither the shop nor the café was open").

One By One: Each, Every

For talking about the members of a group individually — not as one block.

Spot the pattern

  • "Every room in this flat has a window."
  • "Each guest gets a welcome drink at the door."
  • "Each of the students has their own locker."

What do you notice about the verb every time — singular or plural?

Check the pattern CLICK
Always singular: has, gets, has. "Each" and "every" both mean "one at a time", so English treats the group as lots of single things, not one plural group — even "each of the students", with a plural noun straight after it, still takes a singular verb.
each / every + singular noun + singular verb
each of + plural noun / us / them (+ singular verb)

Note: "every" is never followed by "of" — there's no "every of the students". And with pronouns: "Each of us has a favourite café nearby." "Each of them speaks a different language."

Group verb vs. individual verb

"The whole team celebrated together."
→
"Each player got a medal."

"Each" zooms in on the individuals; a whole-group verb like "celebrated together" can't do that.

The Whole Group: All, None, No

For talking about a whole group at once — everyone/everything, or nobody/nothing.

Spot the pattern

  • "All of my friends came to the party."
  • "None of the shops were open on Sunday."
  • "No shops were open on Sunday."

Compare the second and third sentences — they mean almost the same thing. What's different about the words before "shops"?

Check the pattern CLICK
"No" goes straight onto the noun — no article, no "of": no shops. "None" can never do that — it always needs "of the" (or "of my", "of your"...): none of the shops. Never "none shops".
all (of the) + plural noun (+ plural verb)
none of + the / my / your... + noun (+ verb)
no + noun — no article, no "of"

The same "of" pattern works with pronouns: "None of us wanted to leave." "All of them are coming to the wedding."

Your Turn to Talk

Time to use all three groups in one real situation. You do the talking.

The situation

Picture your flat, your family home, or a group of friends planning something together — a trip, a dinner, a house move. Tell the story out loud for one minute. Try to answer:

  • Who does what? Is there something each person is responsible for?
  • Is there anything that's true for everyone — or true for no one?
  • If there are two options (two dates, two places), what do you think — either one, or does it have to be both?

2. Say it out loud: why do we add -s to a present-simple verb after "he", "she", or "it"?

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
Because those are all singular — one person or thing doing the action. It's the exact same rule that makes "each" and "every" take an -s verb: they always mean "one at a time", so they behave like "he/she/it".
