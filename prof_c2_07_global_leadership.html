Setting the Scene

You are the Global Chief Operating Officer. Your organization has offices in 15 countries across four continents. This week, a serious miscommunication between your Asia and EMEA regional heads has caused a significant project failure—a $40m opportunity lost, six months of work derailed, team trust fractured.

In 48 hours, you address the global leadership team via video call. 35 people from Tokyo, Singapore, Beijing, London, Frankfurt, São Paulo, and New York. Different timezones, different cultural contexts, different expectations of how authority should be exercised. Same message. Radically different reception.

Why this matters at C-suite level: Global leadership demands you hold contradictions: clarity and humility, directness and respect for hierarchy, accountability and cultural sensitivity. Miscalibrate the tone by one degree and you either sound like an imperial CEO imposing Western management values, or so accommodating that no one understands what you actually expect.

Global leadership isn't about finding some neutral middle ground. It's about understanding that different cultures have different concepts of authority, directness, disagreement, and shame. Your job is to acknowledge these differences, not erase them. The leader who can say "I know how this lands differently for each of you" shows the cultural intelligence that builds trust across borders.

Language Moves for Global Leadership

These moves honor cultural difference while maintaining clarity and accountability.

1 Cultural Intelligence Language +

This is the move that signals you see the room. You acknowledge that power distance, communication directness, and expressions of disagreement vary by culture. You're not pretending we all speak the same way.

Weak

"We all need to speak up more openly. In some cultures, people are quiet, and that's a problem we need to fix."

Strong

"I know that in some regions, disagreeing with leadership in a public forum is unusual and uncomfortable. In others, it's expected. Neither is wrong. But right now, we need both perspectives. If you disagree with a decision or see a problem, I need to hear it—and I need you to tell me how you'd most comfortably raise it. Some of you will do it here. Some will email me afterward. Both count as speaking up."

2 Power Distance Navigation +

Some cultures expect hierarchy to be clear and respected. Others expect it to be minimized. You navigate this by being explicit about where you're making decisions and where you're inviting input.

What doesn't work

"I'm going to make a decision here. I want your input, but ultimately it's my call." This is either respectfully hierarchical (to some) or offensively autocratic (to others).

Strong

"Here's what happened: miscommunication cost us $40m. As COO, I'm responsible for the structure that allows or prevents this. So here's what I'm doing: I'm restructuring governance and decision rights. I'm not asking your permission—I'm telling you what's changing and why. I am asking for your input on how we execute it. Where that input conflicts with my decision, I'll explain my reasoning and we'll move forward together."

3 The Global Vision That Lands Locally +

The move is to speak the global vision in a way that doesn't erase local context. You're not saying "we're one global company now." You're saying "we have a shared purpose, and it plays out differently in each region."

Weak

"Our mission is to be the global leader in X. That's the same for everyone."

Strong

"Our mission is to be the global leader. But 'leader' means something different in Tokyo than in São Paulo. In Tokyo, it might mean trustworthy partnership with long-term thinking. In São Paulo, it might mean agility and growth. Both are true. Both matter. What we all share is an obsession with our customers in our respective regions. That's the thread that runs through everything we do, regardless of where you sit."

4 Addressing Failure Across Cultures +

Shame and accountability land very differently across cultures. Some cultures prefer to discuss failure indirectly; others demand explicit acknowledgment. You navigate this by being direct about the failure and the path forward, while being culturally sensitive about how you frame individual and collective responsibility.

What gets lost

"Everyone made mistakes. Let's move forward." This avoids accountability in ways that some cultures find evasive and others find merciful.

Strong

"The Asia and EMEA teams failed to coordinate. I'm responsible for that structure. Both regional heads are responsible for escalating the conflict when they saw it. We've all learned hard lessons. The individuals involved are not bad people or bad leaders. But we need a new system, and we need clarity about who decides what. Those changes are non-negotiable. How we talk about it, and how we rebuild trust—that's something I want your input on."

5 Standardisation vs Localisation Framing +

The central tension in global leadership: how much do we standardize to create efficiency, and how much do we localize to honor context? The move is to be honest about this trade-off instead of pretending there's a perfect solution.

What sounds false

"We're going to localize everything while maintaining global standards."

Strong

"Here's what's non-negotiable globally: decision rights, reporting structures, customer data security. Here's what's flexible: how you hire, how you communicate internally, how you structure teams. That flexibility is not chaos—it's constraint with freedom. You have guidelines. You don't have scripts. The tension between those two things is permanent. Our job is to manage it well."

6 Leading the Multicultural Team +

The move is to name the diversity as an asset and as a source of friction. You're not pretending we're all the same. You're saying that these differences are where the strength comes from—and also where the work is hardest.

What sounds patronizing

"I love working with people from different backgrounds. We bring so much diversity of thought!"

Strong

"The fact that we have 15 countries represented means we'll never make decisions quickly. We'll argue, and the arguments will be shaped by different assumptions about what matters. That's expensive. It's also why we'll see problems others miss. The question isn't whether we pay a cost for diversity. We do. The question is whether we're paying it intentionally, in service of better decisions—or accidentally, by pretending the cost doesn't exist."

Live Scenario: The Global Leadership Meeting

The Situation: Asia and EMEA regional leads have misaligned, costing $40m and fractured trust. You're the COO. You have 35 global leaders on a video call spanning Tokyo to New York. You need to: (1) own the structural failure, (2) demand accountability, (3) announce restructuring, (4) restore confidence that this won't happen again.

Complication: In Asia, this kind of direct criticism in a public forum is considered disrespectful to the leaders involved. In Western contexts, it's expected. You need to address both expectations without endorsing either one exclusively.

PART A: Open the Meeting (5 minutes)

You're on video. 35 faces on screen. Some will interpret directness as accountability. Others will interpret it as shame. You need to be clear and culturally intelligent at the same time.

Speak & Record Deliver your opening statement to the global leadership team. Acknowledge the failure, own your part in it, announce next steps, and signal that you understand this lands differently depending on where they are in the world. Speak for 5 minutes.
5:00

Framing scaffold

FACT The miss. The cost. No hedging.
MY ROLE "I'm responsible for the structure that allowed this."
CHANGES New governance, decision rights, escalation pathways.
CULTURE "I know this conversation sounds different depending on where you are."

PART B: The Hard Conversation (4 minutes)

After your opening, the Asia head sends a message: "This conversation is difficult for me. In my culture, this kind of direct criticism should happen privately, not in a public forum." How do you respond—to them, and to the whole group?

Speak & Record Respond to this. Acknowledge their point. But also explain why you chose this forum. And do it in a way that respects their concern while maintaining accountability. Speak for 4 minutes.
4:00

Framing scaffold

HEAR Acknowledge the cultural concern directly. Show you see it.
EXPLAIN Why you chose a public forum (everyone needs to understand the stakes).
OFFER What happens next (private conversation + public accountability).

Global Leadership Pressure Tests

Five scenarios that test your ability to lead across cultures under pressure.

The EMEA head, after the meeting: "You've implied that both regions are equally responsible, but actually Asia didn't escalate the problem when they saw it. I did everything right. Why am I being held accountable equally?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) You need to hold both truths: they may have different levels of responsibility, AND the systemic failure is your responsibility. Go.
A London-based director, in the public meeting: "If we're going to restructure, why not just move everyone to a matrix and make it clear who reports to whom? Why the ambiguity?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) They're asking for hierarchy and clarity. But some of your team experiences too much hierarchy as inflexible. How do you explain that without making either camp feel invalidated?
A Tokyo-based leader, privately: "In my region, people are ashamed. They're updating CVs. How do I convince them to stay?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) Shame is real in some cultures. You can't dismiss it. But you also need to distinguish between "I'm ashamed of my company's failure" and "I'm ashamed of my own performance." How do you help them make that distinction?
A São Paulo leader: "You talk about standardizing decision rights globally. But business in Brazil works differently. If I follow your global rules, I'll lose deals. What do I do?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) This is the real tension: localization vs standardization. You can't solve it. But you can be clear about where flexibility exists and what the trade-offs are.
A Singapore-based director: "With all due respect, the issue is that Western culture is overrepresented in this company. When decisions are made, they reflect Western values. Is that going to change?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) They're right, in some ways. You can't deny the dominance of any culture. But you also can't fix it by simply adding more voices. How do you acknowledge the problem while being honest about its complexity?

Synthesis & Reflection

Discussion 1: Cultural Relativism vs Accountability

There's a tension: some cultures view direct public criticism as shameful and ineffective. Other cultures view it as necessary for accountability. As a global leader, you can't choose one and dismiss the other. So how do you hold both?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) Have you ever felt that a leader was being "culturally sensitive" in a way that actually avoided accountability? When does deference to culture become an excuse?
2:00

Discussion 2: Power Distance and Expectation-Setting

In high power-distance cultures, leaders are expected to have authority and make decisions. In low power-distance cultures, leaders are expected to be collaborative. How do you set expectations globally when the expectations are different?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) What's a decision you've seen a leader make that worked in one culture and failed in another? What would have helped?
2:00

Discussion 3: The Cost of Inclusion

Real inclusion—the kind where all voices genuinely shape decisions—is slow. It's messy. It slows down decision-making. Is it worth it? Or is there a point where you have to say "we're moving forward" regardless of whether everyone feels heard?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) Think about global teams you've been part of. When did inclusion help decisions? When did it slow things down without adding value?
