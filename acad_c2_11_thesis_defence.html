What Makes a Thesis Defence Different?

A thesis defence isn't an exam. It's a conversation with experts who want to see you become one of them. Your committee already believes in your work — they're checking whether you can defend it under pressure, and whether you understand its significance.

Your Turn Think about a time someone challenged your idea or decision. How did you respond? Did you become defensive, or did you listen?
2:00

Why This Matters

In your defence, you'll face three types of moments:

  • Questions about decisions: "Why did you choose this methodology?" You answer by explaining the reasoning, not just the method.
  • Contradictions found: "In Chapter 2 you say X, but in Chapter 5 you say Y." You acknowledge, clarify, or revise on the spot.
  • The contribution question: "So what? How does this change the field?" This is the hardest and the most important.
Your Turn Look at your thesis title. In one sentence, explain why someone outside your field should care. Don't use jargon.

Six Moves for a Strong Defence

1 The Contribution Statement +
The first question is almost always about contribution. You need a 2-minute summary that moves from problem → your response → why it matters.
Structure

"The field has been asking [problem]. Most work assumes [limitation]. My research shows [finding/approach], which means [real-world significance]."

Not This

"My thesis is about the representation of gender in Victorian novels."

This

"Scholars have treated Victorian novels as either texts or historical documents, but rarely both. I use digital methods to show how publishers explicitly gendered their marketing of women-authored fiction—and how this shaped who read these books and what they meant. This changes how we understand the relationship between gender, commerce, and literary reception."

2 Defending Chapter Decisions +
Each chapter exists for a reason. You need to explain not just what you did, but why you had to do it that way—in that order—to get to your findings.
Wrong Move

"In Chapter 3 I used a mixed-methods approach."

Right Move

"Chapter 3 needed both data and interpretation. The quantitative phase gave me the distribution—which chapters were most reprinted—but it didn't tell me why. So I added discourse analysis of publisher archives. Neither method alone would have answered the question."

The Pattern

Problem in earlier chapter → This chapter's purpose → Why I chose this method → What it enabled me to see that the previous approach couldn't

3 Handling Internal Contradictions +
You will be caught. A committee member will find a sentence in Chapter 2 that seems to contradict Chapter 6. Don't panic. Show them how your thinking evolved, or how the contradiction isn't what it looks like.
What They Say

"On page 47 you define representation as textual encoding, but on page 234 you talk about reader reception. Which is it?"

What You Say

"That's a fair catch. In Chapter 2 I needed a working definition of textual representation to establish the archive. But representation only becomes meaningful through reception—someone has to decode it. So by Chapter 6 I'm arguing that representation IS reception. The earlier definition was necessary but incomplete."

The Move

Acknowledge the tension → Show it as evolution not error → Explain why the earlier framing was necessary

4 The "What Would You Change" Question +
Often asked as: "What are the limitations of your work?" or "What would you do differently now?" This isn't a trap. It's a test of intellectual maturity. Show you know your work's edges.
Weak Answer

"I think my work is solid. Maybe I could have looked at more novels."

Strong Answer

"My archive is 1840–1880, so I can't speak to earlier epistolary forms or the shift to realism. That was a choice—I wanted depth, not breadth. But the next phase would have to address how publishing practices changed after 1880, when mass literacy and new formats emerged."

The Move

Name a real limitation → Explain why you made that choice → Point to the next phase of work

5 Engaging with Hostile Questions +
One committee member might challenge your foundational assumption, theoretical framework, or methodology. They're not trying to fail you. They're testing whether you've thought deeply enough.
What They Say

"Your whole argument depends on the idea that gender was the category that mattered in these publishing decisions. But couldn't class have been just as important?"

What You Say

"Class absolutely matters. In fact, my data shows publisher targeting was class-inflected: they gendered certain genres as working-class entertainment. So I'd say gender and class co-produce these decisions. My focus on gender lets me trace a specific mechanism, but you're right that gender alone is incomplete. A full analysis would map their intersection."

The Move

Don't defend narrowly → Agree with the challenge where it's valid → Reframe your work as one lens, not the only lens

6 Closing with Authority +
The defence ends with you. The chair will often ask: "Do you have any final comments?" This is your moment to step back and remind them why your work matters.
What You Say

"Thank you. One thing I want to leave with: this research shows that how books were sold shaped how they were read. Publishing isn't neutral. That insight matters not just for Victorian studies but for understanding how media industries shape culture today. I'm grateful to have had the chance to develop this argument with all of you."

The Move

Restate the real-world significance → Thank the committee → Show you see the work's future

The Immediate Challenge

The chair nods, makes a note, then says: "That's clear. But here's my question: if someone in this committee had told you all of this before you started, would your research have looked different?"

They're asking: How much of your contribution is genuinely new thinking, and how much did you know you were arguing before you did the work?

Response Framework

START "No—and yes." (Show honesty, not defensiveness.)
MIDDLE Explain what you thought going in vs. what the data/analysis revealed.
END Name the moment the project shifted for you.
Speak for 1.5 Minutes Answer the chair's challenge. Show them the difference between your starting hypothesis and what you actually found.

The Hard Questions

Your defence continues. Here come the tough moments. Work through each one. Speak your response aloud, then reveal the move.

A committee member says: "In Chapter 4, you argue that X is the key mechanism. But your evidence for X is fairly thin—it's really only two archive documents. Why should we trust that conclusion when the evidence is so limited?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Don't defend the limitation. Reframe evidence.

Answer: "I appreciate that question. Those two documents are striking because they're publisher correspondence—explicit about business logic. My larger argument rests on 200 titles' patterns, which suggest the mechanism. The documents don't prove the mechanism works; they show that publishers understood it as one. So the evidence is triangulated: statistical pattern plus explicit testimony. That's stronger than either alone."

Someone asks: "On page 89, you say readers were 'surprised' by this representation. How can you claim to know their subjective experience when you only have the books themselves?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Clarify what you can and can't claim. Be precise about evidence.

Answer: "Fair point. I should be more precise. I don't claim to know their subjective experience. What I show is that the marketing framed it as surprising—novel covers used language of shock, violation, the unexpected. This is rhetorical positioning, not reader psychology. Readers may have experienced it differently. What I can argue is: publishers assumed readers would find it surprising, and they built that assumption into how they sold the book."

A hostile committee member says: "Your whole theoretical framework assumes power operates top-down—publishers shape readers. But publishing is competitive. Readers' preferences also shape what publishers produce. Isn't your model too simple?"
Your Response (120 seconds)
2:00
The move: Agree where you can, but defend your scope.

Answer: "You're absolutely right that publishing is bidirectional. Readers do push back—sales data shapes what gets reprinted. My contribution isn't claiming otherwise. It's showing one direction of that dynamic: how marketing shaped what readers encountered first. That's one lens on a complex system. A complete account would need to trace reader feedback loops too—but that's beyond my archive. I'm arguing for this specific mechanism, not claiming it's the whole story."

Someone asks: "You mention in your conclusion that this method could apply to 20th-century publishing. Why didn't you do that? It would have made your work more impactful."
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Own your scope. Point to future work.

Answer: "That's a good question about scope. I chose 1840–1880 because the publishing archives are richest in that period, and because gender ideology around the novel was being actively constructed then. 20th-century publishing has different archives—print runs, sales data, different producers. It would need different methods. So I designed this work as foundational—to show the approach works in one rich case. The 20th century would be the next project, with updated methods."

The external examiner asks: "I notice you don't cite the recent work by [prominent scholar]. How does your argument sit with theirs?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Honesty first. If you missed it, say so. If you disagreed, explain why.

Answer: "That's important. Honestly, their work came out while I was analysing my own data, so my engagement with it is lighter than it should be. But from what I've read, they argue that publishers were primarily driven by economic ratios. My work agrees on the economic motive but traces a different mechanism—gendered marketing as a specific strategy. Where we differ is in whether gender was primary or instrumental. I'd see it as instrumental-but-constitutive."
