II. The Gunpowder Plot & Stuart Crisis

James I & the Union of Crowns (1603)

When Elizabeth died in 1603, there was no heir. The throne passed to James VI of Scotland, who became James I of England. For the first time, the crowns of England and Scotland were united under one king. But James brought with him ideas about royal power that would divide the nation.

The King can do no wrong. What he does must be right and the people must obey and ask no questions. — H.E. Marshall paraphrasing James I, Our Island Story

Marshall tells us that James believed in the divine right of kings—the idea that his power came directly from God, not from Parliament or the people. This would become the fundamental clash that defined his reign and his son's.

The historian's note: James I (1566–1625) was a learned man who wrote books on kingship. He had ruled Scotland alone for many years before coming to England. He believed that kings were appointed by God and answerable only to God. This put him in direct conflict with Parliament, which believed it had rights and powers that could not be overridden. The tension between these two views would define the Stuart age.
♦ ♦ ♦

The Gunpowder Plot (1605)

James was Protestant, and his religious policies disappointed Catholics who had hoped for tolerance. On November 5, 1605, a group of Catholic conspirators led by Robert Catesby hatched a desperate plot: they would blow up the House of Lords while the King and Parliament were inside, kill the King, and install his young daughter Elizabeth as a puppet queen.

A man named Guy Fawkes was assigned to guard the explosives:

The Details of the Plot

36 barrels of gunpowder were hidden in a cellar beneath the House of Lords.

Guy Fawkes, a veteran of Catholic wars in Europe, was positioned to light the fuse when the moment came.

An anonymous letter was sent to a Catholic peer, warning him to stay away from Parliament on November 5. This letter led to the discovery of the plot.

November 5, 1605: Fawkes was arrested with the gunpowder still in place. The King and Parliament were saved.

Marshall describes the event with drama:

Guy Fawkes, a tall, powerful man with fierce eyes, stood in the dark cellar beneath the House of Lords. Above his head, the members of Parliament debated. But beneath their feet lay thirty-six barrels of gunpowder. Guy Fawkes and his fellow conspirators had sworn to blow them all to pieces. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXXI
The historian's note: The Gunpowder Plot remains one of the most famous acts of political violence in European history. Fawkes was executed (along with other conspirators) in January 1606 by being hanged, drawn, and quartered—a brutal punishment reserved for those convicted of treason. In Britain, Guy Fawkes Night (November 5) is still celebrated with bonfires and fireworks, though the meaning has shifted from "glad we survived an attack" to a general celebration. The phrase "Remember, remember the fifth of November" comes from a contemporary rhyme about the plot.

III. The Civil War & the King's Death

Charles I & the Breakdown (1625–1642)

James I died in 1625. His son, Charles I, inherited the throne and the same belief in divine right. But Charles was less skilled politically than his father. He quarrelled bitterly with Parliament over money, religion, and royal power. The tension escalated into open conflict.

The Core Conflict

Charles believed: He was God's anointed king. His will was law. Parliament existed to serve him.

Parliament believed: The King was subject to law. Parliament represented the people and had rights that could not be violated.

The religious issue: Charles wanted to impose Anglican Church practices on Scotland (which was Presbyterian). This angered both Scotland and Protestant members of Parliament.

♦ ♦ ♦

Civil War Begins (1642)

The quarrels between King and Parliament turned violent. England split into two camps:

The Two Sides

Royalists (Cavaliers): Supporters of the King, mostly from the nobility and the countryside. They wore their hair long and dressed in the fashions of Continental courts. Marshall's description of Cavaliers emphasises their loyalty and their romantic idealism, though they were also feudal lords defending their power.

Parliamentarians (Roundheads): Supporters of Parliament, mostly from towns, merchants, and Puritan clergy. They cut their hair short (hence "Roundheads"). They believed in a stricter, more austere form of Protestantism and in the rights of Parliament over royal whim.

Marshall captures the tragedy of civil war:

Englishmen fought Englishmen. Brother fought brother. Fathers and sons stood on opposite sides of the battlefield. All for a question that no one could agree upon: did the King rule because God had chosen him, or did the people have rights that even a king could not ignore? — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, adapted from Chapters LXXII–LXXV
♦ ♦ ♦

Oliver Cromwell Rises

In the early years, the King seemed to be winning. But a man named Oliver Cromwell changed everything. Cromwell was a Puritan gentleman from the Eastern Counties who rose to lead Parliament's army. He was a brilliant military commander and a man of unshakeable conviction.

Marshall preserves a famous saying about Cromwell:

Trust in God and keep your powder dry. — Oliver Cromwell, quoted in H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

This phrase captures Cromwell's character: absolute faith in God, but also practical military wisdom. He drilled his soldiers ruthlessly and created the "New Model Army"—the first modern, professional military force in English history.

The historian's note: Oliver Cromwell (1599–1658) was a complex figure. He was genuinely pious, a convinced Puritan who believed God was on his side. But he was also a ruthless military commander. When he conquered Ireland, he massacred civilians. Yet he also believed in some form of religious toleration (unusual for his time). After the Civil War, he became Lord Protector, effectively a military dictator, but one who believed he ruled for the public good.

Key Battles of the Civil War

1644 — Marston Moor

Parliament's northern victory. Cromwell's cavalry charge ("the Ironsides") breaks the Royalist right wing. Yorkshire falls to Parliament.

1645 — Naseby

Parliament's decisive victory. Cromwell and the New Model Army crush the King's main field force. The war is effectively decided, though fighting continues.

1646

King Charles surrenders to Scottish forces. He is held prisoner for two years while politicians and soldiers argue about what should happen to him.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Execution of Charles I (30 January 1649)

For two years, Charles was held prisoner while Parliament, the Army, and Cromwell debated his fate. Some wanted him restored to power with limits. Others wanted him executed for treason. The King would not negotiate. He refused to acknowledge Parliament's right to try him.

In the end, Cromwell and the radicals won. Charles was put on trial for treason against the people of England. The verdict was guilty. The sentence was death.

Marshall's account of the execution is moving and precise:

It was a cold January morning when King Charles walked to his death. He walked calmly, with dignity, dressed in a dark coat with a white collar. He held his head high. To the very end, Charles believed he was right—that God had made him King and that no power on earth had the right to judge him. He knelt upon the scaffold, he said a short prayer, he put his head upon the block, and the executioner's axe fell. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXXVI, adapted

The execution happened in broad daylight in front of Whitehall Palace in London. It sent shockwaves across Europe. Never before had a European nation executed its anointed king. England had made a revolution.

The historian's note: Charles I's execution on 30 January 1649 was a watershed moment. It was the first time in European history that a nation had put its divinely anointed king on trial and executed him. This act divided the Christian world and raised questions about the limits of royal power that still echo. Charles died a martyr in the eyes of many—his dignity on the scaffold won him sympathy even among some who opposed him. The Commonwealth that followed, under Cromwell, lasted only eleven years. When Charles's son was restored to the throne in 1660, the new reign began with an effort to "forget" what had happened—but the memory could not be erased. England had learned that even kings are subject to law.

V. Room 15 of Your Palace

ROOM 15: THE STUARTS

The Stuart Crisis (1603–1649)

You've now visited 15 rooms. This one holds the moment when England's absolute monarchy enters its final struggle: the Gunpowder Plot, the divine right doctrine, the Civil War, and the execution of a king.

The 5 Images for Room 15

1. Guy Fawkes with a lantern in the cellar — 36 barrels of gunpowder stacked around him, 1605. Parliament above. The moment before discovery.

2. James I on his throne, pointing upward — a burst of light from heaven representing "divine right." A king who will not bend to Parliament.

3. Two armies facing each other across a field — Cavaliers in plumes and gold on one side, Roundheads in drab grey on the other. 1642. Englishmen about to kill Englishmen.

4. Oliver Cromwell on horseback — in plain armour, his face stern and certain, leading the New Model Army. The sword and the Bible in his hand.

5. King Charles I on the scaffold in January cold — in his dark coat with white collar, kneeling calmly. The executioner's axe raised. The moment a nation executes its anointed king.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Divine Right vs. Parliament

James I believed the King's power came from God. Parliament believed it had rights no king could take. How do you think this conflict could have been solved without a civil war?

Why the Gunpowder Plot?

Catholic conspirators tried to blow up Parliament and the King. What drove them to such an act? Was it purely religious, or was there a political desperation?

Cromwell's Contradiction

Cromwell was a man of genuine faith and conviction, yet he became a military dictator. How can a revolutionary fighting for rights end up denying them to others?

Charles I's Dignity

Marshall describes Charles walking calmly to his execution, believing to the end that he was right. Does his dignity in death change how we should judge him as king?

Brothers Against Each Other

The Civil War pitted Englishmen against Englishmen, families against families. How does a nation recover from that kind of internal wound?

The Limits of Power

The execution of Charles I established that even kings are subject to law. How revolutionary was this idea in 1649? What would other European monarchs have thought?
