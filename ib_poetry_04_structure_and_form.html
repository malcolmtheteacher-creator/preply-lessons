Today we are working on structure and form so you can explain how a poem’s shape carries its argument, not just describe it.

The big idea

A poem’s form is a promise. A sonnet promises a neat argument in fourteen lines, with a turn (a volta) near the end. Poets can keep that promise, or break it deliberately. Marking where the poet breaks the pattern is often your best evidence.

Read the sonnet aloud once. Notice how it begins like a love poem and keeps disappointing you.

Sonnet 130 · William Shakespeare (c. 1609)

1My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun;
Coral is far more red than her lips' red;
If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;
If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.
5I have seen roses damasked, red and white,
But no such roses see I in her cheeks;
And in some perfumes is there more delight
Than in the breath that from my mistress reeks.
I love to hear her speak, yet well I know
10That music hath a far more pleasing sound;
I grant I never saw a goddess go;
My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground:
And yet, by heaven, I think my love as rare
As any she belied with false compare.

Rhyme scheme: ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. The pattern is stable, which gives the final couplet its force.

Ask three form questions

  1. What does the form promise (what would a reader expect)?
  2. Where does the poem keep that promise?
  3. Where does it break it, and what does the break make the reader think?

Weak versus strong

Weak: The poem is a sonnet with a rhyming couplet at the end, which shows it is a love poem.
Strong: By building three quatrains of unflattering comparisons in the style of a traditional love sonnet, then reversing direction in the couplet (“And yet, by heaven…”), Shakespeare mocks the exaggerated praise of conventional sonnets while presenting a more honest love, which positions the reader to trust the speaker’s plain statement more than any poetic flattery.

SPEAK: What is wrong with “which shows it is a love poem”? What does the strong version add?

The weak version only names form, says “shows”, and makes a claim that is too obvious to argue. The strong version describes the pattern, identifies the turn, names the effect (mocks the convention, builds trust), and links it to the poem’s argument.

Map the shape

1. Map it

For each quatrain, write what the speaker compares, and what the comparison denies.

Q1 (lines 1–4): eyes, lips, breasts, hair; each is set against a grand comparison (sun, coral, snow, the wires of conventional hair imagery) and found lacking. Q2 (5–8): cheeks and breath, compared with roses and perfume. Q3 (9–12): voice and walk, compared with music and a goddess. The pattern moves from the face to the voice to the way she moves: from sight to sound to motion, covering the whole body.

2. The turn

SPEAK: Why does the poem need the final couplet? Imagine it ended at line 12. What would the reader think of the speaker?

Without the couplet, the speaker would sound insulting, as if he does not love her. The couplet reverses this: his love is “as rare / As any she belied with false compare”. The poem’s target is not the woman but the false, exaggerated comparisons of other poets. The delay makes the final claim more surprising.

3. Plain speech

SPEAK: Compare the style of lines 1–12 (“coral”, “damasked roses”, “perfumes”) with the final couplet. What does the change in language do?

The comparisons are ornate and borrowed from conventional love poetry; the final couplet uses plain words (“I think my love as rare”). The shift from elaborate to simple language sounds sincere, so the reader trusts the speaker’s honest claim more than the decorated praise he has just mocked.

Looking back

SPEAK: Which was easier to find: where the poem keeps its form, or where it breaks it? Why?

I can: identify the form of a poem, find where it keeps or breaks the pattern, and explain how that shapes the argument.
sonnet (noun) — a fourteen-line poem with a fixed rhyme pattern. Example: "The sonnet is a form of love poetry."
quatrain (noun) — a unit of four lines in a poem. Example: "The first quatrain lists comparisons."
volta (noun) — the turn in thought or tone, especially in a sonnet. Example: "The volta arrives at “And yet”."
couplet (noun) — two rhyming lines, often closing a poem. Example: "The couplet delivers the argument."
convention (noun) — an accepted pattern or expectation in a genre or form. Example: "Praise of the beloved was a convention of love poetry."
parody (noun) — an imitation of a style that exaggerates it for mockery. Example: "The poem is a parody of love sonnets."
subvert (verb) — to undermine an expected pattern or authority. Example: "The poet subverts the expected praise."
iambic pentameter (noun) — a line of ten syllables with five alternating weak and strong beats. Example: "The sonnet is written in iambic pentameter."
