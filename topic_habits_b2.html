Twenty-One Days?

Everyone has heard that it takes 21 days to make a habit. This is the true story of where that number came from — and of what scientists found when they actually measured it.

Today: you'll read where the famous "21 days to make a habit" idea came from — and what scientists found when they actually measured it. You'll also practise four structures that are easy to confuse: I used to run · I would run · I'm used to running · I'm getting used to running.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then answer the quick questions from memory before you check. Then discuss the question.

Part 1 — The surgeon's 21 days

In 1960, an American plastic surgeon called Maxwell Maltz published a self-help book, Psycho-Cybernetics. In the preface, Maltz wrote about something he had noticed in his patients: "Following plastic surgery it takes about 21 days for the average patient to get used to his new face."

He gave other examples. After people lose an arm or a leg, the strange feeling that it is still there lasts, he said, about 21 days. People must live in a new house for about three weeks before it begins to "seem like home". His conclusion: it requires "a minimum of about 21 days" for an old picture in our mind to disappear and a new one to form.

Maltz was writing about getting used to a change — a new face, a new home. He was not describing a scientific study of habits. But over the years, his words were repeated, simplified and repeated again. The word "minimum" disappeared. The new face disappeared. What was left was a neat, hopeful rule that you can still find in countless articles and apps today: it takes 21 days to form a habit.

Decades later, psychologists at University College London tracked the famous number back to Maltz's book. Getting used to something and forming a habit, they pointed out, are not the same thing. So how long does it really take?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Maltz really writing about when he mentioned 21 days?

2. How did his idea change over the years?

1. Getting used to a change, such as a new face after plastic surgery or a new house. It was based on his observations, not on a study of habits.

2. People repeated and simplified it. "A minimum of about 21 days" for a mental image to change became "21 days to form a habit".

Discuss:
  • Why do you think the number 21 was so popular? What makes a rule easy to remember and share?

Part 2 — Sixty-six days, or two hundred and fifty-four

In a study published in 2010, Phillippa Lally and her colleagues at University College London decided to measure habit formation properly. They recruited 96 volunteers. Each person chose one new healthy behaviour — for example, drinking a glass of water — and connected it to a daily cue, such as "after breakfast". Then they did it every day for 84 days.

Every day, the volunteers also answered questions about how automatic the behaviour felt. At first, each repetition made a big difference. Then the progress became slower and slower, until the line on the graph became flat. The researchers took that flat point as the moment when the habit had formed.

The average time was 66 days — more than three times longer than the famous 21. If you start a New Year's resolution on 1 January, that takes you to early March. But the most interesting result was the range. For one person, the habit formed in just 18 days. For another, it hadn't formed by the end of the study, and the researchers calculated that it would take about 254 days.

Simple behaviours, like drinking water, became automatic faster than harder ones, like doing fifty sit-ups. Psychologists from the same UCL department summed it up simply: forget the magic number. If you keep doing the same thing in the same situation, a habit will form — but it will probably take longer than you think.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did each volunteer do in the UCL study?

2. What were the average and the range?

1. They chose one healthy behaviour, linked it to a daily cue (for example, after breakfast), did it every day for 84 days and reported how automatic it felt.

2. The average was 66 days. The range was from 18 days to an estimated 254 days.

Discuss:
  • Is it helpful or discouraging to know that a habit takes about two months? Would it change how you start a new one?

Part 3 — The soldiers who came home

In May 1971, two American congressmen came back from Vietnam with shocking news: heroin use among American soldiers there was widespread. The US Department of Defense quickly set up urine tests for soldiers leaving Vietnam. Then a researcher from Washington University in St Louis, Lee Robins, was asked to find out what happened to the men after they returned home.

Robins's team interviewed about 900 soldiers who had come home in September 1971. The numbers were frightening. Heroin in Vietnam was cheap and very strong. 85 percent of the men said they had been offered it, 35 percent had tried it, and 19 percent — almost one in five — had become addicted.

Heroin addiction was seen as one of the hardest habits in the world to break. But when Robins followed the men for three years after they came home, only 12 percent of those who had been addicted in Vietnam had relapsed into addiction at any time. The researchers admitted that the results were very different from what they had expected, and from what doctors saw in addicts who came to them for treatment.

Robins discussed one obvious explanation: back home, almost everything had changed. Heroin was much harder to get, and the men were far from the war, in different places, with different people and different routines. Robins was careful — the war was a very unusual situation. But many psychologists who study habits today see her study as a powerful lesson. A habit is not only inside us. It is tied to the places, people and cues around us. Change the situation, and the habit can lose much of its power.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What percentage of the soldiers became addicted in Vietnam — and what percentage of them relapsed after coming home?

2. What is the explanation that many habit researchers take from this study?

1. 19% became addicted. Only 12% of those relapsed into addiction in the three years after their return.

2. Habits are tied to situations and cues. When the men's whole environment changed, the habit lost much of its power.

Discuss:
  • Think of a time your situation changed completely — a new job, a new city, a holiday. Did any of your habits disappear? Did new ones start?

Grammar — used to, would, be used to, get used to

Maltz wrote that patients need time to get used to a new face. We all used to do things we don't do now. These four structures look similar, but they mean very different things.

Past habits: used to and would
• used to + verb = a past habit or state that is no longer true: "I used to smoke." (I don't now.) "She used to live in Lyon."
• Negative and question: "I didn't use to like coffee." · "Did you use to play football?" (no -d after did)
• would + verb = repeated past actions, often in stories: "Every Sunday my grandfather would make pancakes."
• But not for states: ✓ I used to have a dog. ✗ I would have a dog.
Familiar or not: be used to and get used to
• be used to + noun / -ing = something is normal and familiar for you: "I'm used to getting up early."
• get used to + noun / -ing = the process of it becoming familiar: "It took me months to get used to the noise." · "I'm getting used to my new job."

Don't mix it up: I used to work at night (in the past, not now) ≠ I'm used to working at night (it's normal for me). After be / get used to, the verb takes -ing.

1. Complete the sentences

Use used to, would, be used to or get used to and the verb in brackets.

1. Before the study, people (believe) that habits took 21 days.

2. Maltz said patients needed about three weeks to (their new face).

3. When I was a student, I (stay up) until 3 a.m. every Friday.

4. After ten years in London, she (drive) on the left. It feels normal now.

1. used to believe   2. get used to their new face   3. used to stay up / would stay up   4. is used to driving
3: both are possible because it is a repeated action. 4: be used to + -ing because it is normal for her now.

2. Which is correct? Choose a, b or c

1. I ___ a car, but I sold it when I moved to the city.
  (a) would have   (b) used to have   (c) am used to having
(b) — have here is a state, so we can't use would.
2. The new software is confusing, but I'm slowly ___ it.
  (a) getting used to   (b) used to   (c) use to
(a) — it is a process: it is becoming familiar.
3. ___ you use to drink coffee before work?
  (a) Were   (b) Did   (c) Would
(b) — Did you use to…? Note: use, not used, after did.
4. Nurses who work nights are used to ___ during the day.
  (a) sleep   (b) slept   (c) sleeping
(c) — be used to + -ing.

Going deeper

The studies in this story are real, and the numbers come from the researchers who did them. Together, they turn the usual advice about habits upside down.

Three big ideas from the story.

1. Most of what we do is habit. In two diary studies at Texas A&M University, published in 2002, Wendy Wood and her colleagues asked students to write down what they were doing every hour. Between about a third and almost half of their actions (35–43%) were habits: things they did almost every day in the same place. While they did them, people were often thinking about something else — and they felt less stress than during new activities.

2. There is no magic number. A simple, hopeful rule like "21 days" spreads easily. The real answer — "it depends on the person and the behaviour, but on average about two months" — is less exciting, but more useful. If people expect three weeks, they may give up in week four, thinking they have failed.

3. Change the situation, not only yourself. The Vietnam study suggests that a habit lives partly in the world around us: the place, the people, the cues. That is why a new job, a move or a holiday can be the best moment to start a good habit — or to break a bad one. And it is why many people find it easier to change their surroundings than to rely on willpower.
Talk it through:
  • If habits are tied to places and cues, what does working from home do to them? Has it helped your habits or hurt them?
  • Many apps reward you for a "streak" of days. Do they help build real habits, or just a habit of opening the app?
  • Is it a problem that most of what we do is automatic? Or is that what frees our minds for more important things?
  • Should companies design offices and canteens to push employees into healthier habits? Where is the line?
