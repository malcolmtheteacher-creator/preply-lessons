Connecting the Dots

The small choices we make often look random at the time. It is only later, looking back, that we see how they shaped everything.

The choices that change everything

Sometimes a tiny decision — a class you took, a person you met, a trip you almost didn't make — turns out to matter enormously. But we never know it at the time. Let us start with you.

Let's talk:
  • Think of one important thing in your life. What small moment or decision started it?
  • Have you ever done something that seemed pointless at the time, but became useful much later?
  • Do you believe things happen for a reason, or is life mostly chance?
  • If you could go back and change one small decision, would you? Which one?

Looking forward, looking back

When we look forward, the future is a fog — we cannot see which choices will matter. But when we look back, suddenly everything seems to connect: this led to that, that led to this. Today we will read about exactly that idea, and then talk about your own life.

Over to you:
  • Is it comforting or frightening that we cannot see which choices will matter?
  • Do you plan your life carefully, or do you prefer to stay open to surprises?

A class that seemed useless

Here is a true story about a small choice with enormous results. Read it, then we will talk about it.

When Steve Jobs was young, he started university but soon dropped out. He could not see the point of the classes he was supposed to take, and they were very expensive. But he stayed around the campus for a while, and he quietly went to a few classes that simply interested him — with no plan and no goal in mind.

One of those classes was about calligraphy: the art of beautiful handwriting and letters. He learned about different styles of type, about the spaces between letters, about what makes writing look beautiful. At the time, it seemed completely useless. It had no connection to anything practical in his life.

Ten years later, Jobs was helping to design one of the first personal computers. And he remembered that calligraphy class. He decided his computer should have beautiful lettering and fonts — something no ordinary computer had really had before. If he had never wandered into that class, his computer would probably never have had them. And perhaps the screens we look at every day would not look the way they do.

Years later, he explained it with a simple idea. You cannot connect the dots by looking forward, he said; you can only connect them by looking backwards. While we are living through our choices, they often feel random, even pointless. It is only later, looking back, that we see how one small moment quietly shaped everything that followed.

You cannot join the dots looking ahead. You can only understand your life by looking back at it — so you have to trust that, somehow, they will connect.

Grammar focus — the third conditional

The story is full of one very useful structure: imagining a different past. “If he hadn't taken that class, his computer wouldn't have had beautiful fonts.” This is the third conditional, and it is one of the most powerful tools you have for talking about choices, luck and regret.

1. What it is, and when we use it

We use the third conditional to imagine a past that did not happen — and its imaginary result. It is “unreal”: we are talking about something that is now impossible to change.

If + past perfect ,   would have + past participle
If + had(n't) done…,   would(n't) have done…

If he had taken the class, his computer would have had nice fonts. (He did take it — so this is the real, positive version imagined the other way.)

If she hadn't missed the train, she would never have met him. (She did miss it; she did meet him.)

2. Three things to remember

PointExample
It is unreal — the opposite of what really happened.“If I had known” = I did NOT know.
In speech we usually say 'd have and would've.“I'd have called you.” / “She would've stayed.”
Use might have / could have when you are less sure of the result.“If I'd left earlier, I might have caught it.”

The two halves can swap places (no comma when the “if” part is second): “His computer wouldn't have had fonts if he hadn't taken that class.”

3. Don't confuse it with the second conditional

Second conditional — unreal now / futureThird conditional — unreal past
If + past simple, would + verbIf + past perfect, would have + verb
“If I had more time, I would learn the piano.” (now)“If I had had more time, I would have learned it.” (past)

Practice A — complete the third conditional

1. If Jobs ____ (not / go) to that class, his computer ____ (not / have) beautiful fonts.

2. If I ____ (know) you were coming, I ____ (cook) something special.

3. She ____ (not / find) the job if a friend ____ (not / tell) her about it.

4. We ____ (miss) the whole event if we ____ (leave) five minutes later.

1. hadn't gone … wouldn't have had

2. had known … would have cooked

3. wouldn't have found … hadn't told

4. would have missed … had left

Practice B — second or third? Complete with the right form

1. (now) If I ____ (be) you, I would take the risk.

2. (past) If they ____ (listen) to her advice, they would have saved a lot of money.

3. (now/future) If we ____ (have) more time tomorrow, we would visit the old town.

4. (past) I ____ (call) you last night if I had had your number.

1. were (2nd conditional — “If I were you”)

2. had listened (3rd — past)

3. had (2nd — future)

4. would have called (3rd — past)
