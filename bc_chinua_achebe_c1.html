MINDS WORTH MEETING · C1 · 50 min

Chinua Achebe

The Nigerian novelist who realised that if Africa's story kept being told by outsiders, Africans would have to become its historians.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Just his name and the title of his first novel: Things Fall Apart. Have you read it, studied it, or heard it mentioned? Which books — or films — shaped your picture of Africa, and who made them? Just talk.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

Three short beats. After each one, you do the talking.

Chinua Achebe was born on 16 November 1930 near the Igbo village of Ogidi, in what was then the British colony of Nigeria. His father was a teacher and evangelist for the church, so he grew up between two worlds: Igbo tradition on one side, colonial Christianity on the other. Looking back in a 1994 interview, he said that as a boy reading adventure stories, “I instinctively took sides with the white people” — he did not realise he was supposed to be one of the “savages”.

Over to you:
  • A child cheering for the people who look down on him. Have you ever realised, later, that a story you loved was quietly telling you that you were on the wrong side?

In 1948 he was in the first intake of students at Nigeria's new University College in Ibadan, on a bursary to study medicine. There he became sharply critical of how Western novels portrayed Africa — especially Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness and Joyce Cary's Mister Johnson, whose Nigerian characters were savages or buffoons. He switched from medicine to English, history and theology, and lost his scholarship for it.

Over to you:
  • He gave up a safe career in medicine to write back to the books that insulted him. Was that brave, reckless, or both? Would you have done it?

He posted the handwritten manuscript of his first novel — his only copy — to a London typing agency, paid the fee, and then heard nothing for months. His boss at the Nigerian Broadcasting Service, Angela Beattie, went to the agency in person while she was in London and demanded the typed copy. Things Fall Apart was published by Heinemann in 1958. It has since been translated into more than 50 languages.

Over to you:
  • One of the most important novels of the century nearly vanished in a London office. Who was your “Angela Beattie” — someone who pushed a door open for you at the right moment?

The lions' historians — and you do the thinking out loud

Achebe's big idea is about who gets to tell the story. I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

In a 1994 interview with The Paris Review, Achebe explained why he became a writer. He did not claim the key line as his own — he called it “that great proverb”: “until the lions have their own historians, the history of the hunt will always glorify the hunter.” Then he added: “Once I realized that, I had to be a writer. I had to be that historian.”

Put yourself there

Before I go on: think of a story — from history, the news, your own family or workplace — that has mostly been told by the “hunter”. What would the lion's version sound like?

Things Fall Apart is the lion's version. Its hero, Okonkwo, is a famous wrestler and warrior in the village of Umuofia — proud, hard, and deeply flawed. The Igbo world around him is complex: it has law, religion, debate and art. Then missionaries and colonial officials arrive, and that world begins to break. In the final lines, a British District Commissioner looks at what has happened and thinks it will make an interesting chapter in his book, The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger.

Over to you:
  • Why do you think Achebe ends the whole novel from the colonial official's point of view, not Okonkwo's?
  • Okonkwo is not a perfect hero — he is often harsh and violent. Does that make the novel more convincing as the “lion's history”, or less?

He chose to write in English — he defended it as a way to reach a broad audience — but he reshaped its idiom and rhythm to carry Igbo speech and thought. In the novel's first chapter he writes: “Among the Igbo the art of conversation is regarded very highly, and proverbs are the palm-oil with which words are eaten.” In 1975, in a lecture called “An Image of Africa”, he went after Heart of Darkness directly, calling Conrad “a bloody racist” (the published version says “a thoroughgoing racist”). It caused a storm — but the essay is now included in a standard student edition of Conrad's novel.

The question Achebe forces on every reader is not only “Is this story true?” but “Whose story is it — and who was left holding the pen?”

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

“Only people from a culture can tell its story truthfully.” Argue for it or against it — with real examples of books, films or journalism about places or people you know. Build your case, then I will push back and you defend it.

Vocabulary — click each card to reveal

Ten words you need to discuss Achebe properly — and that work just as well for any serious conversation about history, culture and who tells the story. Tap to reveal the meaning and an example.

colonial / colonialism
adjective / noun
Relating to the rule of one country over another territory and its people.
"Okonkwo's village is changed for ever when the colonial administration arrives."
narrative
noun
A story, or the particular way a series of events is told. Collocations: dominant narrative; control the narrative.
"For decades, the dominant narrative about Africa in English came from outsiders."
to reclaim
verb
To take back something that was taken or lost, such as a story, a name or a right.
"Achebe set out to reclaim the African past from European novelists."
to dehumanise
verb
To treat or describe people as if they were less than human.
"He argued that Conrad's novel dehumanises Africans, turning them into scenery."
stereotype / stereotypical
noun / adjective
A fixed, oversimplified idea about a group of people.
"The boy in the story only meets Africans as stereotypes — savages or comic servants."
postcolonial
adjective
Relating to the period after colonial rule, or to writing that responds to colonialism.
"Things Fall Apart became a model for postcolonial literature across the continent."
oral tradition
noun phrase
Stories, history and wisdom passed on by speaking rather than writing.
"His mother and sister's stories gave him an oral tradition before he ever opened a novel."
proverb / proverbial
noun / adjective
A short traditional saying that expresses a general truth.
"In Umuofia, a well-chosen proverb wins an argument faster than a shout."
the canon / canonical
noun / adjective
The group of books generally accepted as the most important in a tradition.
"Today the novel sits firmly in the canon taught in schools around the world."
to marginalise
verb
To push a person or group to the edge, so they have little power or voice.
"A history written by the hunter marginalises the people it claims to describe."

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that an outsider could write a truer book than an insider.
  • It might well be that writing in English helped the novel travel further than any other choice.
  • I suspect the District Commissioner is the most frightening character in the book.
  • You could argue that every history is somebody's hunt story.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say the proverb applies to every workplace, not just to empires.
  • What strikes me most is that he refused to make Okonkwo a perfect hero.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, the question of who tells the story matters more online than it did in 1958.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure a writer's identity guarantees the truth.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that risk banning writers from imagining other lives?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

A · The Lion's Version (3–4 min)

Pick a well-known story — a historical event, a famous film, a company's success story, even a family legend — that is usually told from one side.

Retell it from the side of the person or group who usually doesn't get to speak. Then argue: does the new version change the facts, or only the meaning? Raise the strongest objection to your view, then answer it.

B · The Language Question (3–4 min)

In the 1950s a fierce debate broke out about which language African writers should use. Achebe chose English partly because, in a country of many languages, it gave people “a language with which to talk to one another”.

If you were a writer from a smaller language community, would you write in your own language or in a global one — and what would you gain and lose? Make the case with one concrete example from your own language.

C · Build Your Own (4–5 min)

Proverbs, Achebe wrote, are “the palm-oil with which words are eaten”. Your turn: choose a proverb from your own language or culture that carries a whole way of seeing the world.

Explain it, translate it, and show me what it reveals about the people who use it. I will then push back and test whether it really holds, and you defend it.

Your take — the conversation that matters most

You introduced me to Achebe, so you get the last word. Pick the questions that grab you — there are no model answers here.

Open discussion

  1. After today, has anything shifted in how you see Achebe — or in how you see the books that shaped your own picture of the world?
  2. Think of your country's own history. Who have been its “hunters”, and who its “lions”? Who is writing the history now?
  3. Achebe rejected the title “father of modern African literature”. Why might a writer reject a compliment like that?
  4. He called a classic novel racist, and many people were furious. Should we still read and teach books whose views we now reject — and if so, how?
  5. If you could ask Achebe one question, what would it be? And what do you think he would say back?
  6. Who is the next writer or thinker you would put in front of me — and what is the one idea of theirs I need to understand?
