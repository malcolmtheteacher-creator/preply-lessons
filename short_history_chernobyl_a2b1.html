The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A test at night

Near a small town called Pripyat, in what is now Ukraine, there was a big power station called Chernobyl. It made electricity for homes and factories. Pripyat was a new, clean town. Many young families lived there. Most of them worked at the power station.

On the night of 25 April 1986, the workers wanted to do a test on Reactor No. 4. To do the test, they had to make the reactor run very slowly. But this reactor was dangerous when it ran slowly, and the workers did not know this. They also turned off some safety systems.

Then, just after one o'clock in the morning, something went wrong. The power went up very fast — too fast to stop. A worker pushed the emergency button to shut the reactor down. But this reactor had a bad design, and the button made everything worse.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the workers want to do that night?

2. What did the workers not know about the reactor?

1. They wanted to do a test on Reactor No. 4. To do it, they had to run the reactor very slowly and turn off some safety systems.

2. They did not know that the reactor was dangerous when it ran slowly. The reactor had a bad design.

Talk about it:
  • The workers were not told that the reactor was dangerous. When something goes wrong, who should we blame — the workers, or the people who built it?

Part 2 — The town that had to leave

There were two big explosions. The top of the reactor was blown off, and the roof was open to the sky. Now the hot, broken reactor sent radiation straight into the air. Firefighters came quickly. They saw the fire, but they could not see the radiation all around them. It was invisible. Many of them became very ill, and 28 workers and firefighters died in the next few weeks.

But in Pripyat, life went on as normal at first. Children went to school. People hung their washing outside, in air that was already poisoned. For a day and a half, the leaders said almost nothing. Then, about 36 hours after the explosion, the buses came. The 47,000 people of Pripyat were told to pack one small bag for "a few days." But they never came back. Today, Pripyat is an empty town: silent flats, a broken fairground, and a swimming pool with no water.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were the firefighters in great danger?

2. What were the people of Pripyat told when the buses came?

1. The reactor was open and sent radiation into the air. The radiation was invisible, so the firefighters did not know how much danger they were in. Many became ill, and 28 workers and firefighters died.

2. They were told to pack one small bag for "a few days." But they never came back — the town is still empty today.

Talk about it:
  • The leaders waited a day and a half before they moved anyone. Why do you think a government might wait, even when people are in danger?

Part 3 — A secret too big to keep

The Soviet government did not tell other countries about the accident. But two days later, on 28 April, alarms started to ring at a power station in Sweden, more than a thousand kilometres away. The workers there checked their own reactor. It was fine. The radiation was coming on the wind, from the east. Now Sweden asked questions, and the government in Moscow could not stay silent. That evening, it finally said that there had been an accident at Chernobyl.

The clean-up was huge. Hundreds of thousands of workers — soldiers, miners and volunteers — were sent to the area. They buried poisoned soil, washed the streets, and built a big concrete cover over the broken reactor. A large area around the power station was closed off, and people are still not allowed to live there today. People say that the danger to the workers was hidden, and the true number of people who got ill is still not clear. Above all, people lost their trust in the government, because it had hidden a disaster from its own people. Many people say that Chernobyl was one reason the Soviet Union later fell apart.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did other countries first learn that something had happened?

2. What did the clean-up workers do?

1. Alarms rang at a power station in Sweden on 28 April. The workers there found no problem of their own and saw the radiation was coming on the wind from the east. Then Sweden asked questions, and Moscow finally admitted the accident.

2. They buried poisoned soil, washed the streets, and built a big concrete cover over the broken reactor.

Talk about it:
  • The accident was kept secret until a country far away found it on its machines. In today's world, could a big disaster like this still be hidden, even for one day?

Grammar: the passive & "people say that…"

This story has many things that were done to people and places — a town that was emptied, a roof that was blown off. It also has things that people say but no one is sure about. English has a simple way to talk about both.

1. The passive: was / were + past participle.
We use the passive when the important thing is what happened, not who did it. We often do not know, or do not need to say, who did it.
• "The town was evacuated." (We do not say who moved the people. The important thing is the town.)
• "The workers were warned." / "The workers were not warned."
• "The roof was blown off."
Form: use was for one thing (the town, the roof) and were for many things (the workers, the people).
2. "People say that…" — for things that are not sure.
When something is not proved, or you do not know who said it, you can start with People say that…, People said that…, or People believe that…
• "People said that the town was safe."
• "People say that the danger was hidden."
• "People believe that Chernobyl was one reason the Soviet Union fell apart."
It is a simple, honest way to report a story when you are not 100% sure it is true.
