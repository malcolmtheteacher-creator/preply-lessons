A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXVII of L

The Decisive Treatise

In which a philosopher in Córdoba argues that faith and reason cannot contradict each other—and nearly destroys philosophy in the process

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet Averroes, the greatest commentator on Aristotle, who believed that the deepest truths could be known through reason, but also through scripture. He tried to reconcile them perfectly. But in doing so, he created a dangerous idea: that some truths are for philosophers only, while ordinary people need stories. The lesson is that the deepest wisdom must be accessible to all—not hidden in elite circles.

I. Before We Begin

What do you know about Averroes, Ibn Rushd, or medieval Islamic philosophy? Have you heard of the conflict between faith and reason?

♦ ♦ ♦

A Question to Hold

Can truth contradict itself? If philosophy tells you one thing and scripture tells you another, must one of them be false? Or can they both be true in different ways?

Averroes believed they must both be true—because truth cannot contradict truth. But his solution created a problem that still haunts philosophy: he said philosophy reveals truths that scripture veils in metaphors, and that ordinary people need the metaphors while philosophers need the demonstrations. This split—two classes of people, two levels of truth—is what we will examine today.

II. The Story—A Commentator in Crisis

Averroes was born in Córdoba in 1126, in Muslim Spain. Córdoba was the capital of al-Andalus, a civilization where Christians, Muslims, and Jews lived in precarious but real coexistence. It was an intellectual crossroads. By the time Averroes was born, the first translations of Aristotle from Arabic into Latin were beginning to reach Christian Europe. Averroes would be the greatest interpreter of Aristotle in the medieval world—so influential that Christian scholastics called him simply “the Commentator.”

The Man and His World

Averroes was trained in law, theology, and medicine. He served as a judge, a physician, and a court philosopher. Unlike Avicenna, his predecessor, Averroes was deeply committed to Aristotle’s philosophy—not Plato’s, and not the Neoplatonism that had crept into Islamic philosophy. He believed Aristotle had said the final word on logic and natural philosophy.

But Averroes lived in a time of crisis. The Islamic world was fragmenting into warring states. Radical theologians were attacking philosophy itself, saying reason contradicted faith. The most famous attack came from al-Ghazali, an earlier theologian, whose book The Incoherence of the Philosophers argued that philosophy leads to heresy. Averroes wrote a response called The Incoherence of the Incoherence—defending philosophy and arguing that al-Ghazali had misunderstood both Aristotle and scripture.

The Decisive Treatise

In 1180, Averroes wrote The Decisive Treatise Determining the Nature of the Connection Between Religion and Philosophy. It is one of the most important texts in medieval intellectual history. His thesis is simple:

The law promotes philosophy and does not forbid it... Therefore whoever forbids the study of philosophy and mathematics because of a misinterpretation of scripture does an injustice to religion and to the law. — Averroes, Decisive Treatise

But Averroes then makes a crucial distinction. There are three levels of understanding:

1. Demonstration (Proof)

This is the highest level. A demonstrated truth is proven through logical argument. Only philosophers can achieve this level.

2. Dialectic (Debate)

This is for theologians and educated people. They use arguments and counterarguments but don't reach absolute certainty.

3. Rhetoric (Persuasion)

This is for the masses. They need stories, metaphors, and images—scripture in its literal sense—not proofs.

The Dangerous Idea: Averroes was saying: the masses cannot understand philosophy, so they need stories instead. The educated can understand theology. Only the rare few can understand philosophy. But all three are correct in their own way. This creates a hierarchy of truth—and a hierarchy of people.

The Problem with Averroes’ Solution

Averroes was exiled from Córdoba for defending philosophy. He died in poverty in 1198. His books were burned in the Islamic world—yet preserved and translated in Christian Europe, where they shaped Thomas Aquinas and the entire medieval synthesis. But the problem he created outlived him.

If the deepest truths are only for philosophers, then ordinary people are condemned to metaphors. If metaphors are enough for them, then either (1) the deepest truths don't matter for how to live, or (2) ordinary people are incapable of true understanding. Both conclusions are false.

III. The Ideas—Unity of Truth and the Divided Soul

Averroes believed something important and true. Let’s trace the three ideas that follow from his work.

Idea 1: Truth Cannot Contradict Itself

If philosophy proves X and scripture says Y, they cannot both be fully true.

This is actually correct. Averroes was fighting against a much older tradition that said faith and reason were completely separate domains. They are not. Reality is one. If reason and revelation are both true, they must ultimately agree. When they seem to disagree, at least one has been misunderstood.

Idea 2: There Are Levels of Understanding

The same truth can be expressed at different levels of sophistication.

This is also true. A child understands gravity differently than a physicist. A person can understand “be honest” without understanding the philosophical theory of virtue ethics. There is nothing wrong with levels of explanation.

Idea 3 (The Problem): Truth Should Be Hidden from the Many

The deepest truths are dangerous if misunderstood, so they should be reserved for those capable of understanding them.

This is where Averroes makes his fatal mistake. He assumes that the capacity to understand truth is distributed unequally. Some people are naturally suited to philosophy, others to theology, others only to rhetoric. This creates a class system of truth—and it justifies elitism. It says: the masses cannot understand; therefore, give them stories.

But this is backwards. The deepest truths about how to live, how to love, what we owe each other—these are for everyone. A poor, uneducated person can understand forgiveness, sacrifice, love, redemption. These truths can be spoken simply without being made less true.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Thesis Seed

Averroes insists that truth is one. But his solution—philosophy for elites, religion for masses—creates a dangerous split. Jesus taught the opposite: the deepest truths were spoken to fishermen and tax collectors, not reserved for scholars. The greatest truths about human life—forgiveness, love, sacrifice, redemption—are not too hard for the masses. They are too important to reserve for anyone.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Can Truth Contradict Itself?

Averroes says truth cannot contradict truth. If philosophy and scripture disagree, one must be misunderstood. Is this right? Are there cases where two truths really do contradict?

The Three Levels

Is Averroes right that philosophy is only for the few? Or are the deepest truths actually accessible to everyone, just expressed differently?

The Elite vs. the Many

Averroes thought ordinary people can't understand pure reason, so they need stories. Does this respect people or insult them? Is there wisdom in simplicity?

The Commentator

Averroes spent his life commenting on Aristotle. Is commentary a form of philosophy, or just scholarship? Can you do something creative while staying in service to a teacher?

Coexistence in Medieval Spain

Córdoba was a place where Christians, Muslims, and Jews lived together intellectually. How did this shape Averroes' thinking? Why couldn't it last?

A Man Forgotten in His Time

Averroes was exiled and his books burned in the Islamic world, but his influence shaped Christian Europe. Does that surprise you? Why might that be?

The Dangerous Hierarchy

The lesson says Averroes' solution creates a dangerous hierarchy. What is the danger? Why does it matter who gets to understand what?
