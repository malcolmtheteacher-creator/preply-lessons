1. With a price on his head and the law at his back, the outlaw never slept twice in the same town, and kept a saddled horse ready in case he had to run.
an outlaw (n) — a criminal who lives outside the law, on the run from the authorities.
2. The town needed someone to keep order, so the citizens elected a sheriff and pinned a tin star to his chest, trusting him to arrest troublemakers and run the local jail.
a sheriff / marshal (n) — a law officer responsible for keeping order and enforcing the law in a town or district.
3. Beyond the last farms and fences the land stretched out empty for a thousand miles — the frontier, where the settled country ended and the unmapped wilderness began.
the frontier (n) — the edge of settled land, where civilisation meets unexplored or wild territory.
4. After the long ride the men pushed through the swinging doors of the saloon, ordered whiskey at the bar, and sat down to a game of cards in the smoke and noise.
a saloon (n) — a bar or drinking place, especially the kind found in old frontier towns.
5. When the robbers fled into the hills, the sheriff swore in a dozen armed riders, formed a posse, and sent them galloping after the gang at first light.
a posse (n) — a group of people gathered by a lawman to help chase and catch criminals.
6. The notice nailed to the post offered a bounty of five hundred dollars — payable to anyone who brought the wanted man in, dead or alive.
a bounty (n) — a sum of money offered as a reward for catching or killing a particular person.
7. Under cover of darkness the thieves crept up to the herd, cut the fence, and began to rustle the cattle, driving them quietly off toward the next territory before dawn.
to rustle (v) — to steal farm animals, especially cattle or horses.
8. The two men had hated each other for years, and the whole town knew that sooner or later it would end in a showdown — face to face, in the street, with everyone watching.
a showdown (n) — a final confrontation meant to settle a long dispute once and for all.
9. The family claimed a patch of empty prairie, built a small house of earth and timber, and worked their new homestead from sunrise to dusk to make the dry land pay.
a homestead (n) — a house and the area of land around it, claimed and farmed by a settler family.
10. So far from any judge, court or jail, the mining camp was utterly lawless: every man carried a gun, and disputes were settled however the strongest saw fit.

The story, in four parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Opening the West

The "Wild West" — also called the Old West — names a particular time and place: the vast lands west of the Mississippi River, in the years roughly between 1865 and 1895. The American Civil War had just ended, and the United States turned its full energy westward, toward a frontier that seemed, to the settlers, almost endless.

Two laws lit the fuse. The Homestead Act of 1862 promised any citizen 160 acres of public land, almost for free, if they would live on it and farm it for five years. And the first transcontinental railroad, completed in 1869, suddenly linked the eastern cities to the Pacific coast. Together they pulled hundreds of thousands of people west: farmers, miners, ex-soldiers, freed slaves, and immigrants from every corner of Europe and beyond, all chasing land, gold, or simply a fresh start.

It was a land of cattle and grass. Huge herds were driven north from Texas to the new railroad towns, where they could be loaded onto trains and sold in the east. Along these cattle drives rode the cowboys — and for thirty years, on this fast-changing edge of the map, the legend of the Wild West was born.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which two developments pulled so many people west after 1865?

2. Why were huge herds of cattle driven north to the railroad towns?

1. The Homestead Act of 1862, which gave away cheap land, and the first transcontinental railroad, completed in 1869, which connected the east to the west.

2. So they could be loaded onto trains at the railroad towns and sold in the cities of the east.

Discuss:
  • The promise was "free land and a fresh start." What kind of person packs up everything and sets off into an unknown wilderness on a promise like that?

Part 2 — The outlaws

On a frontier that was often genuinely lawless, some men became famous for living outside the law altogether. Jesse James and his brother Frank, former Confederate fighters, robbed their first bank in 1866 and went on to hold up banks, stagecoaches and trains across the Midwest for sixteen years. To some, the James gang were dangerous killers; to others, a kind of Robin Hood who stood up to the powerful railroads and banks. In 1882 Jesse was shot dead in his own home by Robert Ford, a young gang member tempted by the bounty on his head.

Further south, a young man named Henry McCarty — better known as Billy the Kid — drifted through New Mexico, breaking out of jail more than once and reportedly killing several men before he was twenty-one. In 1881 the lawman Pat Garrett, who had set out to track him down, finally caught up with him at Fort Sumner and gunned him down in the dark.

These outlaws ran off with fortunes, but rarely kept them, and almost none died of old age. Their lives were short, violent, and — even while they lived — already turning into stories that newspapers stretched far beyond the truth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did some ordinary people admire Jesse James rather than fear him?

2. How did the lives of Jesse James and Billy the Kid both end?

1. They saw him as a kind of Robin Hood figure who stood up to powerful railroads and banks that many people resented.

2. Both were shot dead — Jesse James by a gang member, Robert Ford, in 1882; Billy the Kid by the lawman Pat Garrett in 1881.

Discuss:
  • The same man could be a hero to one group and a murderer to another. What turns a criminal into a folk hero in people's eyes?

Part 3 — The lawmen and the gunfight

Where there were outlaws, there were lawmen — though the line between the two was often blurred. The most famous of all was Wyatt Earp. In 1879 he and his brothers arrived in Tombstone, Arizona, a booming silver town, and took on jobs keeping the law. They soon fell into a bitter feud with a loose group of local cattle rustlers and outlaws known as "the Cowboys."

On 26 October 1881 the quarrel finally boiled over. Wyatt Earp, his brothers Virgil and Morgan, and their friend Doc Holliday walked down to confront the Clanton and McLaury men near the O.K. Corral. The most famous gunfight in American history then lasted around thirty seconds. About thirty shots were fired. Three of the Cowboys — Billy Clanton and the two McLaury brothers — were killed; two of the Earps and Holliday were wounded. Wyatt Earp walked away untouched, and into legend.

The reality of frontier law was less glamorous. Most towns, in fact, had strict gun laws and made visitors hand over their weapons on arrival. Violence was bad for business, and disputes were far more often settled with fists, or in court, than in a showdown in the street. The famous gunfight was the exception, not the rule — but it was the exception that everyone chose to remember.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who fought whom at the O.K. Corral, and how long did the gunfight last?

2. What does the real history suggest about how common street gunfights actually were?

1. The Earp brothers and Doc Holliday fought the Clanton and McLaury men ("the Cowboys") on 26 October 1881; the gunfight lasted about thirty seconds.

2. They were rare. Many towns had strict gun laws, violence was bad for business, and disputes were usually settled with fists or in court — the famous gunfight was the exception.

Discuss:
  • If real frontier towns were mostly quiet, why has the world chosen to remember the gunfights instead? Who benefits from that version of the story?

Part 4 — Legend and cost

The Wild West was already becoming a show while it was still happening. Cheap "dime novels" turned real outlaws and lawmen into giants. From 1883, Buffalo Bill Cody toured America and Europe with his "Wild West" show, with real cowboys, sharpshooters and staged battles — selling the frontier back to the world as spectacle. Later, Hollywood would do the same on a far larger scale, and the cowboy became one of the most powerful symbols of America itself.

But the romance hides a heavy cost. The land that settlers claimed as "empty" was not empty at all. It was the home of Native American nations — the Lakota, the Cheyenne, the Apache, the Comanche and many others. As railroads and homesteaders pushed west, the great buffalo herds the Plains nations depended on were hunted almost to extinction, and a long series of wars and broken treaties drove the survivors onto reservations. In 1890, US soldiers killed up to 300 Lakota men, women and children at Wounded Knee — a massacre often taken to mark the end of the frontier era.

That same year, 1890, the US census declared that the frontier line no longer existed: the West had been settled. The "Wild West" had lasted barely a single generation. Yet its legend has never crumbled. More than a century later, its outlaws, lawmen and cowboys still ride through our films and imaginations — a saga in which the glory and the cost have always been told together, whether we choose to hear both or not.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How was the legend of the Wild West sold to the public even as it was happening?

2. What was the cost of westward expansion to the Native American nations of the Plains?

1. Through cheap "dime novels" and, from 1883, Buffalo Bill's touring "Wild West" show, which turned outlaws, lawmen and cowboys into spectacle; later, Hollywood did the same.

2. Their land was taken, the buffalo herds they depended on were hunted almost to extinction, wars and broken treaties forced them onto reservations, and up to 300 Lakota were killed at Wounded Knee in 1890.

Discuss:
  • The frontier was "won" by some and lost by others at the very same moment. Can a story be celebrated and mourned at the same time — and should it be?

The grammar this story runs on: phrasal verbs (hold up, break out, track down)

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word like up, out, down, off — together they make a new meaning. The Wild West runs on them: outlaws hold up trains, break out of jail and run off with the money; lawmen set out to track them down and stand up to them. Get these right and you'll sound far more natural; the trick is knowing where the little word can go.

Literal or idiomatic?
• Sometimes the meaning is literal — you can guess it from the words: stand up, set off down the road, round up the cattle into one place.
• Often it's idiomatic — the meaning is a surprise: to hold up a train means to rob it; to break out of jail means to escape; to gun someone down means to shoot them dead.
The key rule — separable or inseparable?
• Separable phrasal verbs can split around their object: track down the outlaw OR track the outlaw down. And if the object is a pronoun (him, it, them), it must go in the middle: track him down ✓ — never track down him ✗.
• Inseparable phrasal verbs never split: you stand up to a bully — never stand a bully up to. The object always comes after the whole verb.
In this story: hold up, round up, track down, gun down, run off are typically separable; stand up to, break out of, set off, set out stay together.

2. Where does the pronoun go?

Each separable phrasal verb has a pronoun object (him, it, them). Rewrite the verb + pronoun in the correct order.

1. The marshal wanted to track down (him). → track

2. The robbers held up (it — the train). → held

3. The posse rounded up (them). → rounded

4. Ford gunned down (him). → gunned

1. track him down (pronoun must sit in the middle — never "track down him")

2. held it up

3. rounded them up

4. gunned him down
