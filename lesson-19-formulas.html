The clerk who changed the world in a year

Bern, Switzerland, 1905. A young man in his twenties sits at a high desk in the patent office, reading other people's ideas for new machines. He is a clerk, and nobody thinks of him as a scientist. At home, in his spare time, he is thinking about the nature of light, time and space.

In that one year he sent four papers to a leading German journal. Any one of them would have made his name. They explained how light carries energy in small packets, how tiny particles in water jiggle, how time and space change at high speed, and, in a short paper at the end of the year, how mass and energy are connected. His name was Albert Einstein.

What was Einstein's job in 1905?
He was a clerk in a patent office in Bern, working on physics in his spare time.
Talk about it. What would you do with spare time and a hard question? Would you work on it alone, or with other people?

The last paper gave the world the most famous formula ever written: E = mc². Energy equals mass times the speed of light, squared. Five characters, and yet they say that a tiny amount of matter holds an enormous amount of energy.

Millions of people know this formula without knowing what it means. And that is a clue to something about memory. You can remember a formula as a picture of symbols, but if you do not understand it, it will not last, and you will not know when to use it. A formula is a sentence. First understand what it says.

What does E = mc² say?
Energy equals mass times the speed of light squared: a little mass holds a huge amount of energy.
Talk about it. Explain E = mc² in your own words, as if to a ten-year-old.

Once you understand a formula, the problem is the symbols. E, m, c, a little 2: they look like nothing. They are abstract, and we found in the last lessons what the mind does with abstract things: it lets go.

So we give them life. Each symbol becomes a character, each operation an action, and the whole formula becomes a short scene. It sounds silly, and it is: that is what makes it work.

Why do symbols need characters?
Symbols are abstract, and abstract things are forgotten. A character you can picture holds them.
Talk about it. Which formulas or rules do you still remember from school? How do you remember them?

A character for every symbol

Understand the formula first. Then make it a scene.

  1. Say what it means. In words, in one sentence. 'Force equals mass times acceleration.'
  2. Give each symbol a character. Use the first letter or the meaning: E = eagle (energy), m = mouse (mass).
  3. Give each operation an action. '=' is a balance or a table; '×' is a crossing; '²' is a twin or a square box; '+' is a joining.
  4. Join them in one scene. The characters, in the same order as the formula, doing the actions.
  5. Test it with a number. Put in simple numbers and check the result. If it works, the formula is yours.

Meaning before pictures

  • Understand first. A picture of a formula you do not understand will fade; a picture of one you do will stay.
  • Check with numbers. If the sides of a triangle are 3 and 4, the longest side is 5: 9 + 16 = 25.
  • Say it in words. If you can say it, you can build the scene.
  • Review formulas the way you review any list: soon, then at longer gaps.

Build F = ma

Write your own scene for F = ma. Use a character for each symbol, and an action for each operation.
A giant Fist is punching a Mouse across the room. The mouse flies away faster and faster like an Arrow. The harder the fist (force), the faster the mouse goes.

Build the Pythagorean theorem

Make one scene for a² + b² = c². Then test it with 3, 4 and 5.
A ladder (c) leans on a wall (a) with its foot on the floor (b). The wall's garden (a-squared) and the floor's garden (b-squared) are poured into the ladder's giant square (c-squared), and exactly fill it. Test: 9 + 16 = 25.

Two circle formulas

Make scenes for C = 2πr and A = πr². Make sure the two scenes are different enough not to mix.
C: a Cheetah runs twice round a giant Pie with a Rabbit in the middle. A: a Rabbit sits on a pie, twice, and that is the Area of the pie. Give C a race and A a picnic.
