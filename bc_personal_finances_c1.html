Structures for Giving Advice

When we give financial advice, certain grammar structures help us sound tactful and thoughtful. Study these patterns.

Pattern 1: The sooner you..., the better
"The sooner you start saving, the better off you'll be."
Use infinitives (to save, to start, to pay) to express urgency while remaining encouraging.
Pattern 2: My main recommendation is to...
"My main recommendation is to create a monthly budget."
Positioning advice with "My recommendation" makes it personal and thoughtful, not commanding.
Pattern 3: If I were in your position, I would...
"If I were in your position, I would talk to your partner about shared financial goals."
This conditional structure (would + infinitive) is empathetic and avoids sounding judgmental.
Pattern 4: Your only option is to...
"Your only option is to face the problem and create a plan together."
Use this when the situation is serious; it conveys necessary rather than optional action.
Pattern 5: Be cautious about...
"Be cautious about taking secret loans. They damage trust."
Use verb-ing forms (taking, hiding, spending) after "cautious about" to warn gently.

Give Advice in Real Situations

Read each financial problem. Then give advice using the structures you've learned. Be thoughtful and practical.

Scenario 1: The Hidden Account

Your spouse has been hiding money in a secret bank account. When you find out, they justify it by saying they "didn't trust you with their future." There's £12,000 saved. You feel betrayed. You want to plan for your family's future together, but now you don't know how to rebuild trust around money.

Scenario 2: Controlling Parents

You're 28 years old and still living at home. Your parents control your money—they give you an allowance, check your spending, and won't let you have a bank account in your own name. They say it's "for your own good." You feel trapped and childish. Your siblings have moved out and become independent. You want freedom but fear disappointing your family.

Scenario 3: The Struggling Student

You're in your second year of university. Tuition is expensive. Your part-time job pays minimum wage and barely covers rent. You've turned to high-interest payday loans to make ends meet. You owe £3,500 in loans and the interest keeps growing. Your parents can't help. You don't see how you'll ever get out of this cycle.

Scenario 4: The House That Won't Sell

You own a house in an area where property values are falling. You need to sell it to move for a job opportunity, but the asking price keeps dropping and you still can't find a buyer. You'd lose £40,000 at current prices. You're trapped—you can't afford to keep the house and move, but selling feels like throwing money away. Your family is depending on this move.

Deeper Reflection

These questions invite you to think about money in your own life and in the wider world.

On your own experience:

Do you consider managing finances a strength? Why or why not? What habits do you have around money?

Learning and family:

How did you learn about money? Did your family talk openly about it? Did you receive pocket money? What messages did you absorb about wealth and spending?

On wealth and inequality:

How do people get rich? Is it luck, hard work, inheritance, good timing—or a mix? What role does privilege play? Is it possible to get rich ethically?

A personal thought experiment:

Imagine you suddenly had access to a significant amount of money—say, £1 million. What would you do with it? What does your answer reveal about your values?

On ethics and responsibility:

Do wealthy people have a responsibility to contribute to society? Should governments tax the rich more heavily? What makes a financial system fair?
