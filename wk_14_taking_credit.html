Taking the Credit

She said "we" once, quietly, at the start — and then "I" for nineteen minutes. It was your model. Two people have already congratulated her in front of you. Say it once, today, without a courtroom.

The Scene 🎬

Small enough to look petty. Big enough to matter. That's the trap.

Ana presented the analysis to the leadership team this morning. The model was yours, the late nights were yours, the idea about the returns was yours. She said "we" once, at the start, and then spoke for nineteen minutes in the first person singular.

You have two bad options and one good one. You can say nothing, and be quietly furious for a year until it comes out badly. You can go over her head to Priyesh, which makes you the person who escalates. Or you can say it to her, once, today, before it hardens.

The thing to hold onto: Ana is almost certainly not a thief. She's someone who got carried away in a room that was going well. Leaving her room to be that person is what makes this work.

  1. Has your work ever been presented as someone else's? What did you do?
  2. Is taking credit always deliberate — or is it mostly people telling a story with themselves at the centre?
YouDid the work. Not after an apology, after a correction — and you have to work with her next week.
AnaGenuinely baffled at first, then defensive, then — when she hears it — a bit shaken, because she knows she'd have been furious.
First: read the conversation and watch how carefully You leaves Ana a way to be a decent person who did a thoughtless thing. Corner her as a villain and she has to defend herself; leave the door open and she walks through it.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for claiming your own work without a courtroom. They work on a colleague, a co-author, a business partner, a sibling. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Level and slightly bored is the target — the tone of someone correcting a factual error rather than nursing a wound. Said hot it's a grievance and she'll defend herself; said cold it's an accusation and she'll harden. Aim for mildly embarrassed and completely clear, and keep it under two minutes.

🚪 Opening without a trial

Phrase 1

The job: flagging the discomfort and the size, so she doesn't brace for a war. Which opening?

A. "Can I say something slightly awkward? It'll take a minute."
B. "So that was interesting this morning."
C. "We need to talk about what happened in that meeting."
A — "Can I say something slightly awkward? It'll take a minute."
B is sarcasm with a smile on it and she'll hear the edge immediately. C sounds like a disciplinary. A admits the awkwardness — which is disarming, because you're showing you'd rather not be doing this — and "it'll take a minute" promises it's contained. She can afford to listen.

Take it anywhere: "Can I say something slightly awkward? It'll take a minute."
Phrase 2

The job: stating why you're raising it at all — and why it isn't a campaign. Complete it:

"I'm not making ___ ___ ___ this. I just don't want to say nothing."

"I'm not making a thing of this. I just don't want to say nothing."
To make a thing of something is to turn it into a bigger issue than it warrants. The second sentence is the honest one and it's the one that lands: not saying anything is its own choice, and it's the one that quietly poisons a working relationship. Together they set the exact size of the conversation.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not making a thing of this — I just don't want to say nothing."

📌 Naming it plainly

Phrase 3

The job: the claim itself — specific, factual, no adjectives. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "That was my analysis, Ana. The model, the returns idea — that was mine."
Notice there's no accusation in it at all: no "you took", no "you presented my". It's a statement about who did the work, which is a fact rather than a charge, and facts are much harder to argue with than charges. Naming the specific components makes it checkable and stops it sounding like a vague sense of grievance.

Take it anywhere: "That was mine. The ___ and the ___ — that was mine."
Phrase 4

The job: giving the evidence, including the slightly embarrassing admission that you counted. Put the pieces in order:

and "I" for the rest of it.You said "we" onceI counted, which tells youhow it felt.
"You said 'we' once and 'I' for the rest of it. I counted, which tells you how it felt."
The admission is what makes it work. Saying "I counted" sounds like it weakens you — it reveals you sitting there tallying pronouns — and instead it's the most persuasive thing in the sentence, because nobody counts unless something has genuinely got to them. Self-aware rather than self-pitying.

Take it anywhere: "I counted, which tells you how it felt."

🧱 Closing the two escape routes

Phrase 5

The job: answering "everyone knows it was a team effort" — the reasonable-sounding defence. Complete it:

"'Everyone knows it was a team effort' — they ___, though. That's the point."

"'Everyone knows it was a team effort' — they don't, though. That's the point."
Quoting her own defence back before answering it is what stops this being a squabble — she can hear it as a sentence rather than as her position. And the answer is simply true: leadership rooms attribute work to whoever was standing up, which is the whole reason credit matters. Two words do the work: they don't.

Take it anywhere: quote their defence, then answer it in four words.
Phrase 6

The job: refusing the intent defence without calling her a liar. Which version?

A. "I'm sure you didn't mean anything by it."
B. "I don't think you did it on purpose. I do think you did it."
C. "Come on, you knew exactly what you were doing."
B — "I don't think you did it on purpose. I do think you did it."
A lets her off entirely — you've withdrawn the complaint while making it. C accuses her of deliberate theft, which she'll deny with total conviction because it probably isn't true, and now you're arguing about her character instead of the facts. B separates intent from effect, which is the only honest position: she didn't plan it, and it still happened. The stressed do in the second sentence is the whole thing.

Take it anywhere: "I don't think you did it on purpose. I do think you did it."

🎯 Asking for something small

Phrase 7

The job: the ask — narrow enough that refusing would be strange. Complete it:

"I'd like you to say my ___ to Priyesh. That's all."

"I'd like you to say my name to Priyesh. That's all."
One sentence, to one person, once. Big asks — a correction email, a formal note — turn a private wobble into an incident and she'll resist. This is small enough to grant on the spot, and granting it is what actually fixes the problem, because the credit gets to the person who allocates work. "That's all" caps it explicitly.

Take it anywhere: ask for the smallest concrete action that would genuinely fix it.
Phrase 8

The job: setting up the future rather than relitigating the past. Complete it:

"Next time, I'd rather be in the ___ than in the ___."

"Next time, I'd rather present it with you."
The forward-looking request: instead of relitigating what happened, you name how you want it to work from now on — which is much easier to say yes to than an apology. It's also the sentence that produces Ana's offer.

Take it anywhere: "Next time, I'd rather ___ with you." Present it, be in the meeting, sign it jointly — the request that fixes the future instead of the past.

🤝 Landing it as colleagues

Phrase 9

The job: explaining the motive by inverting it. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I'd want you to tell me if I'd done it to you."
This is the sentence that makes the whole conversation a favour rather than an attack. It also puts her in your shoes for a second, which is exactly when she says she'd have been livid — she's just tested it herself and found you were entitled to be annoyed.

Take it anywhere: "I'd want you to tell me if I'd done it to you."
Phrase 10

The job: closing it completely, so nothing hangs over next week. Put the pieces in order:

We're fine.That's it.That's the whole thing.
"That's it. That's the whole thing. We're fine."
Three short sentences that shut the door. The fear she now has is that this is the first instalment of something — a grudge with more episodes — and saying explicitly that it's finished removes it. Say it, then talk about something else immediately, and mean it: if you bring it up again next month, none of the above was true.

Take it anywhere: "That's it. That's the whole thing." Any time you've said the difficult thing and want it over.

🔑 Make them yours

These work anywhere your contribution has gone unmentioned — a colleague, a co-author, a partner, a sibling telling a family story. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"That was mine. The ___ and the ___ — that was mine." — where has your work been attributed elsewhere?

"I don't think you did it on purpose. I do think you did it." — try it on your own situation.

"I'd like you to ___. That's all." — what's the smallest thing that would actually fix it?

The Echo 🕰️

Nikola Tesla built the system that carries electricity into every building you have ever stood in. Alternating current, the induction motor, the polyphase system — his. He also had almost no interest in being the person standing at the front of the room when it was explained.

Edison had a great deal of interest in that. He had a laboratory, a press strategy, and a genius for being the face of things, and for most of the twentieth century the schoolbooks said Edison. Tesla died in a New York hotel room owing money, with the pigeons.

Nobody stole anything, exactly. One man did the work and the other one told the story, and the story is what gets remembered. Which is the whole reason a sentence to Priyesh is worth having.
