Taking the Credit

She presented your work this morning. She said "we" once, quietly, and "I" for the next nineteen minutes. Small enough to look petty. Big enough to matter.

The Scene 🎬

It's ten past eleven on a Thursday. This morning Ana presented the analysis to the leadership team — Priyesh, the two directors, the lot. It went well. It went very well, because it was good work, and it was your work: your model, your three late nights rebuilding it when the numbers wouldn't behave, your idea about the returns that turned the whole thing around.

She said "we" once, near the start, quietly. Then she said "I" for nineteen minutes. Priyesh has already congratulated her in front of you, in the corridor, and you did the thing where you smile and nod and feel slightly ill. Sam did the same at the coffee machine.

Here's the trap. It's small enough that raising it makes you look petty, and big enough that it will sit in your chest for a year — which is exactly why most people say nothing, and then, eleven months later, say all of it at once, badly, in a review, to the wrong person. The alternative is to catch it now, while it's still just a thing that happened this morning: name it once, plainly, and don't hold a trial.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Has anyone ever presented your work as theirs — even slightly, even by accident? What did you do at the time, and what do you wish you'd done?
  2. Why is it so much easier to say "that's not quite right" about a number than to say "that was mine" about an idea? What are we actually afraid of in the second one?

Who's who

You
The One Who Did the Work 📊

You built it, you're proud of it, and you have roughly ninety seconds of courage available. You don't want an apology, a scene, or an enemy. You want your name said out loud to the person who matters.

The other voice
Ana 🎤

A colleague, not a villain. She's good in a room, she was nervous, she got carried along by her own momentum — and she genuinely has not noticed what she did. She'd be furious if it had happened to her.

First: read the conversation in the next tab, and watch one thing in particular. Every line You says leaves Ana a way to be a decent person who did a thoughtless thing, rather than forcing her to defend herself as someone dishonest — because the moment she has to defend her character, she stops listening and starts arguing, and you'll never get your name said to Priyesh. Notice how often You describes what happened instead of what Ana is.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Ana offers two perfectly reasonable-sounding defences before she actually hears what's being said. What were they — and why does neither of them survive?

2. There's a moment where Ana stops arguing altogether. What was said just before it, and what does she then realise about herself?

3. You could have asked for all sorts of things — an apology, an email to the whole team, a rule about future presentations. Instead the ask is tiny. Why does small work better here?

1. First she says she said "we" — which is true, but only once, and being counted is the answer to that. Then she says everyone knows it was a team effort, which sounds generous but is a way of making the credit belong to nobody in particular, and the corridor congratulations show it isn't true.
2. She stops when You separates the intention from the act — she was braced to defend her character, and nobody attacked it, so there was suddenly nothing to push against. What she realises is that she'd have been furious herself, which is worse for her than being told off.
3. A small, specific ask is easy to say yes to, and it's the thing that actually matters, because Priyesh is the one whose memory decides whose work it was. Big asks turn a two-minute conversation into a negotiation, and negotiations are where people start defending themselves again.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for claiming your own work without holding a trial. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Tone does almost all the work here. Said hot, every one of these lines becomes a grievance, and the other person hears an attack and defends themselves instead of listening. Said cold, the same words become an accusation, which is worse, because now it's a formal matter. What you're aiming for is somewhere flatter and more ordinary than either: the person opposite you should end up mildly embarrassed and completely clear about what you're asking for. Mildly embarrassed is fine. Mildly embarrassed is how people change their behaviour.

🚪 Getting into it without a trial

Phrase 1

You've found Ana by the lifts. You need to open this — signalling that it's uncomfortable, that you know it is, and that it's short. Which opener does that without either apologising it away or turning it into a summons?

A. "Great presentation. Really. Well done you."
B. "Can I say something slightly awkward? It'll take a minute."
C. "Have you got half an hour later? We need to talk about this morning properly."
B — "Can I say something slightly awkward? It'll take a minute."
Naming the awkwardness out loud takes the sting out of it, and "it'll take a minute" promises this isn't an ambush, which is what lets the other person listen instead of bracing. A is the sarcastic aside — it feels satisfying for about four seconds and tells her nothing, so nothing changes. C books a meeting, and a booked meeting turns a small thing into a formal one before either of you has said a word.
Phrase 2

You're two sentences in and she looks alarmed. You want to say: this is not an escalation, but I'm also not going to pretend it didn't happen. Complete the chunk: "I'm not ___ a ___ of this. I just don't want to say ___."

"I'm not making a thing of this. I just don't want to say nothing."
To make a thing of something is to treat it as bigger than it is — to turn a moment into an issue — so saying you're not doing that lowers the temperature before she raises it. The second half is what stops the sentence collapsing into an apology, because it says plainly that silence was the other option and you've chosen against it.

🗣️ Naming it, plainly

Phrase 3

Now the actual sentence. No preamble, no "I don't know if you noticed but", no question mark on the end. Say it out loud, in your own words: the work was yours, and here is exactly which parts. Then reveal.

"That was my analysis, Ana. The model, the returns idea — that was mine."
Two things are doing the work. Her name, which makes it a conversation between two people rather than a complaint being filed; and the list, because "that was my work" is arguable while "the model, the returns idea" is a set of specific objects that either came from your head or didn't. Notice there's no question in it — you're not asking her whether she agrees.
Phrase 4

She insists she credited you. She did — once, at the start, quietly. You want to give her the evidence, and admit in the same breath that you were keeping score, because that admission is the honest part. Put it in order:

how it felt.and 'I' for the rest of it.You said 'we' oncewhich tells youI counted,
"You said 'we' once and 'I' for the rest of it. I counted, which tells you how it felt."
The first half is a fact she can check against her own memory, which is much harder to argue with than an impression. The second half is the clever bit, although it isn't trying to be clever: admitting you counted looks like it weakens you, and instead it explains why this is worth two minutes of her time. People don't count things that don't hurt.

🚧 Refusing the two escape routes

Phrase 5

"I didn't mean to" is the first escape route, and it's usually true — which is why you agree with it and keep going anyway. Complete the chunk: "I don't think you did it on purpose. I do think you ___ ___."

"I don't think you did it on purpose. I do think you did it."
This is the sentence that ends the argument about her character and returns you to the argument about the facts, which is the only one you can win. She gets to keep being someone who wouldn't do that deliberately; she just doesn't get to use it as proof that nothing happened. Intention and effect are separate, and you've handed her the first one for free.
Phrase 6

"It was a team effort" is the second escape route — warm, modest-sounding, and it dissolves the credit into a fog where nobody has to be named. Which reply takes it apart without calling her a liar?

A. "No no, it's fine, honestly, forget I said anything."
B. "Fine. I'll put it to Priyesh myself and copy you in."
C. "'Everyone knows it was a team effort' — they don't, though. That's the point."
C — "'Everyone knows it was a team effort' — they don't, though. That's the point."
Repeating her phrase back before answering it shows you heard her rather than steamrollered her, and then "they don't, though" simply denies the fact she's relying on, which is where the whole defence lives. A is the collapse — you've raised it and then taken it back, so she'll remember only that you were odd by the lifts. B goes over her head to the boss, which turns a colleague into an opponent for the rest of your career there.

🎯 Asking for something specific

Phrase 7

She's asking what you want. This is the moment people ruin by asking for everything or nothing. Ask for one small thing from one specific person, and stop. Put it in order:

to Priyesh.I'd like you toThat's all.say my name
"I'd like you to say my name to Priyesh. That's all."
It's specific enough to be done this morning, and "that's all" closes the account — she now knows the exact size of what she owes you, which is a relief to her and a result for you. Vague asks like "I'd just like a bit more recognition" can't be completed, so they never are.
Phrase 8

One more ask, about next time rather than this time — you don't want to be thanked at the end, you want to be there. Put it in order:

than in the footnotes.I'd rather beNext time,in the room
"Next time, I'd rather be in the room than in the footnotes."
A footnote is the small print at the bottom of a page where you put the things that are technically necessary and nobody reads, so being "in the footnotes" means being credited in a way that costs nothing and changes nothing. Setting the two against each other — the room or the footnotes — makes the difference concrete, and it's aimed forward, so it's a request rather than a complaint.

🤝 Landing it and staying colleagues

Phrase 9

She's agreed. She's a bit shaken. Now the hardest skill in the whole conversation: stopping. Which line ends it cleanly and puts the relationship back?

A. "That's it. That's the whole thing. We're fine."
B. "And it's not just this morning, actually — it's been building up for a while, if I'm honest."
C. "Well, let's see if it happens again, shall we."
A — "That's it. That's the whole thing. We're fine."
Saying out loud that there is no second instalment is what stops her spending the afternoon wondering what else is coming, and it's the difference between a conversation and a grievance. B is the year of silence arriving all at once, which is the exact failure this whole lesson is trying to prevent. C leaves a threat hanging in the air, and she'll hear that threat every time she sees you.
Phrase 10

She asks, genuinely, why you're bothering to say any of it. The honest answer isn't about credit — it's about what you'd want from her. Complete the chunk: "I'd want you to ___ ___ if I'd done it to you."

"I'd want you to tell me if I'd done it to you."
This explains your motive without claiming any moral high ground, because you've just put yourself in the position of the person who might do the same thing. It also quietly says you expect to still be working together next month, which is more reassuring to her than any amount of "no hard feelings".

🔑 Make them yours — for the one that's actually coming

These are no use in general. They're for a specific person, a specific piece of work, a specific week. Fill these in about something real, and say each answer out loud until it sounds ordinary rather than rehearsed.

Something of yours that got absorbed into someone else's name — recently, or years ago. What exactly was yours? Name the parts.

Now the ask. One small thing, from one named person, that could be done this week.

And the ending — how do you stop, so they know there's nothing else coming?

The Twist

🔄 She says yes — and then says something true

Back in your own seat. This time Ana agrees straight away: no bafflement, no team effort, she's mortified, and she offers to tell Priyesh before lunch. You've won in about forty seconds. And then, entirely reasonably, she says: "Can I say something as well, though? I've been doing your presenting for about a year. Every time there's a room, you say you'd rather I did it. I've asked you four times. So — yes, I'll tell Priyesh. But you're not in the footnotes because I put you there." She's right, and you know she's right. Start the timer. The credit conversation has just turned into a completely different one about why you're never in the room, and you have to have it now, honestly, without pretending you didn't hear her.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said what happened rather than what she is — she never had to defend her character.
  • I didn't apologise for raising it, and I didn't take it back halfway through.
  • I asked for one specific thing that could be done this week.
  • I stopped when it was finished, and we were still colleagues at the end.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that will cost you next time. Run Round 1 again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Credit in an organisation doesn't accumulate where the work was done. It accumulates wherever the story of the work was last told, and it sticks to whoever was standing there when it was told — which is usually not the person who was up at one in the morning with the spreadsheet.

So there are two currencies in most jobs, contribution and visibility, and although everyone will tell you the first one matters, promotions are paid out in the second. That isn't a conspiracy, and mostly nobody is being dishonest; it's just that senior people decide with what they can remember, and they remember rooms.

Which is why "it was mine" is so much harder to say than the work was to do. The work is safe — nobody can accuse you of wanting anything. Claiming it means admitting, in front of a colleague, that you wanted to be seen, and that feels like a confession rather than a fact.

Two bigger questions

  1. If visibility is what gets rewarded and contribution is what gets asked for, is the person who quietly does excellent work being noble — or just avoiding a conversation they find frightening? Where's the honest line?
  2. In your own working culture, who is allowed to claim credit without seeming arrogant, and who isn't? What decides that — and how much of it is nothing to do with the work?
