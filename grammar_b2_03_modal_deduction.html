Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Your neighbour's curtains haven't opened in two days and their car hasn't moved. Say three guesses out loud about what's happened — before you read on.

Listen in on this mystery

Dan: Have you seen Priya? She was meant to be here at six.

Rosa: No sign of her. She must have missed the train.

Dan: Or maybe she's forgotten — she can't have left work yet, it's not even five thirty there.

Rosa: True. She could have got stuck in traffic too, I suppose.

Look at what Rosa does in three lines. Three different levels of certainty about something in the past, and she's never actually there to check. Can you spot all three? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "must have missed" — almost certain, based on the evidence (no sign of her).
2. "can't have left" — almost certain it's NOT true, based on the time.
3. "could have got stuck" — just one possibility among others.

This is why these forms matter: you weren't there to see it, but you can still talk about the past with exactly the right amount of certainty — from "definitely" to "maybe."
Today: working out what probably happened — must have, can't have / couldn't have, and might have / may have / could have — plus how to talk about things that were still going on, so you can guess about the past with the right level of certainty, not just guess blindly.

Working Out What Happened

You already guess about right now — today's job is guessing about the past, with the same confidence.

Spot the pattern

  • "The door's unlocked but no one's in — she must have popped out for a minute."
  • "He can't have read my message — he'd never ignore something like that."
  • "They couldn't have gone far without a car; the last bus left an hour ago."

What's the shared machinery, and what's the difference between the first sentence and the other two? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
modal + have + past participle. Must have = I'm almost sure it happened. Can't have and couldn't have mean the same thing in the negative — I'm almost sure it did NOT happen. They're just two ways of saying it; couldn't have can feel a touch stronger in speech. Both are built on solid evidence, not a hunch.
must have + past participle  (I'm sure it happened)
can't have / couldn't have + past participle  (I'm sure it didn't happen)
Quick reminder from before: must be / can't be guesses about right now — "She must be home, the lights are on." Must have / can't have guesses about something already finished — "She must have got home already, the lights are on and the car's here." Same logic, different time.
One trap: for a past guess you're sure is WRONG, English never uses "mustn't have." Use can't have or couldn't have instead.

Quick check

1. You get home and the biscuit tin is empty except for crumbs, and the lid's not even back on. Deduce with must have + eat:

"Someone the whole tin."

2. Your flatmate says she posted your parcel yesterday, but the post box on the corner has had an "out of order" sign on it all week. Use can't have + post:

"She it there — that box's been broken for days."

3. A friend claims he ran the whole route in under two hours, but you know he's never managed more than 5k in one go. Use couldn't have + run:

"He it that fast — he's never run more than 5k!"

Maybe, Maybe Not

Not every past mystery has a clear answer — sometimes you're just offering a possibility.

Spot the pattern

  • "The line's been busy for an hour — she might have left the phone off the hook."
  • "He may have forgotten it was today; he's been so distracted lately."
  • "I could have left my keys at the office, or maybe in the car — I'm honestly not sure."

How is the confidence here different from must have and can't have? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
might have / may have / could have + past participle — all roughly equal: "it's possible, one explanation among others." No strong evidence pushes you towards certainty either way, so you leave the door open.
might / may / could + have + past participle  (possible — not sure)
One extra job for could have: it can also mean "was able to, but didn't" — "I could have helped, but nobody asked me." Context tells you which meaning is which.

Quick check

1. Your friend hasn't replied to any texts all weekend, but he's sometimes just busy and bad with his phone, nothing more sinister. Use might have + lose:

"He his phone somewhere, or he's just been busy — hard to say."

2. The vase is broken on the floor. The cat was in the room, but the window was also open and it's been windy. Use may have + knock:

"The cat it over, but that gust of wind is just as likely."

3. Your colleague is late for the meeting — could be traffic, could be an earlier appointment running over, nobody actually knows. Use could have + go:

"She straight to the client's office instead — we don't actually know."

Now say it — offer two explanations

SPEAK

A shop you always pass on your way somewhere is suddenly closed, with no sign explaining why. Give me two different possible explanations out loud, using might have and could have.

Still Going, Probably

Sometimes you're not guessing about one finished action — you're guessing about something that was in progress.

Spot the pattern

  • "Her eyes are red — she must have been crying."
  • "The engine's still warm; it can't have been sitting here long."
  • "He looked exhausted at the meeting — he might have been working all night."

What's the one extra piece these sentences add? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
modal + have been + verb-ing. Use it for something that was going on, not a single completed action. Compare: "she must have cried" (one event, now over) vs "she must have been crying" (it was happening, and the evidence — red eyes — is still visible now).
must / can't / couldn't / might / could + have been + verb-ing

Quick check

1. The bathroom floor is soaking wet and the shower's still steamy when you get there. Use must have been + shower:

"Someone for ages — the floor's flooded."

2. The neighbours upstairs are usually loud, but it was dead silent all evening. Use can't have been + argue:

"They up there tonight — not a sound, which isn't like them."

3. A colleague seemed distracted and miles away in the meeting, and you're not sure why. Use might have been + worry:

"She about something — she wasn't really listening."
