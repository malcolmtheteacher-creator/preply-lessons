Now — the words for today's story

1. For months the rebels met in back rooms by candlelight, swearing one another to silence, binding themselves into a conspiracy so tight that not even their own families suspected a thing.
a conspiracy (n) — a secret plan made by a group to do something illegal or harmful; the act of plotting together.
2. To raise your hand against your own king was, in those days, the very worst crime of all — treason, punishable by a slow and public death.
treason (n) — the crime of betraying your own country or trying to kill or overthrow its ruler.
3. Each conspirator had sworn the same oath; if one of them talked, every man around that table would hang.
a conspirator (n) — one of a group of people who secretly plan something illegal or harmful together.
4. They packed the wooden kegs tight with gunpowder, the same black powder that loaded the cannons of every army in Europe, knowing a single spark could level a building.
gunpowder (n) — an explosive black powder used in guns, cannons and fireworks; it burns very fast and violently.
5. Down a flight of cold stone steps, beneath the great hall, lay a damp undercroft where no daylight ever reached and where things could be hidden for months among the coal and firewood.
an undercroft (n) — a vaulted underground room beneath a building, especially a church or hall, often used for storage.
6. He had given his word and shared every secret, so when he chose to betray them and run to the authorities, it felt like a knife in the back.
to betray (v) — to harm someone who trusts you, especially by telling their secrets to their enemies.
7. Somewhere in the group there was an informer — someone quietly passing word of every meeting to the men in power, while smiling at the table like a friend.
an informer (n) — a person who secretly gives information about others to the police or authorities.
8. When he would not speak, they took him to a cold room and began to torture him, certain that enough pain would finally drag the names out of him.
to torture (v) — to cause someone severe pain on purpose, often to force them to give information or confess.
9. They rolled the last heavy cask across the floor and stood it beside the others — thirty-six of them in a row, each one bound with hoops of iron.
a cask (n) — a large wooden container with curved sides and a wider middle, used to store liquids, powder or goods; a barrel.
10. The warning arrived just in time, and the guards moved in to foil the plan hours before it could be carried out, arresting the man at the door.
to foil (a plan) (v) — to stop a plan from succeeding; to prevent something just before it happens.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A promise broken

In England in 1605, your religion could decide your whole life. The country was officially Protestant, and Catholics — who still followed the older form of Christianity, led from Rome — lived under harsh laws. They were fined for not attending Protestant church, watched, and at times imprisoned. When the old queen, Elizabeth I, died and James I came to the throne in 1603, England's Catholics dared to hope. James had a Catholic mother, and he had hinted that life might become easier for them.

It did not. Far from easing the laws, James spoke of how he "detested" the Catholic faith, and ordered Catholic priests out of the country. For a small group of angry young Catholic gentlemen, that broken promise was the final straw. They decided that the king, his ministers and his Parliament would never give them freedom — and so the king, his ministers and his Parliament would have to be destroyed, all at once.

The man behind the plan was Robert Catesby, a tall, charming, persuasive figure from a Catholic family that had suffered for its faith. Around him he gathered a small circle of trusted men, including Thomas Wintour and Thomas Percy. They needed one more thing: someone who understood explosives. They found him in a battle-hardened soldier who had spent ten years fighting for Catholic Spain in the Netherlands — a cool, careful man named Guy Fawkes.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did England's Catholics hope for better treatment when James I became king — and what actually happened?

2. Who led the plot, and why was Guy Fawkes brought in?

1. James had a Catholic mother and had hinted that the laws might be eased; instead he said he detested the Catholic faith and expelled Catholic priests.

2. Robert Catesby led it; Fawkes was a soldier with ten years' experience and knowledge of explosives, so he was put in charge of the gunpowder.

Discuss:
  • The plotters felt every legal door had been shut in their faces. Does that ever justify violence — or does it never? Where would you draw the line?

Part 2 — Thirty-six barrels

The plan was breathtaking in its boldness. On 5 November 1605, the King would open Parliament in person. James, his eldest son, the leading nobles, the bishops and the members of both Houses would all be gathered in one place: the House of Lords. If the plotters could destroy that building at that exact moment, they would wipe out the entire government of England in a single blast.

They rented a property beside Parliament and, crucially, gained the use of an undercroft — a large cellar directly beneath the House of Lords. Through 1605 they quietly moved in barrels of gunpowder, hiding them under piles of firewood and coal: thirty-six barrels in all, far more than enough to bring the great chamber down. Guy Fawkes, using the false name "John Johnson," posed as a servant looking after the property, and took charge of guarding the powder. The plan was simple and terrible. As soon as Parliament opened and the chamber was full, Fawkes would light a slow fuse, slip out, and let the gunpowder do the rest.

But a conspiracy of this size could not stay perfectly secret. Some plotters worried about the Catholic lords who would die alongside the king. Then, late in October, an anonymous letter reached one of them — Lord Monteagle — warning him to stay away from Parliament, because it would "receive a terrible blow" and yet not see who struck it. Monteagle, alarmed, took the letter straight to the King's ministers. The hunt was on.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the State Opening of Parliament the perfect moment for the attack?

2. What was the Monteagle letter, and what did the receiver do with it?

1. The King, his heir, the nobles, bishops and members of both Houses would all be gathered in the House of Lords at once, so a single blast could destroy the whole government.

2. An anonymous letter warning Lord Monteagle to stay away because Parliament would "receive a terrible blow"; he took it straight to the King's ministers, who began to investigate.

Discuss:
  • One quiet warning undid months of careful planning. Do big secrets, shared among many people, always leak in the end? Can you think of examples?

Part 3 — Caught in the cellar

Acting on the warning, the King's men searched the buildings around Parliament. Just after midnight, in the early hours of 5 November 1605, they went down into the undercroft and found a tall man in a cloak and boots standing among a great pile of firewood. He gave his name as John Johnson. When they cleared away the wood, they found the thirty-six barrels of gunpowder — and, on the man himself, matches and the means to light a fuse. He was arrested on the spot. The plot had been foiled with only hours to spare.

"John Johnson" gave little away at first, even admiring the King's questioners for their persistence. But James ordered that he be tortured, and over the following days, stretched on the rack, he was broken. He confessed, and gave up the names of the others. By then the rest of the plotters had fled into the Midlands, hoping to raise a Catholic revolt. None came. Catesby and several others were cornered and shot dead resisting arrest; the survivors were dragged back to London. In January 1606 the captured men, Guy Fawkes among them, were condemned for treason and executed in the most brutal way the law allowed. Fawkes, weak from torture, climbed the scaffold last — and at the final moment fell or jumped so that his neck broke, sparing himself the worst of it.

The plot failed completely, and the laws against Catholics grew harsher, not gentler. Yet the night never disappeared. To celebrate the King's survival, people lit bonfires across London — and they have lit them every year since. Four centuries later, on the evening of 5 November, Britain still fills with bonfires and fireworks, and children still burn a "Guy" on the flames. A handful of men set out to be remembered as liberators. Instead, they gave their country a festival of fire — and the most famous failed plot in English history.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How exactly was Guy Fawkes caught, and how were the other names discovered?

2. The plot failed — so why is 5 November still remembered every year?

1. The King's men searched the undercroft just after midnight, found him guarding the barrels with the means to light a fuse, and arrested him; under torture on the rack he confessed and named the others.

2. People lit bonfires to celebrate the King's survival, and the tradition continued — Bonfire Night, with fireworks and a burning "Guy," is still marked on 5 November.

Discuss:
  • Britain celebrates the failure of a plot to commit mass murder — yet "Guy Fawkes" has also become a symbol of rebellion against power. How can one man mean two opposite things?

Grammar — First Conditional & Future Time Clauses

A plot is pure intended future: if this works, then that will happen; we will act the moment such-and-such happens. English has a neat rule for talking about a real future condition or a future moment — and the trick is what you do not do: you don't use will in the if-part or after time words like when and until. You use the present there, even though the meaning is the future.

1. First conditional — a real future condition:
If + present simple, … will + base verb.
Use it for something that may well happen, and its likely result. From this story:
• "If the plot succeeds, the king will die."
• "If one of them talks, they will all hang."
• "If Monteagle warns the ministers, the search will begin."
Not: If the plot will succeed… — the if-part stays in the present.
2. Future time clauses — a future moment:
After when, as soon as, before, after, until, once, use the present (not will) for the future; the main clause takes will.
• "As soon as Parliament opens, they will light the fuse."
• "They will wait until the chamber is full."
• "Once the powder explodes, the government will be gone."
• "Fawkes will slip out before the gunpowder catches."
Not: As soon as Parliament will open… — the time clause stays in the present.
The one rule to hold on to: the future idea is carried by will in the main clause only. The if-clause and the time clause (when / as soon as / until / once / before) both stay in the present, even though they point to the future.

1. First conditional — put the verbs in

One verb goes in the present (the if-part), the other takes will + base verb.

1. If the guards (search) the cellar, they (find) the barrels.

2. If Fawkes (light) the fuse, the chamber (explode).

3. The plotters (hang) if anyone (betray) them.

1. search / will find (if + present, will + base)

2. lights / will explode

3. will hang / betrays (the if-part stays present even when it comes second)

2. Future time clauses — present after when / as soon as / until / once

After the time word, use the present. The other part takes will.

1. As soon as Parliament (open), Fawkes (light) the fuse.

2. They (wait) until the chamber (be) full.

3. Once the king (arrive), there (be) no turning back.

1. opens / will light (present after "as soon as")

2. will wait / is (present after "until")

3. arrives / will be (present after "once")

3. Mix them — which is wrong, and why?

Each sentence has one verb in the wrong form. Write the corrected verb. Then read the fixed sentence aloud.

1. If the letter will reach the ministers, the plot is in danger. →

2. They will strike as soon as the doors will close. →

3. Before the fuse will burn down, Fawkes will be gone. →

4. We will not move until the chamber will fill with lords. →

1. reaches (if-clause stays present)

2. close (present after "as soon as")

3. burns (present after "before")

4. fills (present after "until")

4. Build the plot — finish each line your own way

Speak first, then write. Keep the rule alive: if/when/until/once + present, will in the other part.

1. If the king dies, …

2. As soon as the powder explodes, …

3. They will not light the fuse until …

4. Once the people hear the news, …

1. … the whole government will fall with him.

2. … the House of Lords will collapse into rubble.

3. … the chamber is completely full.

4. … they will rise up across the country. (Yours will differ — just keep the present after the time word and "will" in the main clause.)
