Now — the words for today's story

1. At the heart of the old city stood a great stone shrine, its towers carved with gods and dancers, where for centuries people had come to pray and leave their gifts.
a shrine (n) — a holy place dedicated to a particular god, saint or sacred figure, where people come to worship.
2. At its height the kingdom stretched across many lands, and every year dozens of conquered peoples carried tribute to the ruler in the capital, bowing low as they laid it before his throne.
tribute (n) — a payment made by one state or people to a more powerful one, often as a sign of submission or for protection.
3. Without rain for months, the farmers depended entirely on irrigation: a network of channels that carried river water out across the dry fields so the rice could grow.
irrigation (n) — the supplying of water to land or crops through channels, canals or pipes, to help them grow.
4. Behind the long earth wall lay an enormous reservoir, holding millions of litres of water collected in the rains and released slowly through the dry months.
a reservoir (n) — a large natural or artificial lake used for storing water for later use.
5. Year after year the great canals began to silt up, until the slow build-up of mud and sand choked the channels and the water could no longer flow to the fields.
to silt up (phrasal v) — (of a river, canal or channel) to become gradually blocked by fine sand and mud carried by the water.
6. Every year the monsoon arrived on time: weeks of heavy, drenching rain that flooded the rivers and turned the dust to thick mud.
a monsoon (n) — a seasonal wind in South and Southeast Asia that brings a period of very heavy rain.
7. The pilgrims climbed the steps on their knees, and the look on their faces — eyes closed, lips moving in prayer — showed a deep devotion to their god.
devotion (n) — deep love, loyalty and religious feeling toward a god, a person or a cause.
8. The capital was vast and sprawling, spreading out over the plain in every direction for many miles, with no clear edge where the city stopped and the countryside began.
sprawling (adj) — spreading out over a large area in an irregular, untidy way.
9. Within a few decades the forest had begun to reclaim the ruins: roots split the stone walls apart, and vines wrapped the towers until they almost disappeared into the green.
to reclaim (v) — (of nature) to take back land or a place that people once controlled; to make something wild again.
10. Father, son and grandson each took the throne in turn; for over four hundred years a single dynasty of kings ruled the land.
a dynasty (n) — a line of rulers who all belong to the same family, passing power down the generations.

The story, in three parts

Part 1 — A city built on water

In the year 802, on a sacred mountain in what is now Cambodia, a Khmer prince named Jayavarman II declared himself a "universal ruler" and broke free of foreign control. That moment is usually taken as the birth of the Khmer Empire. Over the next six hundred years his successors built, on the flat plain north of a great lake, the largest city the world had yet seen: Angkor. At its peak it may have spread over a thousand square kilometres and held perhaps three quarters of a million people — a sprawling capital of wooden houses, markets, palaces and temples, bigger in area than any city in medieval Europe.

What made such a city possible, in a land of fierce seasons, was water. For half the year the monsoon poured down; for the other half, almost no rain fell at all. The Khmer answer was engineering on a colossal scale. They dug enormous reservoirs, called barays, by hand — one of them, the West Baray, was over eight kilometres long and held more than fifty million cubic metres of water. A web of canals and channels carried that water out across the fields for irrigation, allowing the farmers to grow rice all year round and feed a vast population. The empire, quite literally, was built on the control of water.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What event in 802 is usually treated as the start of the Khmer Empire?

2. Why was water management so important to a city like Angkor?

1. Prince Jayavarman II declared himself a "universal ruler" on a sacred mountain and broke free of foreign control.

2. The climate swung between heavy monsoon rains and a long dry season; reservoirs and canals stored monsoon water and used it for irrigation, so a huge population could grow rice and be fed all year.

Discuss:
  • Angkor's whole power rested on controlling one resource — water. Which single resource do you think modern cities are most dependent on today?

Part 2 — The temple-mountain and the god-kings

The greatest of Angkor's rulers expressed their power in stone. In the early twelfth century, the king Suryavarman II began a temple unlike anything before it. Built over some thirty-seven years and dedicated to the Hindu god Vishnu, Angkor Wat was a mountain of carved stone surrounded by a moat so wide it looked like a square lake. It remains the largest religious monument on Earth. Its walls carry hundreds of metres of carving — armies marching, gods and demons pulling at a serpent, rows of dancing spirits — a record in stone of the kingdom's devotion and its myths.

Later in the same century, the empire nearly fell. A rival people, the Cham, attacked by river and plundered Angkor itself in 1177. Out of that disaster rose its most ambitious king, Jayavarman VII. He drove the invaders out, and then rebuilt on a scale never seen before — a new walled capital, Angkor Thom, with giant stone faces gazing out from its central temple, the Bayon. Unlike his predecessors he was a devoted Buddhist, and across the empire he built temples, roads and, the inscriptions claim, more than a hundred hospitals. Over the generations Angkor Wat itself shifted from a Hindu shrine to a Buddhist one, its statues quietly changing faith along with its kings.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who built Angkor Wat, and which god was it first dedicated to?

2. What did Jayavarman VII do after the Cham attack of 1177?

1. King Suryavarman II built it in the early 1100s; it was first dedicated to the Hindu god Vishnu (and only later became a Buddhist site).

2. He drove the Cham out, then rebuilt on a huge scale — the new capital Angkor Thom and the Bayon — and, as a Buddhist king, built temples, roads and many hospitals.

Discuss:
  • Angkor Wat changed religion — Hindu, then Buddhist — without being torn down. Why might a new ruler choose to reuse an old monument rather than destroy it?

Part 3 — The forest takes it back

No empire lasts forever, and Angkor's strength slowly became its weakness. The very water system that fed the city was fragile: canals silted up, walls broke, and a giant network like this needed constant repair by a strong central government. In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the climate turned against the Khmer — modern tree-ring studies show decades of harsh drought broken by years of violent flooding, exactly the conditions that could wreck such a system. At the same time, power and trade were shifting south, and a rising Thai kingdom pressed from the west, sacking Angkor in 1431. The court abandoned the old capital and moved nearer the coast, toward what is now Phnom Penh.

Angkor was never completely forgotten. Buddhist monks stayed on at Angkor Wat and kept it swept and living, which is why that one temple never fully fell silent. But around it, the sprawling wooden city simply dissolved, and the forest began to reclaim the rest — roots cracking the stone, trees rising straight through the ruins. For centuries the wider world barely knew the place existed. Then, in 1860, a French naturalist, Henri Mouhot, published a breathless account of the towers he had found half-buried in the jungle, and the West suddenly "discovered" what local people had never lost. Today Angkor Wat appears on Cambodia's flag, and well over a million visitors a year walk where the god-kings once stood — proof that an empire can vanish from the map and still refuse to be forgotten.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Give two reasons the Khmer Empire declined and Angkor was abandoned.

2. Why did Angkor Wat survive when so much of the city did not?

1. Any two of: the huge water system silted up and broke down; severe drought and flooding (shown by tree-ring studies) wrecked it; trade and power shifted south; a rising Thai kingdom sacked Angkor in 1431.

2. Buddhist monks stayed on and kept it in use, so it was never fully abandoned to the forest like the rest of the city.

Discuss:
  • Mouhot is often credited with "discovering" Angkor, though local people had always known it was there. What does that tell us about how history gets written?

Grammar — Nominalisation (the rise & fall of…)

This is a story of grand events — an empire rises, a city collapses, a forest takes over. When we want to write about events like these in a more formal, weighty way, English often turns the verb or adjective into a noun. That move is called nominalisation, and it is everywhere in serious history writing, essays and reports.

The idea in one line: take the action or quality and make it a thing.
• verb → noun: the empire collapsed → the collapse of the empire
• verb → noun: they failed to adapt → their failure to adapt
• verb → noun: the city was abandoned → the abandonment of the city
• verb → noun: they declined slowly → the slow decline of the kingdom
• adjective → noun: the system was fragile → the fragility of the system
Why bother? Nominalisation lets you pack a whole idea into a noun phrase and then build on it: "The construction of Angkor Wat took thirty-seven years." It sounds more formal and more objective than "They spent thirty-seven years building Angkor Wat." Useful patterns to keep: the construction of…, the collapse of…, the abandonment of…, the decline of…, the rediscovery of…, their failure to… Just don't overdo it — too many nouns in a row and a sentence turns heavy and lifeless.

1. Make the noun — then build the phrase

Turn the verb or adjective in brackets into a noun to complete each formal phrase from this story.

1. the of the empire (collapse — verb)

2. their to maintain the canals (fail — verb)

3. the of the city to the jungle (abandon — verb)

4. the gradual of Khmer power (decline — verb)

5. the of the water system (fragile — adjective)

1. collapse — the collapse of the empire

2. failure — their failure to maintain the canals

3. abandonment — the abandonment of the city to the jungle

4. decline — the gradual decline of Khmer power

5. fragility — the fragility of the water system

2. Rewrite formally — verb sentence → noun phrase

Rewrite each plain sentence using nominalisation, beginning with the words given. Keep the meaning the same.

1. Suryavarman II built Angkor Wat over 37 years. → The of Angkor Wat took 37 years.

2. The Cham attacked and plundered the city in 1177. → The of the city in 1177 nearly ended the empire.

3. Mouhot rediscovered the ruins in 1860. → The of the ruins in 1860 amazed the West.

4. The Khmer managed water brilliantly. → Their brilliant of water made the city possible.

1. construction — The construction of Angkor Wat took 37 years.

2. plunder (or plundering / sacking) — The plunder of the city in 1177 nearly ended the empire.

3. rediscovery — The rediscovery of the ruins in 1860 amazed the West.

4. management — Their brilliant management of water made the city possible.

3. Build a formal paragraph

Fill each gap with the noun form of the word in brackets. Read it aloud once you've finished — notice how formal it sounds.

The (rise) of the Khmer Empire depended on the careful (manage) of water. Yet the (fragile) of that system, together with the (decline) of central power, led to the eventual (abandon) of Angkor. Its (rediscover) in the nineteenth century turned a ruined capital into a national symbol.

rise · management · fragility · decline · abandonment · rediscovery

Notice how each noun packs a whole event into a phrase you can then build a sentence around — the hallmark of formal register.
