Where Do Stereotypes Come From?

We all have pictures in our heads about other countries. Where do they come from?

Today: you will learn why stereotypes exist, and use some, most and many.
Talk first:
  • What do people say about your country?
  • Is it true?
  • Do you have a stereotype about another country?

Key words

Read the sentence. What does the bold word mean? Then click Reveal.

1. That is just a stereotype.
stereotype (n) — a simple idea about a group of people, often not true. Example: “That is just a stereotype.”
2. Is the story true?
true (adj) — right, not false. Example: “Is the story true?”
3. He told a joke about Italians.
joke (n) — something you say to make people laugh. Example: “He told a joke about Italians.”
4. A study asked people about their country.
study (n) — careful work to find facts. Example: “A study asked people about their country.”
5. The average is 20.
average (n) — the middle or typical amount. Example: “The average is 20.”
6. Give me an example.
example (n) — one thing that shows what a group is like. Example: “Give me an example.”
Talk it through:
  • Which word is new for you? Say a sentence with it.
The stereotype: “Stereotypes are true.”

The story

Part 1 — The word “stereotype”

The word “stereotype” first meant a metal plate for printing. In 1922, an American writer called Walter Lippmann used it for “the pictures in our heads”. They are simple ideas that help us think about a difficult world.

Quick check:

1. What did “stereotype” first mean?

2. Who used it in 1922?

1. A metal plate for printing.

2. Walter Lippmann.

Discuss:
  • What is a picture in your head about another country?

Part 2 — A big study

In 2005, a team studied about 50 cultures. They compared ideas about “typical” people with real tests of real people. The ideas were similar in each country, but they did not match the real tests.

Quick check:

1. How many cultures did the team study?

2. Did the ideas match the real tests?

1. About 50.

2. No.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think the ideas were wrong?

Part 3 — Why they stay

Stereotypes stay for three reasons. We hear them in jokes, films and adverts. We notice the one rude waiter and forget ten friendly ones. And a country's average does not tell us about one person. Many people are not “typical”.

Quick check:

1. Name one reason stereotypes stay.

2. Does an average tell us about one person?

1. Jokes, films, adverts; or we only notice what fits.

2. No.

Discuss:
  • What can you do about stereotypes?

Grammar — some, most and many

some = a small or middle number: “Some people are late.”
many = a big number: “Many people believe it.”
most = more than half: “Most people are not typical.”
Use them to be careful: not “all”, but “some” or “many”.

1. Choose the word

1. people are on time, and some are late. (Most / All)

2. people believe stereotypes, but not everyone. (All / Many)

3. It is not true that Spanish people are late. (all / some)

2. Many

3. all
